## Slide 2 (hidden=False)
Learning objectives
Explain the efficient-market hypothesis and distinguish its weak, semi-strong, and strong forms.
Interpret event-study evidence and major return anomalies without treating historical patterns as guaranteed trading rules.
Separate informational efficiency from a chosen asset-pricing benchmark and explain the role of arbitrage.
Apply limits to arbitrage, index-fund logic, and long-horizon evidence to disciplined investment decisions.
Use real and experimental bubble evidence to explain how expectations and constraints can sustain mispricing.

## Slide 42 (hidden=False)
The efficient-market hypothesis says prices reflect available information, so investors should not consistently earn risk-adjusted excess returns.
Event studies often show rapid reactions to public news, although some returns drift after announcements.
Size, value, momentum, and reversal challenge simple efficiency, but they do not guarantee profitable trading rules.
Every efficiency test is joint with an asset-pricing model. Abnormal returns can reflect mispricing or a misspecified risk benchmark.
Markets become more efficient when active traders exploit anomalies. Risk, trading costs, and publication can erode the returns.

## Slide 53 (hidden=True)
U
nder EMH and a standard asset-pricing model, assets with the same priced risk exposures should have the same expected (risk-adjusted) returns.
EMH (information efficiency): Prices already reflect available information, so no free lunch from public signals. You can’t earn persistent alpha after adjusting for risk and costs.
Equilibrium + no-arbitrage: If two assets have the same risk (meaning the same exposure to the risks investors are paid to bear) but different expected returns, investors would buy the higher-return one and sell the lower-return one. That demand shift moves prices until expected returns equalize for equal risk.
Risk compensation: Investors are risk-averse and require a risk premium only for non-diversifiable (systematic) risk. Idiosyncratic (diversifiable) risk doesn’t earn a premium.
52
Sources: Fama (1970); Sharpe (1964); Lintner (1965).

## Slide 75 (hidden=True)
What can explain this?
“Smart” investors were limited in their ability to short-sell Palm (as documented in Lamont and Thaler), so it wasn’t their fault.
But this cannot explain why anybody would buy Palm instead of 3Com – for this one needs irrationality.
In facts 2 things are needed for mispricing to exist:
Irrational investors
Limits to arbitrage (here due to implementation costs)
3Com & Palm case illustrates that mispricing does not imply a free lunch!
