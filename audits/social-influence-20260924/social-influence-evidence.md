# Chapter 28: social influence evidence and allocation

Review date: 24 September 2026. Chapter 28 is now **Social Influence**, subtitled *Compliance, Authority, and Group Decisions*. This expands the existing chapter rather than adding a second survey of norms or renumbering the book.

## Allocation

- Chapter 25: cooperation, reciprocity as a social preference, group membership, and social norms in allocation games.
- Chapter 26: informational and normative influence, conformity, social proof, and cascades.
- Chapter 28: requests and compliance; favors, request sequences, scarcity and liking; authority, group discussion, and responsibility.
- Chapter 29: culture, identity, and the meaning of the same action in different relationships.
- Chapter 30: changes in beliefs and attitudes through messages; credibility, elaboration, self-persuasion, and resistance.

The influence-cue table retains its identifier `tbl-25-influence-signals`, but now appears in Chapter 28. Its eight rows are a practical checklist, not an experimentally validated eight-factor theory. The old Chapter 30 anchor remains a bridge to the new `social-influence-cues` section. Foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face index entries now point to Chapter 28; self-persuasion remains in Chapter 30.

## Primary evidence checked

| Source | Comparison and result used | Limits and interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| [Langer, Blank, & Chanowitz (1978), original article](https://litfl.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/The_mindlessness_of_ostensibly_thoughtfu.pdf) | Table 1: small request, 60% compliance with no reason versus 93% with the circular reason; larger request, 24% in both conditions. | Permission to use the copier, not attitude change; a conditional low-cost interaction, not a universal effect of a word. |
| [Regan (1971), original article](https://www.uni-muenster.de/imperia/md/content/psyifp/aeechterhoff/vorlesungkommunikation/regan_favorandlikingcompl_jesp1971.pdf) | A requester's favor, a favor supplied by the experimenter, and no favor. More than one raffle ticket bought by 58% in the requester's-favor condition versus 25% across controls. | Male university students; liking manipulation affected rated liking but its purchase effect was not significant at .05. Pattern consistent with reciprocal obligation, not a unique identified mediator. |
| [Freedman & Fraser (1966), original article](https://www.bulidomics.com/w/images/6/6c/Freedman_fraser_footinthedoor_jpsp1966.pdf) | Experiment 2, Table 2: small road-safety sign followed by large lawn sign, 76% versus 16.7% with only the large request; comparison cells n=25 and n=24. | Denominator is the initially approached condition, not a post hoc subset of initial compliers. Historical small experiment; multiple explanations for sequential-request effects. |
| [Cialdini et al. (1975), original article](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Influence_Compliance/Cialdini.et.al.Reciprocal.Concessions.Procedure.1975.article.pdf) | Experiment 1: two hours a week for at least two years followed by a two-hour zoo outing, versus the outing alone. Willingness 50% versus 16.7%. | Expressed willingness, not attendance. A perceived concession is an interpretation of the request sequence, not a universal obligation. |
| [Cialdini et al. (1978), original article](https://www.bulidomics.com/w/images/1/15/Low-ball_cialdini-cacioppo-bassett-miller_1978.pdf) | First experiment: disclose 7 a.m. after versus before an initial decision. Attendance 18/34 (53%) versus 7/29 (24%). | Attendance differs from the appointment-agreement rates. The study involved course credit; the chapter does not call it unpaid volunteering. |
| [Worchel, Lee, & Adewole (1975), original article](https://www.bulidomics.com/w/images/6/69/Effects-of-supply_worchel-lee-adewole_1975.pdf) | First experiment: jars containing two versus ten cookies, including scarcity attributed to demand versus accident; desirability varied with supply and its explanation. | Product evaluations, not purchasing, taste improvement, or nutritional quality. The passage explicitly identifies the first experiment. |
| [Burger et al. (2001), publisher article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/01461672012712002) | Three experiments involving brief interaction, mere exposure, and similarity before a request. | Qualitative result verified; no unverified sample size or effect magnitude imported. These manipulations do not validate a general claim that warmth defeats judgment. |
| [Burger (1999), review record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15661679/) | Multiple-process explanation of foot-in-the-door results. | Guards against presenting self-perception as the one established mechanism. |
| [Cialdini & Goldstein (2004), review](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.psych.55.090902.142015) | Scientific account of social influence, conformity, and compliance. | Chapter definitions distinguish observed behavior from private judgment without assuming these never overlap. |

The existing Milgram, Burger (2009), hidden-profile, groupthink, bystander, and psychological-safety material was retained. Shared identity links to the substantive studies already in Chapters 25 and 29. Safeguards are presented as reasoned applications of the evidence, not a validated package of interventions.
