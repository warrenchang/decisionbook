# Quotation source notes

Verified on 16 September 2026. The [selection record](epigraph-selections.json) supplies each quotation's linked source, locator, attribution, and intended connection to the chapter. The [quotation guide](quotation-guide.md) presents the complete set for editorial review.

Wording was checked against transcribed books, letters, plays, poems, and speeches where available. Other checks used the author's official archive or a responsible institution's published transcription or quotation record: the Eisenhower Presidential Library, American Foundation for the Blind, Feynman website, King Institute, Berkshire Hathaway, Joan Didion website, Fred Rogers Institute, official Mandela exhibition materials, Van Gogh Letters, UK Parliament, and TED. These are source or institutional records, not unsourced quotation collections. Institutional quotation records do not establish a quotation's first-ever utterance.

The epigraphs reproduce short, continuous passages, apart from the retained Bacon quotation's marked ellipsis. Opening capitalization and terminal punctuation are normalized where a clause is extracted from a longer sentence; typographic apostrophes and dashes follow the book. Poetic line breaks in Tennyson and Oliver are joined to suit the existing epigraph layout. Translated quotations identify the translator where relevant. Quotations from fiction remain attributed to their works.

## Bibliographic decisions

- **Chapter 2, Eisenhower:** the presidential library records the planning quotation in his 14 November 1957 remarks. He described it as an Army saying. The attribution identifies his speech without claiming he coined it.
- **Chapter 5, Shaw:** *Pygmalion*, Act V, follows the linked English book text. The date 1916 refers to the English book publication, rather than the play's earlier stage premiere.
- **Chapter 12, Shakespeare:** Folger's Act II, scene ii text places the quotation in Hamlet's answer to Rosencrantz, lines 268–270. The extracted clause begins after “for”; its first letter is capitalized.
- **Chapter 13, Aldous Huxley:** the sentence appears in Chapter III of *Brave New World*. It is a fictional remark about repeated messages, not a research estimate.
- **Chapter 15, Feynman:** the official website supplies the quotation and identifies the 1981 BBC interview. The attribution distinguishes the interview from the later book with the same title.
- **Chapters 16 and 24, Keller:** the American Foundation for the Blind dates the adventure quotation to 1941. Its progress-quotation page identifies the cooperation quotation as a vaudeville script. The approximate date c. 1920 reflects the performance period; AFB's [chronology](https://afb.org/about-afb/history/helen-keller/biography-and-chronology/chronology) dates the start of Keller and Sullivan's vaudeville work to February 1920. No unsupported originating book title is supplied.
- **Chapter 27, Buffett:** the quotation occurs in the 1986 shareholder letter, which is dated 27 February 1987. Both dates are retained in their proper roles.
- **Chapter 29, Montaigne:** “Of Cannibals” is numbered Book I, Chapter XXX in the linked Cotton/Hazlitt edition. The locator follows that edition, rather than Chapter XXXI used in some other editions.
- **Chapter 32, Feynman:** the retained quotation comes from “Cargo Cult Science,” his 1974 Caltech commencement address. The attribution now identifies the public address.
- **Chapter 34, Rogers:** the Fred Rogers Institute reproduces the quotation and cites *The World According to Mister Rogers* (2003), p. 93. The attribution follows that documented book location.
- **Chapter 37, Mandela:** official exhibition teaching materials hosted by Milwaukee Public Museum reproduce the quotation and attribute it to *Long Walk to Freedom* (1994). The chapter attribution states this source route.
- **Chapter 39, van Gogh:** the wording follows the authoritative English translation of letter 274 to Theo, dated 22 October 1882. It avoids the smoother but nonverbatim version often circulated online.
- **Chapter 41, Russell:** the linked 1922 Conway Memorial Lecture text uses “its exact opposite.” The quotation follows that edition.

The quotations introduce a theme or invite reflection. The scientific evidence and conclusions remain in the chapter text and its unchanged references.
