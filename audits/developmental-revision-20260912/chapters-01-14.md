## Checked study results and primary sources

| Claim used | Verification and boundary | Primary source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Everyday choice overload, Ch02 | Scheibehenne et al. report an average effect close to zero with substantial variation. Chernev et al. examine conditions under which overload is more likely; these are not interchangeable unconditional claims. | [Scheibehenne et al. (2010), original paper](https://digitalwellbeing.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/ScheibehenneGreifenederTodd2010.pdf); [Chernev et al. (2015), author paper](https://chernev.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ChoiceOverload_JCP_2015.pdf) |
| Inattentional blindness, Ch03 | Main experiment: 192 retained observers; 46% missed the unexpected event, averaged across sixteen task/display conditions. This is not a universal gorilla-miss rate or the result of a single video condition. | [Simons and Chabris (1999), author PDF](https://www.chabris.com/Simons1999.pdf) |
| Phone notifications, Ch03 | Notifications impaired the tested attention task even without direct phone interaction. The paragraph concerns interruption, not the mere presence of a phone, addiction, or a dopamine mechanism. The feed/stopping-point discussion is a design application, not a claim that this experiment tested infinite scroll. | [Stothart et al. (2015), original abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26121498/) |
| Iowa Gambling Task, Ch06 | Introduction belongs to the 1994 Cognition paper. The 1997 physiological/knowledge interpretation is qualified by Maia and McClelland's more sensitive questioning; the passage does not claim their study establishes that explicit knowledge caused the choices. | [Bechara et al. (1994)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8039375/); [Maia and McClelland (2004)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC528759/) |
| Forced compliance, Ch07 | Enjoyment ratings on −5 to +5: $1 group 1.35; $20 group −0.05; control −0.45. Twenty participants remained in each group. Original sample: 71 male introductory psychology students; eleven excluded. Conditions and exclusions are stated rather than presenting a universal attitude-change effect. | [Festinger and Carlsmith (1959), original paper at MIT](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Motivation/Festinger_Carlsmith_1959_Cognitive_consequences_of_forced_compliance.pdf), procedure and Table 1 |
| Affect and risk/benefit judgments, Ch09 | The cited experiment changed risk/benefit information and examined the corresponding movement in the other evaluation. The chapter uses the procedure to explain affective substitution without inventing a numerical result. | [Finucane et al. (2000), original paper](https://web.stanford.edu/~knutson/jdm/finucane00.pdf) |
| Myside bias and intelligence, Ch10 | Little relationship between cognitive ability and myside bias in the reviewed tasks; this does not identify an argument-generation mechanism or test whether education/training can improve performance. | [Stanovich et al. (2013), author PDF](https://maggietoplak.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Stanovich-K.-E.-West-R.-F.-Toplak-M.-E.-2013.-Myside-bias-rational-thinking-and-intelligence.pdf) |
| Motivated cognitive reflection, Ch10 | Kahan's ideologically patterned responses are described as a finding in the particular task, not a universal claim that greater intelligence creates more bias. | [Kahan (2013), original article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/ideology-motivated-reasoning-and-cognitive-reflection/F8A6A74C9022363D672B0FD14DD8B89F) |
| Self-serving attribution, Ch10 | Miller and Ross found greater support for success credit than failure blame and argued that information/expectations could explain some observations. Their skeptical interpretation is kept alongside the later broader meta-analysis. | [Miller and Ross (1975), original paper](https://www.bulidomics.com/w/images/7/70/Self-servingBiasesInTheAttributionOfCausalityFactOrFiction_Miller-Ross-1975.pdf) |
| Bias blind spot, Ch10 | Stronger blind spot predicted less use of other people's estimates in Study 4 and weaker response to debiasing training in Study 5; these are presented as associations, without treating blind spot as randomly manipulated. | [Scopelliti et al. (2015), accepted/published paper](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/5901/1/Blind%20Spot%20-%20published.pdf) |
| Wheel-of-fortune anchor, Ch11 | Starting numbers 10 and 65 were followed by higher/lower judgments and estimates; median estimates were 25% and 45%. No sample size is supplied in the chapter because the passage in the source does not provide one. | [Tversky and Kahneman (1974), original paper](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~aronatas/project/academic/tversky%20and%20kahneman%20judgment%20under%20uncertainty.pdf) |
| Attraction-effect limits, Ch11 | Qualitative, pictorial, tasted, or otherwise directly experienced attributes can weaken the effect relative to numerical attribute displays. The implication is to test the actual offer, not infer that attraction never occurs. | [Frederick et al. (2014), author paper](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/The-Limits-of-Attraction.pdf); [Yang and Lynn (2014), original article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmr.14.0020) |
| Disease-problem framing, Ch12 | Lives-saved version: N=152, 72% sure program. Lives-lost version: N=155, 22% sure and 78% gamble. These are different groups, not within-person switching rates. | [Tversky and Kahneman (1981), original paper](https://stanford.edu/class/psych205/papers/Tversky-Kahneman-1981.pdf), Problems 1–2; [alternate original scan](https://web.stanford.edu/~jlmcc/Presentations/tversky_kahneman_1981.pdf) |
| Question wording and memory reports, Ch12 | Experiment 1: 45 students in five groups of nine, seven films; contacted 31.8 mph, hit 34.0, smashed 40.8. Experiment 2: nonexistent-glass reports one week later, 16/50 smashed, 7/50 hit, 6/50 controls with no speed question. Immediate estimates and later reports remain separate outcomes. | [Loftus and Palmer (1974), original paper](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/1974-loftus.pdf), Tables 1–2 |
