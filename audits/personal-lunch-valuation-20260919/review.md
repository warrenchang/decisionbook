# Personal story: lunch, culture, and valuation

Added an expanded personal-experience note to Chapter 6, within “Experience and social meaning shape value,” immediately before the music examples. The story preserves the user's restaurant observation, weekend detail, rye-bread-and-carrot lunch, hot-lunch contrast, midday breaks, and opportunities to nap. The interpretation returns to how experience shapes what feels like a proper meal.

## Editorial and scientific decisions

- Treated restaurant quietness as the author's encounters at lunchtime, inferred from the lunch/dinner comparison in the supplied story, rather than a measured national restaurant-occupancy statistic.
- Kept meal patterns and breaks as the author's encountered routines, rather than universal claims about all Danish or Chinese people. Replaced “formal dinner” with the fuller meal/restaurant occasion the context describes.
- Separated a substantial meal's possible contribution to sleepiness from the endogenous afternoon dip and the timetable that permits a nap. Did not claim that carbohydrate-rich lunches caused national differences in institutional schedules.
- Added a compact methodological footnote and two primary references. An independent reviewer checked the proposed wording before insertion.

## Sources checked

- Monk, T. H., Buysse, D. J., Reynolds, C. F., III, & Kupfer, D. J. (1996). Circadian determinants of the postlunch dip in performance. Chronobiology International, 13(2), 123–133. https://doi.org/10.3109/07420529609037076 — original study; five of twelve participants showed a vigilance dip with hourly liquid supplements replacing lunch. Supports a contribution independent of a conventional lunch. Metadata and abstract verified at https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8877121/.
- Reyner, L. A., Wells, S. J., Mortlock, V., & Horne, J. A. (2012). ‘Post-lunch’ sleepiness during prolonged, monotonous driving—Effects of meal size. Physiology & Behavior, 105(4), 1088–1091. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physbeh.2011.11.025 — original counterbalanced experiment with twelve sleep-restricted men; heavier lunch increased EEG sleepiness and lane drifting. Energy, fat, and carbohydrate covaried, so this does not isolate a carbohydrate effect. Metadata and abstract verified at https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22155490/.
- Denmark's official food-culture pages describe a typical rye-bread lunch and family evening meals: https://denmark.dk/people-and-culture/recipes and https://denmark.dk/people-and-culture/cuisine. Consulted as contextual support, not as evidence for restaurant attendance or individual preferences.
- DTU's 2013 report on adults with shorter education describes rye-bread lunches and hot dinners in its 2005–2008 sample. Its restricted population does not warrant treating that pattern as universal: https://www.food.dtu.dk/-/media/institutter/foedevareinstituttet/publikationer/pub-2013/rapport-maaltidsvaner_kortuddannede_voksne.pdf.
- Orr et al. (1997), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9284488/, was checked by the independent reviewer: its small evening meal experiment did not detect significant sleep-latency differences between high-fat, high-carbohydrate, and mixed meals. This was not added as a new book citation because the note makes no nutrient-specific causal claim.
