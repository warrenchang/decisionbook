## Claim-to-evidence review

- **Thought suppression can backfire.** Wegner et al. (1987), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3612492/ and the author-hosted paper, report more white-bear thoughts during a subsequent expression period after prior suppression. The monitoring mechanism is attributed to Wegner's (1994) theoretical account, rather than represented as a direct neural measurement. Application of the focusing illusion to an urge is identified as an application, not a tested mediation.
- **An urge need not fade before behavior changes.** Bowen and Marlatt (2009), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20025372/: 123 undergraduate smokers randomly assigned to brief mindfulness instructions or a no-instruction comparison; no significant group difference in reported urges, fewer reported cigarettes in the mindfulness group over seven days. Moved this central finding into the main text.
- **Suppression can have different immediate and subsequent effects.** Erskine et al. (2010), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20660892/; author manuscript https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/2557/2/905037.pdf. Randomized groups; 90 recruited, 85 analyzed; middle-week thought manipulation within three weeks of daily monitoring. Suppression group smoked less in week 2 and more than both comparison groups in week 3. Participants had no current intention to quit. Do not claim stress mediated the rebound: the authors' observed association did not support that account.
- **Suppression is not invariably harmful.** Litvin et al. (2012), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3990001/: 162 adult smokers randomized to acceptance, suppression, or control. Suppression reduced smoking-related thoughts; both instructed strategies benefited some craving measures. No laboratory rebound or between-group difference in cigarettes during the three-day follow-up. The research lens reports these results alongside Erskine rather than claiming universal superiority of acceptance.
- **Rumination can impair useful thinking under low mood.** Lyubomirsky and Nolen-Hoeksema (1995), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7643299/ and the author-hosted paper. In three experiments, dysphoric participants induced to ruminate showed more negative interpretations/predictions and poorer interpersonal problem solving than comparable distracted participants. Preserve the low-mood population qualifier. This does not establish that self-blame globally reduces consciousness or attention.
- **Compassion can support renewed effort and repair.** Breines and Chen (2012), https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167212445599; full paper https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/publications/selfimp.motivation.pdf. Experiment 3: more study time than no intervention; the difference from the self-esteem condition was only marginal, and test performance did not significantly differ. Main text uses the supported no-intervention contrast and does not claim improved scores. Experiment 2: greater reported motivation to make amends and avoid repeating a transgression than self-esteem and positive-distraction conditions; these were motivations, not observed later apologies or recidivism.
- **Stress is not a universal switch to habitual behavior.** Schwabe and Wolf (2009), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19494141/, found reduced outcome sensitivity after acute stress. Zwosta et al. (2025), https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0327807, found no increased habit strength across two experiments with 129 participants. Footnote preserves this boundary and states that neither manipulation tests self-blame.

The self-blame → distress → familiar relief → repetition sequence is presented as a possible explanatory cycle in the bedtime example. It is not a demonstrated universal serial causal mechanism. “Negative energy” is not treated as a physical or neural substance; the discussion describes attention, affect, learned relief, and feasible alternatives. Accepting an urge is distinguished from carrying out the urged action. Recovery retains responsibility and repair without condemning personal worth.
