## Evidence and interpretation

- **Willpower:** The fixed-fuel interpretation is not an established explanation of self-control. The text distinguishes the large near-zero result in [Vohs et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797621989733) from the small effect in [Dang et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550619887702). A performance cost would not by itself identify a consumed resource. Fatigue and effort are not dismissed.
- **Behavior design:** [Duckworth et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615623247) supports intervening in situations before strong impulses arise. The student example is an application to test, rather than a reported intervention result.
- **Opportunity and craving:** [Dar et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017778) provides naturalistic flight-attendant evidence; [Bailey et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2009.02760.x) experimentally manipulated cigarette availability and deprivation. The account concerns reported craving and perceived access, not proof that withdrawal is irrelevant or all automatic impulses are consciously chosen.
- **Variable rewards:** [Shen et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucy062) supports repetition under specified uncertainty-resolution conditions, not a universal claim about automatic habit formation.
- **Investment:** Eyal's *Hooked* is identified as a product-design framework. The [author's account](https://www.nirandfar.com/makeyourusersdothework/) explains investment through future value and renewed triggers. Investment and variable rewards are possible supports for repetition, not requirements for every habit.
