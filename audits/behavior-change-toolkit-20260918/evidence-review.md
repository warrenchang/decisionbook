## Organization and coverage

The chapter now proceeds from goals and diagnosis to feasibility, prompts, plans, immediate appeal, commitment, attention, social support, maintenance, and evaluation. The term-paper example remains the main case. Existing studies, figures, table identifiers, research lenses, and legacy anchors are preserved.

| Family | Main-text treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Goals and meaning | Outcome versus behavior goals, SMART as a clarity checklist, learning goals, and explaining why the action matters. |
| Ability and environment | Lower friction for the desired action; raise friction for the competing action; prepare materials, resources, access, and time. Distinguish momentary feasibility from personal skill. |
| Skill and task size | Small first actions, graded tasks, instruction, demonstration, rehearsal, and feedback. |
| Cue and prompt | Reliable anchors, habit stacking, actionable reminders, appropriate timing and location, removal of competing prompts, and repeated cue–response practice. |
| Planning and substitution | Implementation intentions, WOOP, coping plans, and links to Chapter 21's replacement response and BRAIN practice. |
| Immediate consequences | Enjoyment, temptation bundling, contingent incentives, reinforcement versus punishment, and gamification. Concurrent bundling is distinguished from a reward after completion. |
| Advance structure | Defaults, automation, soft and hard commitment, time blocking, batching, and attention boundaries. |
| Social conditions | Practical and emotional support, agreed accountability, modeling, social comparison, and truthful norm messages. |
| Maintenance | Repetition, fresh starts, context change, flexible identity, and recovery after missed occasions. |
| Monitoring and learning | Self-monitoring as an intervention, actionable feedback, outcome quality, maintenance, costs, and revision of the initial diagnosis. |

The compact comparison table contains 17 practical families. It is not presented as all 93 items in a research taxonomy, nor as evidence that each named package works equally well. The exercise asks readers to choose one primary intervention before combining tools. Clinical treatment remains outside the everyday practice exercise.
