# Before/after demonstration — chapters/28, "## Authority and responsibility"

Chapter 28 is the flattest chapter in the book by every measure I computed (composite +6.26 against
a next-worst +2.99; longest paragraphs at 61 words; second-most-hedged; second-fewest short
sentences; 924 words per visual). It also contains the most inherently gripping material in the
manuscript. The gap is not the author's judgment — every caveat in it is correct and worth keeping.
The gap is that the findings are reported *about* rather than *shown*.

## BEFORE (verbatim, chapters/28-authority-groupthink-and-shared-responsibility.qmd)

> Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments remain among the most famous and disturbing studies in
> psychology. Participants were instructed by an experimenter to administer increasingly severe
> electric shocks to a learner whenever the learner made mistakes. The shocks were not real, but
> participants were led to believe that they were. Credibility was not uniform: archival reanalysis
> indicates that skepticism varied and may itself have increased continuation in some variants
> (Perry et al., 2020). Many participants nevertheless continued after the learner protested when
> the experimenter urged them onward (Milgram, 1963, 1974).
>
> Institutional trust, uncertainty about harm, gradual escalation, pressure to complete the task,
> and identification with the scientific project offer competing or interacting explanations
> (Haslam & Reicher, 2017). Together, these studies show how a legitimate-looking institutional
> sequence can produce continuation with apparently harmful actions despite visible discomfort,
> especially when responsibility seems to move upward. Burger (2009) reproduced substantial
> continuation to a lower stopping point in a modified protocol designed around modern ethical
> constraints.

Two paragraphs, 152 words, zero numbers, one 44-word sentence, four hedges, and the reader never
learns what actually happened.

## AFTER (proposed — same claims, same caveats, nothing added that the sources do not support)

> In Milgram's baseline study, forty men were told to shock a learner for every wrong answer, one
> step higher each time. The switches were labelled up to 450 volts. The learner — an actor —
> protested, then pounded the wall, then fell silent. Every one of the forty went past 300 volts.
> Twenty-six of the forty went to the end of the board (Milgram, 1963).
>
> That number is the finding. What it means is contested, and the contest matters more than the
> number. The shocks were not real, and participants did not uniformly believe they were: archival
> reanalysis indicates that skepticism varied and may itself have increased continuation in some
> variants (Perry et al., 2020). Institutional trust, uncertainty about harm, gradual escalation,
> pressure to finish, and identification with the scientific project all remain live explanations
> (Haslam & Reicher, 2017). Burger (2009) rebuilt the procedure to stop at 150 volts and still
> found most participants willing to continue past it.
>
> What survives every one of those qualifications is the shape of the thing. A legitimate-looking
> institutional sequence, run by someone who accepts the consequences on your behalf, can carry
> people past the point their own discomfort has already marked. Responsibility moved upward, and
> the hand on the switch stayed where it was.

## What changed, and what deliberately did not

- Every hedge with epistemic content is kept: "contested", "varied and may itself have increased",
  "remain live explanations". The word "Many" — which hid a number — is gone.
- The published figures do the work the adjectives were doing. "Famous and disturbing" is deleted;
  showing 26 of 40 is what makes it disturbing. Telling the reader a study is disturbing is the
  weakest available move.
- The 44-word sentence is broken up, and three short sentences carry the emphasis.
- The caveats move AFTER the finding rather than interrupting it, so the reader knows what is being
  qualified before the qualification arrives. This is the single most transferable fix in the book.
- One image-bearing clause at the end ("the hand on the switch stayed where it was") replaces the
  abstract "continuation with apparently harmful actions".

VERIFY BEFORE PUBLISHING: confirm 26/40 and the all-past-300 figure against Milgram (1963) p. 376,
and Burger's (2009) continuation rate against the published paper. I am recommending the *move*,
not certifying the digits.

## The same fix, applied twice more in this chapter
- Bystander section: Darley and Latane (1968) is currently described with no rates at all. The
  contrast between participants who believed they were alone with the victim and those who believed
  four others were present is the entire point and is currently invisible. Add both published
  percentages in the sentence.
- Fischer et al. (2011) is given as "a meta-analysis covering more than 7,700 participants found an
  overall negative individual-level effect". Give the effect size. A reader cannot tell whether
  "negative" means decisive or marginal.
