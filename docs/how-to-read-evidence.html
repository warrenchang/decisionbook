How to Read the Evidence in This Book

Behavioral science contains durable findings, useful models, context-sensitive effects, and claims that became more famous than their evidence deserved. The right response is neither automatic belief nor automatic cynicism. It is calibrated trust.

Begin with the claim

Before judging a study, make the proposed claim explicit.

  • Is it descriptive—a pattern occurred in a defined sample and task?
  • Is it predictive—the observation improves forecasts in a specified population?
  • Is it causal—changing one factor changed an outcome under defensible identification?
  • Is it mechanistic—the evidence distinguishes why the effect occurred from other explanations?
  • Is it general—the claim is expected to travel across people, institutions, cultures, and time?

Evidence that supports the first claim may not support the fifth. A laboratory manipulation can identify a causal contrast while saying little about its frequency outside the task. A large observational association can travel across settings while leaving the causal direction unresolved. The strength of evidence cannot be judged without the target claim.

Separate size from certainty

A p-value is not an effect size, the probability that a theory is true, or evidence that an estimate is practically important. Ask instead:

  1. How large is the estimated difference or association in meaningful units?
  2. How uncertain is that estimate?
  3. Which values remain compatible with the data and design?
  4. Would the smallest plausible effect matter for the decision at hand?

A noisy estimate can be statistically significant and exaggerated. A precise estimate can be small but important when applied repeatedly or across a large population. The book therefore treats magnitude, uncertainty, and decision relevance as separate questions.

Ask what was measured

Constructs such as trust, risk preference, happiness, cooperation, attention, and persuasion are not observed directly. They are inferred from answers, choices, response times, behavior, physiology, or model parameters. The measure may capture the intended construct together with comprehension, incentives, demand effects, language, social meaning, or measurement error.

When two mechanisms predict the same observation, the observation does not identify the mechanism by itself. A person who rejects an unequal offer may care about inequality, reciprocity, reputation, norms, anger, or strategic teaching. A person who delays a purchase may be patient, cash constrained, uncertain, or distrustful. Strong evidence rules out plausible alternatives rather than attaching a confident label to ambiguous behavior.

Distinguish an illustration from evidence

A case, photograph, video, historical episode, or classroom demonstration can make a mechanism visible. It cannot by itself establish prevalence, size, causality, or generality. In this book:

  • illustrations help the reader see a possibility;
  • single studies identify a defined comparison under stated conditions;
  • replications and multi-study programs test whether the pattern survives new samples and implementations;
  • meta-analyses synthesize a literature but inherit its selection, measurement, and comparability problems;
  • field evidence tests behavior in consequential settings but often sacrifices experimental control;
  • formal models make assumptions and implications precise but are not empirical confirmation by themselves.

The useful question is not which category is automatically best. It is which combination of evidence is needed for the claim.

Treat replication as information, not a verdict

A failed replication can reflect a false or exaggerated original finding, a context-sensitive effect, a weak manipulation, measurement differences, sampling variation, or an implementation failure. A successful replication does not make a claim universal. Replication changes the weight assigned to competing explanations.

Results are especially vulnerable when studies are underpowered, many analytical choices are available, only successful results become visible, or a surprising estimate is treated as its true long-run size. Preregistration, adequate precision, transparent exclusions, robustness checks, registered reports, data and code sharing, and independent replication reduce these risks. They do not replace judgment; they make judgment more inspectable.

Ask whether the finding travels

Generalization requires a theory of what matters. Consider:

  • the population and how participants were recruited;
  • incentives and stakes;
  • whether the task was familiar or artificial;
  • cultural and institutional meaning;
  • timing, feedback, and repeated experience;
  • the comparison condition;
  • implementation fidelity;
  • whether the outcome was immediate, behavioral, or self-reported.

A boundary condition is explanatory evidence. If an effect appears only when preferences are uncertain, options are difficult to compare, or social observation is public, that pattern helps identify the mechanism. “It depends” becomes scientific when the dependence is specified in advance and tested.

CautionEvidence Boundary

No checklist turns evidence evaluation into a mechanical score. A transparent study can still ask a weak question, and an imperfect study can still contain useful information. The aim is to align confidence with the design, estimate, replication record, measurement, and scope of the claim.

A six-question evidence audit

For any memorable claim, ask:

  1. What exactly is being claimed? Description, prediction, causation, mechanism, or generality?
  2. What is the comparison? Compared with what, for whom, and over what period?
  3. How large and uncertain is the estimate? Not merely whether it crossed a threshold.
  4. What else could produce the observation? Identify at least one competing mechanism.
  5. What would make the result weaker or fail to travel? State the boundary.
  6. What evidence would change my confidence? Make the belief answerable to future information.
