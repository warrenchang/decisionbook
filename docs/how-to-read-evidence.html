How to Read the Evidence in This Book

Imagine a training provider advertising a programme that “raises productivity by 20%.” That number is difficult to use until we know what was compared. Did participants improve relative to their own earlier performance, or relative to people who did not receive the training? Were they randomly assigned? Did the study measure actual work, a test score, or participants’ impressions?

The following questions help turn a memorable result into a claim we can evaluate.

Begin with the claim

A descriptive claim reports what happened in a sample. A predictive claim says an observation helps forecast another outcome. A causal claim says that changing something produces a difference. A mechanistic claim goes further by identifying how the difference arises. A claim of generality says that the result should extend to other people or settings.

The training participants might improve because of the programme, because unusually motivated people enrolled, or because a temporary slump ended. An association between attendance and improvement would not separate those explanations. Even a credible causal effect would leave questions about why the programme worked and whether it would work elsewhere.

Read the boundary with the finding

A useful account of evidence tells you the supported claim, its important limit, a plausible alternative explanation, and what further observation would change confidence. You will find these elements in the chapter discussions and evidence boxes.

For the training example, a limit such as “the outcome was a short knowledge test” changes the practical implication. It would give us a reason to examine learning, while leaving workplace performance to be tested. The qualification belongs beside the finding because it changes what the finding means.

Separate size from certainty

Ask how large the estimated difference is in meaningful units, how uncertain the estimate is, and whether the range of plausible effects would matter for the decision. A 20% relative increase could be large or small in absolute terms, depending on the starting level and the outcome being measured.

A p-value is not an effect size or the probability that a theory is true. A statistically significant result can be too small to matter in practice, while an imprecisely estimated effect may still include important possibilities. Magnitude, uncertainty, and practical relevance need separate attention.

Ask what was measured

Trust, happiness, attention, and similar constructs are inferred from observations such as answers, choices, response times, or physiological measures. Each measure captures some aspects more directly than others.

A person who rejects an unequal offer might dislike inequality, want to punish the proposer, or be concerned about reputation. The rejection alone does not identify the motive. A convincing explanation needs a comparison that helps distinguish the alternatives, rather than a new label for the same observation.

Distinguish an illustration from evidence

Fictional cases and classroom demonstrations help make an idea concrete. They show how something could happen. A study investigates a specified comparison; replications examine whether the result survives new samples or implementations. Field studies examine behavior in everyday or organizational settings and may be observational or experimental. Formal models make assumptions and their implications explicit.

A meta-analysis combines studies but inherits difficulties in the available literature, including selective publication and differences in measurement. No source type answers every question. Look for the evidence that bears on the particular claim.

Treat replication as information, not a verdict

A failed replication may weaken confidence in an original finding, reveal a boundary, or expose a difference in measurement or implementation. Those possibilities need evidence of their own. A successful replication strengthens support within the conditions tested; it does not make the result universal.

Low precision, flexible analysis, and selective publication can make a literature look more convincing than it is. Preregistration, transparent reporting, accessible data and code, and independent replication help readers examine these problems. Appendix F’s evidence-status map applies this approach to prominent behavioral findings.

Ask whether the finding travels

Return to the training claim. Results from volunteers may differ from results among employees required to attend; a short course may work differently when instructors, incentives, or opportunities to practise change. The outcome and follow-up period matter too.

Generalization becomes more informative when we specify which differences should matter and test them. “It depends” is the beginning of an explanation only when we can say what it depends on.

A six-question evidence audit

For any claim you might use, ask:

  1. What exactly is being claimed? Description, prediction, causation, mechanism, or generality?
  2. What is the comparison? Compared with what, for whom, and over what period?
  3. How large and uncertain is the estimate? Consider the units and the range of plausible values.
  4. What else could explain the observation? Identify a competing account.
  5. Where might the finding change? Consider the population, task, and setting.
  6. What evidence would change my confidence? Make the belief open to revision.

You do not need to become a methods specialist before reading the book. Keep these questions nearby, and return to them when a result surprises you or seems useful. Appendices E and F develop the research methods behind them.
