Part II Review

These ten questions connect ideas from Chapters 8–15. Use the assumptions given in each question and choose the one best answer. Work through the complete set before checking the explanations.

Practice questions

Choose one answer for each of the 10 questions. Complete the set, then select Check answers to see your score and explanations. Each question is worth one point.

1. An estimate starts near an arbitrary number supplied by an interviewer. Separately, a risk estimate rises because a dramatic example comes easily to mind. Which pair fits, in order?

2. A report changes “8% failures” to “92% successes” for the same binary outcome. Another task presents cooking words before an ambiguous word. Which processes are manipulated, respectively?

3. A policy slogan claiming that a reform cuts waiting times is easy to process and repeated often. Its supporters accept it without checking the evidence. Which procedure most directly tests its accuracy?

4. A recruitment panel wants to prevent an engaging interview from dominating unrelated skill judgments. Which procedure best targets that problem?

5. Every application has a final outcome recorded as either success or failure. A report says 3% fail. Its complementary frame must describe the same applications and period. Which statement qualifies?

6. A trader says a new ritual improves forecasting accuracy. Which test most directly estimates its causal effect on comparable forecasting tasks?

7. An applicant resembles a successful engineer. A selection test was validated on experienced engineers, but the current applicant pool consists of novices with a different base rate. What should a probability estimate use?

8. The lowest-scoring branches receive coaching and improve next month. Which comparison best tests whether coaching contributed beyond regression to the mean?

9. A project succeeds if at least one of two independent components works. Each component works with probability 0.8. Which pair gives equivalent success and failure descriptions for the project?

10. Across 200 resolved events, A assigned 20% to 100 events, of which 20 occurred, and 80% to 100 events, of which 80 occurred. B assigned 50% to all 200. What does this record show?

0 of 10 answered

Answers and explanations

  1. Question 1: B

    A. Availability; anchoring (Incorrect)
    This reverses the mechanisms.
    B. Anchoring; availability (Correct)
    The first uses a numerical starting point; the second uses ease of recall.
    C. Priming; regression to the mean (Incorrect)
    The second is not a repeated extreme measurement.
    D. Halo; conjunction fallacy (Incorrect)
    Neither a global impression nor a probability conjunction is described.
    E. Framing; overplacement (Incorrect)
    No equivalent descriptions or ranking of personal ability is specified.

    Review: Chapter 9 · Chapter 11. Back to question 1

  2. Question 2: C

    A. Defaults and anchoring (Incorrect)
    No preselected option or initial numerical estimate is manipulated.
    B. Semantic priming and framing (Incorrect)
    This reverses the described manipulations.
    C. Framing and semantic priming (Correct)
    The first changes equivalent description; the second changes concept accessibility.
    D. Loss aversion and overprecision (Incorrect)
    Neither outcome demonstrates these particular judgment patterns.
    E. Mere exposure and a conjunction error (Incorrect)
    Repetition-based liking and intersecting probabilities are not the tasks.

    Review: Chapter 12 · Chapter 13. Back to question 2

  3. Question 3: A

    A. State a falsifiable prediction and examine independent outcome data. (Correct)
    This replaces fluency and repetition with an evidential test.
    B. Compare how much the two sides like their slogans. (Incorrect)
    Liking is not a test of the claim.
    C. Use a harder font to make the slogan feel less familiar. (Incorrect)
    Lower fluency does not itself establish falsity or truth.
    D. Ask supporters how natural it sounds. (Incorrect)
    Naturalness is another fluency judgment.
    E. Repeat it to a larger group and count recognition. (Incorrect)
    Recognition measures exposure, not accuracy.

    Review: Chapter 10 · Chapter 13. Back to question 3

  4. Question 4: D

    A. Remove the work sample because it takes longer to assess. (Incorrect)
    This discards relevant evidence in favor of easier impressions.
    B. Give the first speaker more time to defend the favorite candidate. (Incorrect)
    This can strengthen early impressions rather than test them.
    C. Use an arbitrary numerical starting score for everyone. (Incorrect)
    This introduces an anchor without adding skill evidence.
    D. Score each job-relevant dimension before making an overall rating. (Correct)
    Separate initial assessments reduce spillover from a global impression.
    E. Discuss overall likability before reviewing any work sample. (Incorrect)
    This establishes the impression likely to contaminate later ratings.

    Review: Chapter 8 · Chapter 11. Back to question 4

  5. Question 5: B

    A. 3% of accepted applicants later fail. (Incorrect)
    This uses a new conditional denominator and outcome.
    B. 97% of those applications succeed. (Correct)
    Success complements failure within the specified population.
    C. 97 applications succeed. (Incorrect)
    A count needs the total number of applications to express the same rate.
    D. 3% fewer applications fail than last year. (Incorrect)
    This introduces a comparison over time absent from the original.
    E. 97% of rejected applicants would succeed elsewhere. (Incorrect)
    This changes both the population and the outcome.

    Review: Chapter 12 · Chapter 14. Back to question 5

  6. Question 6: C

    A. Ask successful traders whether they remember using the ritual. (Incorrect)
    Selection and retrospective recall bias the comparison.
    B. Compare confidence before and after performing the ritual. (Incorrect)
    Confidence can change without forecast accuracy changing.
    C. Randomly assign ritual and no-ritual trials, then score all forecasts. (Correct)
    Randomization and complete prospective scoring separate the ritual from selected successes.
    D. Compare voluntary ritual users with people who decline it. (Incorrect)
    Self-selection may explain performance differences.
    E. Compare forecasts before and after the ritual began. (Incorrect)
    Changes in task difficulty or experience can confound the comparison.

    Review: Chapter 8 · Chapter 15. Back to question 6

  7. Question 7: C

    A. The test's sensitivity as the probability of success after passing. (Incorrect)
    This reverses the relevant conditional.
    B. Equal prior probabilities because the applicant is unfamiliar. (Incorrect)
    Unfamiliarity does not imply a 50–50 base rate.
    C. A justified novice reference class and test performance relevant to that class. (Correct)
    Resemblance alone cannot supply the prior or justify transporting the likelihoods.
    D. The number of favorable adjectives in the applicant's description. (Incorrect)
    Descriptive detail does not determine diagnostic evidence.
    E. The engineer base rate because the applicant matches the prototype. (Incorrect)
    Resemblance does not establish membership in the validated population.

    Review: Chapter 9 · Chapter 14. Back to question 7

  8. Question 8: E

    A. Attribute every gain after coaching to the intervention. (Incorrect)
    Temporal order alone does not isolate the causal contribution.
    B. Compare only each coached branch with its own worst month. (Incorrect)
    This retains the selection-on-extremes problem.
    C. Compare coached low scorers with the highest-scoring branches. (Incorrect)
    Different initial selection creates different regression tendencies.
    D. Ask coaches to select only branches that later improved. (Incorrect)
    Selection on outcomes biases the assessment.
    E. Randomly assign similarly low-scoring branches to coaching or control. (Correct)
    Both groups are selected on low scores, separating treatment from expected regression.

    Review: Chapter 10 · Chapter 15. Back to question 8

  9. Question 9: E

    A. 80% success and 20% failure (Incorrect)
    These are individual-component rates.
    B. 64% success and 36% failure (Incorrect)
    This treats success as requiring both components to work.
    C. 40% success and 60% failure (Incorrect)
    These do not follow from either the rule or independence.
    D. 100% success and 0% failure (Incorrect)
    Independent redundancy reduces failure without eliminating it.
    E. 96% success and 4% failure (Correct)
    Failure requires both components to fail: 0.2×0.2 = 0.04.

    Review: Chapter 12 · Chapter 14. Back to question 9

  10. Question 10: D

    A. Both are calibrated; B has greater resolution. (Incorrect)
    B assigns a constant probability and does not distinguish risk groups.
    B. Only B is calibrated; A has greater resolution. (Incorrect)
    A's two forecast bins each match their outcome frequency.
    C. Neither is calibrated; both have equal resolution. (Incorrect)
    The observed frequencies match both sets of stated probabilities.
    D. Both are calibrated on this record; A has greater resolution. (Correct)
    Both match frequencies at their stated probabilities, while A separates lower- from higher-frequency groups.
    E. Only A is calibrated; both have equal resolution. (Incorrect)
    B's 50% equals the overall 100/200 frequency, and A separates risks better.

    Review: Chapter 8 · Chapter 15. Back to question 10
