Part V Review

These ten questions connect ideas from Chapters 30–34. Use the assumptions given in each question and choose the one best answer. Work through the complete set before checking the explanations.

Practice questions

Choose one answer for each of the 10 questions. Complete the set, then select Check answers to see your score and explanations. Each question is worth one point.

1. A reader correctly explains a proposal but rejects it because its costs conflict with her priorities. Which conclusion follows?

2. A presentation opens with one moving patient story and then claims a treatment usually works. What evidence is missing for the frequency claim?

3. A manager says a pilot is optional but uses a threatening tone and implies refusal will be remembered. Which redesign best preserves meaningful choice?

4. A story increases sympathy for a proposal's beneficiary, but the audience still worries about funding. What is the most useful next step?

5. A colleague says a schedule change made her feel disregarded. Which response best checks understanding before proposing a fix?

6. A brief follows the STORY drafting framework and is easy for readers to summarize, but omits uncertainty that could change the recommended action. What revision best completes the message?

7. Two messages are randomly assigned. The study detects higher perceived credibility for one message but no reliable differences in comprehension or uptake. What result should the report state?

8. Two colleagues agree that a missed handoff was caused by an omitted message, but one still feels publicly humiliated by the accusation. What remains to be addressed?

9. A training advertisement features a top performer whose income doubled after attending. There is no comparison group, and attendees selected themselves. Which revised claim best fits the evidence?

10. Staff accurately explain a proposed workflow, believe it could work, but object that it removes professional discretion. Which response most directly addresses the remaining disagreement?

0 of 10 answered

Answers and explanations

  1. Question 1: D

    A. A clearer font would necessarily resolve the dispute. (Incorrect)
    The obstacle is a substantive cost–priority conflict.
    B. Rejection proves the proposal was not understood. (Incorrect)
    The reader has demonstrated understanding.
    C. The reader's priorities must be factually mistaken. (Incorrect)
    Values are not shown to be erroneous by disagreement alone.
    D. Understanding the message does not require agreeing with it. (Correct)
    Grounding and persuasion are different accomplishments.
    E. Agreement can be established by repeating the same words. (Incorrect)
    Repetition need not change the underlying trade-off.

    Review: Chapter 30 · Chapter 33. Back to question 1

  2. Question 2: C

    A. Several quotations from the same patient (Incorrect)
    Repeated testimony from one case remains one case.
    B. A shorter name for the treatment (Incorrect)
    Naming does not establish effectiveness.
    C. Representative outcome data for comparable patients (Correct)
    One case does not establish how commonly recovery occurs.
    D. A photograph of the presenter (Incorrect)
    Appearance does not supply outcome evidence.
    E. A more dramatic ending to the same story (Incorrect)
    Drama does not identify an outcome rate.

    Review: Chapter 31 · Chapter 32. Back to question 2

  3. Question 3: D

    A. Replace the threatening words while preserving the penalty. (Incorrect)
    Practical freedom remains compromised.
    B. Keep the implied penalty but add the word voluntary twice. (Incorrect)
    Labels cannot cancel coercive consequences.
    C. Count quiet agreement as informed acceptance. (Incorrect)
    Silence may reflect pressure rather than support.
    D. Remove refusal penalties and explain the options without pressure. (Correct)
    Both the practical consequences and delivery should support autonomy.
    E. Offer more promotional stories without explaining refusal. (Incorrect)
    Stories do not resolve the choice constraint.

    Review: Chapter 30 · Chapter 32. Back to question 3

  4. Question 4: C

    A. Treat sympathy as proof that the funding plan is feasible. (Incorrect)
    Emotional response does not establish feasibility.
    B. Repeat the story until the funding question is no longer raised. (Incorrect)
    Repetition avoids the decision-relevant concern.
    C. Address the funding concern with evidence while retaining the story's concrete perspective. (Correct)
    Emotional engagement and the unresolved substantive objection need different support.
    D. Label the question a lack of empathy. (Incorrect)
    This treats legitimate scrutiny as a character defect.
    E. Omit all cost information to preserve the story's impact. (Incorrect)
    That undermines informed evaluation.

    Review: Chapter 30 · Chapter 31. Back to question 4

  5. Question 5: E

    A. “The schedule is efficient, so there is no problem.” (Incorrect)
    Efficiency does not resolve the relational concern.
    B. “I know exactly how you feel, so let us move on.” (Incorrect)
    Certainty closes the opportunity to correct your understanding.
    C. “You should use the calendar more carefully.” (Incorrect)
    Advice arrives before checking what mattered.
    D. “Everyone feels that way about schedules.” (Incorrect)
    Generalization replaces the colleague's specific meaning.
    E. “Was being excluded the main concern, or have I misunderstood?” (Correct)
    A tentative paraphrase invites correction of the inferred concern.

    Review: Chapter 33 · Chapter 34. Back to question 5

  6. Question 6: B

    A. Add another story illustrating the preferred outcome. (Incorrect)
    Illustration does not reveal the consequential uncertainty.
    B. Keep the clear structure and add the uncertain assumption and its decision implications. (Correct)
    A drafting aid organizes communication; it does not replace evidence or uncertainty checks.
    C. Treat accurate paraphrasing as proof that the recommendation is correct. (Incorrect)
    Understanding does not establish evidential adequacy.
    D. Remove the structure and retain only the central recommendation. (Incorrect)
    This neither preserves clarity nor supplies the missing assumption.
    E. Describe the framework as validation of the underlying evidence. (Incorrect)
    A communication framework is not a research design.

    Review: Chapter 32 · Chapter 33. Back to question 6

  7. Question 7: E

    A. The message established the underlying claim's truth. (Incorrect)
    Perceived credibility is not external verification.
    B. The audience understood the message better. (Incorrect)
    No reliable improvement in comprehension was detected.
    C. The message improved actual follow-through. (Incorrect)
    No reliable improvement in uptake was detected.
    D. The message lowered all resistance mechanisms. (Incorrect)
    A single rating does not measure every mechanism.
    E. The detected difference concerns perceived credibility. (Correct)
    The reported effect should match the measured outcome that changed.

    Review: Chapter 30 · Chapter 32. Back to question 7

  8. Question 8: D

    A. An instruction to treat shared understanding as forgiveness (Incorrect)
    Understanding does not obligate forgiveness.
    B. Only a second statistical test of the same message log (Incorrect)
    The remaining issue is not uncertainty about the cause.
    C. A stronger accusation to ensure future compliance (Incorrect)
    This would intensify the identified harm.
    D. Repair of the unfair public account and its relational impact (Correct)
    Agreement on facts does not by itself repair the harm.
    E. Proof that the harmed colleague has no emotions (Incorrect)
    Emotions can identify a real consequence needing attention.

    Review: Chapter 33 · Chapter 34. Back to question 8

  9. Question 9: A

    A. Income doubled afterward; the training's causal contribution remains unknown. (Correct)
    Selection and other changes prevent causal attribution from the story alone.
    B. Training caused this person's full income increase. (Incorrect)
    No counterfactual isolates that causal contribution.
    C. Self-selection proves that the training had zero effect. (Incorrect)
    Failure to identify an effect is not proof of no effect.
    D. Training doubled income for a typical attendee. (Incorrect)
    The selected case does not estimate a typical outcome.
    E. The case establishes the expected gain for a new applicant. (Incorrect)
    Transport to new applicants is unsupported.

    Review: Chapter 31 · Chapter 32. Back to question 9

  10. Question 10: D

    A. Add another technical success story. (Incorrect)
    They already accept its potential feasibility.
    B. Interpret the objection as evidence of deficient listening. (Incorrect)
    Accurate explanation shows that understanding is present.
    C. Repeat the instructions more slowly. (Incorrect)
    Staff already understand the procedure.
    D. Negotiate which decisions require discretion and revise those boundaries. (Correct)
    The unresolved issue is value and control, not basic comprehension or feasibility.
    E. Test whether staff can recall the platform's name. (Incorrect)
    Name recall does not address discretion.

    Review: Chapter 30 · Chapter 33 · Chapter 34. Back to question 10
