Part V Review

These ten questions connect ideas from Chapters 30–34. Use the assumptions given in each question and choose the one best answer. Work through the complete set before checking the explanations.

Practice questions

Choose one answer for each of the 10 questions. Complete the set, then select Check answers to see your score and explanations. Each question is worth one point.

1. A client accurately summarizes a proposal but declines it. What should the adviser investigate before preparing another explanation?

2. A presentation opens with a moving patient story and claims that similar patients usually recover after the treatment. Which evidence best supports the frequency claim?

3. A manager says a pilot is optional but uses a threatening tone and implies refusal will be remembered. Which redesign best preserves meaningful choice?

4. A story increases sympathy for a proposal's beneficiary, but the audience still worries about funding. What is the most useful next step?

5. A colleague says a schedule change made her feel disregarded. Which response best checks understanding before proposing a fix?

6. A policy brief recommends expanding a service. The expansion is worthwhile at high demand but costly at low demand, and future demand is uncertain. Which addition would best help readers assess the recommendation?

7. A message earns higher credibility ratings in a randomized study. The team is considering using it to increase completed vaccinations. Which follow-up would best test that objective?

8. After publicly blaming a colleague for a missed handoff, a manager discovers that an omitted instruction caused the problem. Which response most directly repairs the harm from the accusation?

9. A training advertisement features a top performer whose income doubled after attending. There is no comparison group, and attendees selected themselves. Which revised claim best fits the evidence?

10. A workflow pilot succeeds on routine cases, but staff report that unusual cases require exceptions the system blocks. A manager proposes a more persuasive presentation before expanding the pilot. Which alternative best tests the substance of the objection?

0 of 10 answered

Answers and explanations

  1. Question 1: D

    A. Which communication channel would make the same explanation more familiar (Incorrect)
    Familiarity with a channel does not identify the client's substantive objection.
    B. Which technical terms need a simpler definition (Incorrect)
    This targets a comprehension problem despite the accurate summary.
    C. Which visual layout would make the summary easier to scan (Incorrect)
    Improved presentation does not directly investigate why an understood proposal was rejected.
    D. Which consequences conflict with the client's priorities (Correct)
    Accurate understanding leaves open substantive disagreement about the proposal's value to the client.
    E. Which section should be repeated more prominently (Incorrect)
    Repetition need not resolve disagreement about consequences.

    Review: Chapter 30 · Chapter 33. Back to question 1

  2. Question 2: C

    A. The number of favorable treatment stories reported in the local press (Incorrect)
    Media selection and an absent patient denominator prevent a recovery-rate estimate.
    B. Clinicians' recollections of the cases they found most striking (Incorrect)
    Memorable cases can be unrepresentative of the patients treated.
    C. Follow-up outcomes from a representative sample of comparable patients (Correct)
    An appropriate sample with outcome follow-up supports an estimate of how frequently recovery occurs; it does not by itself establish a treatment effect.
    D. Satisfaction ratings from patients who completed the full treatment (Incorrect)
    Satisfaction among completers is neither recovery frequency nor a representative outcome measure for all comparable patients.
    E. Detailed accounts from several patients selected for successful recovery (Incorrect)
    Selected successes do not provide the denominator needed to estimate a recovery rate.

    Review: Chapter 31 · Chapter 32. Back to question 2

  3. Question 3: D

    A. Replace the threatening words while preserving the penalty. (Incorrect)
    Practical freedom remains compromised.
    B. Keep the implied penalty but add the word voluntary twice. (Incorrect)
    Labels cannot cancel coercive consequences.
    C. Count quiet agreement as informed acceptance. (Incorrect)
    Silence may reflect pressure rather than support.
    D. Remove refusal penalties and explain the options without pressure. (Correct)
    Both the practical consequences and delivery should support autonomy.
    E. Offer more promotional stories without explaining refusal. (Incorrect)
    Stories do not resolve the choice constraint.

    Review: Chapter 30 · Chapter 32. Back to question 3

  4. Question 4: C

    A. Treat sympathy as proof that the funding plan is feasible. (Incorrect)
    Emotional response does not establish feasibility.
    B. Repeat the story until the funding question is no longer raised. (Incorrect)
    Repetition avoids the decision-relevant concern.
    C. Address the funding concern with evidence while retaining the story's concrete perspective. (Correct)
    Emotional engagement and the unresolved substantive objection need different support.
    D. Label the question a lack of empathy. (Incorrect)
    This treats legitimate scrutiny as a character defect.
    E. Omit all cost information to preserve the story's impact. (Incorrect)
    That undermines informed evaluation.

    Review: Chapter 30 · Chapter 31. Back to question 4

  5. Question 5: E

    A. “The schedule is efficient, so there is no problem.” (Incorrect)
    Efficiency does not resolve the relational concern.
    B. “I know exactly how you feel, so let us move on.” (Incorrect)
    Certainty closes the opportunity to correct your understanding.
    C. “You should use the calendar more carefully.” (Incorrect)
    Advice arrives before checking what mattered.
    D. “Everyone feels that way about schedules.” (Incorrect)
    Generalization replaces the colleague's specific meaning.
    E. “Was being excluded the main concern, or have I misunderstood?” (Correct)
    A tentative paraphrase invites correction of the inferred concern.

    Review: Chapter 33 · Chapter 34. Back to question 5

  6. Question 6: B

    A. A comprehensive list of forecasting difficulties arranged by data source (Incorrect)
    Cataloguing difficulties does not establish which ones can change the choice.
    B. A demand range and the level at which expansion ceases to be worthwhile (Correct)
    These connect the uncertainty to the decision rather than merely acknowledging that estimates may be wrong.
    C. A general limitations paragraph noting that future conditions may change (Incorrect)
    A general caveat does not show when uncertainty would alter the recommended action.
    D. A precise central demand estimate accompanied by the authors' confidence rating (Incorrect)
    A central estimate and confidence rating do not reveal the decision's sensitivity to other plausible demand levels.
    E. A successful expansion elsewhere with a similar estimated average demand (Incorrect)
    A comparison case may be informative but does not locate the relevant decision threshold for this service.

    Review: Chapter 32 · Chapter 33. Back to question 6

  7. Question 7: E

    A. Compare how accurately recipients remember the messages after a week. (Incorrect)
    Memory is a possible intermediate outcome, not the intended completed behavior.
    B. Ask a larger sample how likely they would be to share each message. (Incorrect)
    Sharing intentions need not translate into recipients completing vaccination.
    C. Repeat the study with a more detailed scale measuring the message's credibility. (Incorrect)
    More precise credibility measurement does not establish an effect on completed vaccinations.
    D. Compare recipients' ratings of the medical expertise conveyed by each message. (Incorrect)
    Perceived expertise is another judgment about the message, not a direct test of the target behavior.
    E. Randomly assign the messages in the intended setting and compare completed vaccinations. (Correct)
    This tests the behavioral outcome the team aims to change rather than treating credibility as an established substitute for it.

    Review: Chapter 30 · Chapter 32. Back to question 7

  8. Question 8: D

    A. Ask the team to stop discussing the incident and focus on the next delivery. (Incorrect)
    Moving attention elsewhere leaves the unfair account in place.
    B. Privately send the missing instruction and agree on the next handoff date. (Incorrect)
    This helps the work resume but leaves the public accusation uncorrected.
    C. Explain privately why the available information originally suggested the colleague was at fault. (Incorrect)
    Explaining the mistake to the colleague does not repair the public record.
    D. Correct the account before the same audience and acknowledge the colleague's treatment. (Correct)
    Repair should address the false public account and the harm it caused, alongside any operational correction.
    E. Introduce a checklist that prevents similar omissions in future handoffs. (Incorrect)
    A process safeguard does not correct what colleagues were told about this incident.

    Review: Chapter 33 · Chapter 34. Back to question 8

  9. Question 9: A

    A. Income doubled afterward; the training's causal contribution remains unknown. (Correct)
    Selection and other changes prevent causal attribution from the story alone.
    B. Training caused this person's full income increase. (Incorrect)
    No counterfactual isolates that causal contribution.
    C. Self-selection proves that the training had zero effect. (Incorrect)
    Failure to identify an effect is not proof of no effect.
    D. Training doubled income for a typical attendee. (Incorrect)
    The selected case does not estimate a typical outcome.
    E. The case establishes the expected gain for a new applicant. (Incorrect)
    Transport to new applicants is unsupported.

    Review: Chapter 31 · Chapter 32. Back to question 9

  10. Question 10: D

    A. Give staff a simpler explanation of why consistent procedures improve performance. (Incorrect)
    The value of consistency does not establish that blocked exceptions are unnecessary.
    B. Collect more favorable evaluations from staff who mainly handle routine cases. (Incorrect)
    These evaluations provide limited evidence about the cases behind the objection.
    C. Have the pilot's strongest advocates demonstrate the routine cases to another team. (Incorrect)
    Further demonstrations of routine success do not test the problem reported for unusual cases.
    D. Agree on an exception procedure with staff and test it on the unusual cases. (Correct)
    This investigates the reported conflict between standardization and professional discretion through a concrete design change.
    E. Ask an external expert to endorse the workflow before the next implementation meeting. (Incorrect)
    An endorsement does not test whether the current boundaries work for unusual cases.

    Review: Chapter 30 · Chapter 33 · Chapter 34. Back to question 10
