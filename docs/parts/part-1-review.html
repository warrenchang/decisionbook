Part I Review

These ten questions connect ideas from Chapters 1–7. Use the assumptions given in each question and choose the one best answer. Work through the complete set before checking the explanations.

Practice questions

Choose one answer for each of the 10 questions. Complete the set, then select Check answers to see your score and explanations. Each question is worth one point.

1. A club overlooks renting equipment, exaggerates the durability of buying, and never decides how much storage space matters. Which sequence names these three problems?

2. A family tradition makes a meal feel especially valuable to one person. Which conclusion distinguishes explanation from normative evaluation?

3. A job comparison page makes salary prominent and hides commuting time. Applicants focus on salary. Which redesign best addresses the resulting comparison problem?

4. A supplier recommendation reverses if an uncertain failure rate rises from 3% to 4%, both plausible values. The choice is costly to reverse. A quick, inexpensive check could substantially improve the estimate. What analysis is most valuable next?

5. A coach predicts an athlete will excel and gives that athlete extra practice. Later success is treated as proof that the coach merely recognized innate ability. What has been overlooked?

6. Customers give higher ratings to coffee sold at higher prices, but both the coffees and customers differ. Which follow-up best separates a price-induced expectation effect from a physical coffee difference?

7. A person recognizes a blurred face using context, then forecasts that the recognized person will accept an invitation. Later she explains why she had expected acceptance. Which distinction best organizes the sequence?

8. A job seeker initially compares salary alone. She then discovers that one employer will trade a little salary for flexible hours, which she values highly. What should she do next?

9. An expected-profit maximizer can take a certain €50 or launch a product earning €120 on success and €0 otherwise; success has probability 0.4. Perfect information costs €15 and arrives before the choice; both actions remain available. What policy maximizes expected net profit?

10. A randomized label study detects a change in reported enjoyment but no reliable performance difference. Participants later say the label helped concentration; concentration was not independently measured. Which claim best fits?

0 of 10 answered

Answers and explanations

  1. Question 1: E

    A. Valuation, prediction, implementation (Incorrect)
    The first is omission of an option; the third is weighting a consequence.
    B. Implementation, learning, prediction (Incorrect)
    The problems arise before implementation or outcome feedback.
    C. Learning, option construction, valuation (Incorrect)
    The first problem is not learning from an outcome.
    D. Prediction, valuation, option construction (Incorrect)
    This shifts each problem to the wrong stage.
    E. Option construction, prediction, valuation (Correct)
    The problems concern alternatives, consequences, and their importance respectively.

    Review: Chapter 1 · Chapter 2. Back to question 1

  2. Question 2: D

    A. An inherited tradition determines the ethically best meal for everyone. (Incorrect)
    Origins do not establish universal moral worth.
    B. A meal has value only through its market price. (Incorrect)
    Valuation also reflects identity, relationships, and experience.
    C. A culturally learned value cannot influence a real preference. (Incorrect)
    Learned meanings can shape subjective value.
    D. The tradition helps explain the value; it does not settle what everyone ought to choose. (Correct)
    Socially acquired meaning explains preferences without supplying a universal prescription.
    E. A utility representation must ignore the meal's social meaning. (Incorrect)
    Preference ordering can reflect consequences with social meaning.

    Review: Chapter 1 · Chapter 6. Back to question 2

  3. Question 3: D

    A. Explain the selected job only after the contract is signed. (Incorrect)
    Later justification does not repair the original comparison.
    B. Ask applicants to ignore their personal travel preferences. (Incorrect)
    Preferences over commuting remain relevant.
    C. Increase the prominence of the highest salary further. (Incorrect)
    This intensifies the original attentional imbalance.
    D. Show salary and commuting time together on clearly labeled scales. (Correct)
    Making both consequences interpretable helps them enter valuation.
    E. Remove salary so that commuting becomes the only visible attribute. (Incorrect)
    That replaces one incomplete comparison with another.

    Review: Chapter 3 · Chapter 6. Back to question 3

  4. Question 4: C

    A. Treat the central estimate as certain because it fits the current model. (Incorrect)
    A plausible model prediction still carries uncertainty.
    B. Refine an unrelated estimate that leaves the recommendation unchanged. (Incorrect)
    Precision in an inconsequential input has less decision value.
    C. Improve the consequential failure-rate estimate and compare outcomes across its plausible range. (Correct)
    The consequential sensitivity and inexpensive informative check make this uncertainty worth investigating.
    D. Average the suppliers' names to remove personal preference. (Incorrect)
    The issue is uncertainty about consequences, not naming.
    E. Stop analysis because a numerical recommendation already exists. (Incorrect)
    A fragile consequential recommendation warrants a proportionate check.

    Review: Chapter 2 · Chapter 4. Back to question 4

  5. Question 5: A

    A. The prediction may have altered the athlete's learning environment. (Correct)
    Treatment induced by a forecast can help cause the outcome.
    B. The prediction must have been consciously insincere. (Incorrect)
    The coach can sincerely believe the forecast.
    C. A correct forecast rules out effects of the coach's actions. (Incorrect)
    Predictive success and causal influence can coexist.
    D. Practice cannot be measured as part of a causal pathway. (Incorrect)
    Practice is precisely a potentially measurable mediator.
    E. The outcome supplies a direct measure of innate ability alone. (Incorrect)
    Changed opportunities prevent that simple interpretation.

    Review: Chapter 4 · Chapter 5. Back to question 5

  6. Question 6: C

    A. Compare premium beans with cheaper beans at their usual prices. (Incorrect)
    Physical quality and price then vary together.
    B. Replace tasting ratings with the retailer's preferred price. (Incorrect)
    A preferred price does not measure the experience under study.
    C. Randomly assign price labels to tasters served the same coffee. (Correct)
    Identical coffee and random labels isolate the label manipulation.
    D. Change both the price and serving temperature. (Incorrect)
    Temperature becomes an additional explanation.
    E. Ask only participants who already prefer expensive coffee. (Incorrect)
    Selection limits the comparison and does not isolate the manipulation.

    Review: Chapter 4 · Chapter 6. Back to question 6

  7. Question 7: B

    A. An action, an outcome, then a controlled experiment (Incorrect)
    No controlled manipulation is described.
    B. Perceptual inference, consequence forecast, then retrospective explanation (Correct)
    The stages answer what is present, what will happen, and why a prior judgment was made.
    C. A consequence forecast followed by two perceptual inferences (Incorrect)
    Recognition comes first, and the final account concerns a prior judgment.
    D. Three direct recordings of external reality (Incorrect)
    Inference, forecasting, and explanation all involve interpretation.
    E. Three judgments of the invitation's monetary value (Incorrect)
    None is necessarily a valuation in money.

    Review: Chapter 4 · Chapter 7. Back to question 7

  8. Question 8: D

    A. Choose the higher salary because only money is comparable. (Incorrect)
    Nonmonetary consequences can be valued and compared.
    B. Assume flexibility has the same value for every applicant. (Incorrect)
    The chapter emphasizes differences in subjective value.
    C. Infer that the employer's first offer was irrational. (Incorrect)
    Negotiability does not establish irrationality.
    D. Evaluate the newly feasible salary–hours package against alternatives. (Correct)
    Option construction and valuation should incorporate the negotiable trade-off.
    E. Treat the original salary as a cost already paid. (Incorrect)
    An offer is not an incurred sunk cost.

    Review: Chapter 2 · Chapter 6. Back to question 8

  9. Question 9: A

    A. Buy information; launch only on success, otherwise take €50. (Correct)
    Perfect information yields 0.4×120 + 0.6×50 − 15 = €63, above €50.
    B. Decline information and launch the product. (Incorrect)
    Its expected profit is €48, below the certain €50.
    C. Buy information and launch regardless of its result. (Incorrect)
    This earns €48 − €15 = €33 in expectation.
    D. Buy information and take €50 regardless of its result. (Incorrect)
    This earns €35 and wastes the information.
    E. Decline information and take €50. (Incorrect)
    This misses the available €63 expected net payoff.

    Review: Chapter 2 · Appendix A: decision analysis. Back to question 9

  10. Question 10: E

    A. The label had no effect on any unmeasured experience. (Incorrect)
    Lack of measurement cannot establish no effect.
    B. The label changed performance despite the reported comparison. (Incorrect)
    The study did not reliably detect that difference.
    C. The label changed concentration, which mediated the rating effect. (Incorrect)
    The proposed mediator was neither independently measured nor manipulated.
    D. The retrospective account identifies the participants' original causal process. (Incorrect)
    A sincere account can be a post hoc explanation.
    E. The label changed reported enjoyment; the concentration mechanism remains untested. (Correct)
    The outcome comparison supports the rating effect, not the retrospective causal account.

    Review: Chapter 3 · Chapter 5 · Chapter 7. Back to question 10
