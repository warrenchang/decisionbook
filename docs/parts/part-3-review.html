Part III Review

These ten questions connect ideas from Chapters 16–22. Use the assumptions given in each question and choose the one best answer. Work through the complete set before checking the explanations.

Practice questions

Choose one answer for each of the 10 questions. Complete the set, then select Check answers to see your score and explanations. Each question is worth one point.

1. A small firm has never suffered a fire, but credible evidence gives a 1% annual risk of a €200,000 loss that would bankrupt it. Insurance costs more than the €2,000 expected loss. What should guide the decision?

2. An age-progressed self-image increases hypothetical retirement allocations. Which follow-up most directly tests whether the effect extends to saving behavior?

3. A person rejects a fair gamble over final wealth but has made no explicit gain–loss comparison. Which evidence would most specifically help distinguish reference-dependent valuation from stable concave utility over final wealth?

4. A buyer chooses a smaller immediate rebate over a larger delayed one. Which comparison best separates impatience from concerns about receiving payment?

5. A saver chooses an account earmarked for saving that also delays withdrawals. Which pair of mechanisms could support the saving goal?

6. A traveler selects a holiday for its spectacular highlights but dislikes most days of the trip. Which forecasting method best addresses that mismatch next time?

7. Maya automatically opens a service when she sees its icon. Leo consciously keeps using it because he paid a nonrefundable annual fee, despite a better free alternative and no switching costs. Which diagnosis fits each stated reason?

8. A person judges a service from one vivid final failure, despite records of many ordinary successful uses. Which review would best distinguish recent memory from the service's overall performance?

9. With linear utility and an annual discount factor of 0.9, compare a certain €70 today with an 80% chance of €100 in one year and €0 otherwise. Ignore liquidity and payment risks beyond those stated. Which is preferred?

10. An investment independently multiplies wealth by 1.5 or 0.6 with equal probability each period. A sample contains one of each outcome. Which statement correctly compares expected one-period return with the realized two-period path?

0 of 10 answered

Answers and explanations

  1. Question 1: D

    A. Reject insurance solely because the premium exceeds expected loss. (Incorrect)
    Expected money alone omits the firm's capacity to survive the loss.
    B. Buy insurance at any premium because the loss is large. (Incorrect)
    Price and alternatives still matter.
    C. Treat the failure-free history as a zero-risk estimate. (Incorrect)
    Rare events can be absent from limited experience.
    D. Evaluate the premium alongside the firm's capacity to bear the uninsured loss. (Correct)
    A favorable average and a failure-free history do not remove an unaffordable tail exposure.
    E. Assume a fire is due because none has occurred recently. (Incorrect)
    Absence of recent events does not create a balancing requirement.

    Review: Chapter 16 · Chapter 18. Back to question 1

  2. Question 2: C

    A. Ask whether viewing the image makes retirement saving seem more important. (Incorrect)
    A changed attitude is not an observed contribution.
    B. Compare balances of people who volunteer to view the image with those who decline. (Incorrect)
    Self-selection prevents isolating the image's effect on saving.
    C. Randomly assign the image intervention and compare subsequent account contributions. (Correct)
    This tests the intervention against an actual saving outcome.
    D. Repeat the hypothetical allocation with a different windfall amount. (Incorrect)
    Another hypothetical allocation does not establish behavioral transfer.
    E. Compare recognition accuracy for current and age-progressed photographs. (Incorrect)
    Recognition does not measure saving behavior.

    Review: Chapter 20 · Chapter 22. Back to question 2

  3. Question 3: D

    A. Ask whether the person has ever bought insurance. (Incorrect)
    Insurance can reflect several motives and constraints.
    B. Measure only whether the gamble has positive expected value. (Incorrect)
    Expected value alone does not identify the value function.
    C. Record the person's dislike of this one gamble again. (Incorrect)
    One risky choice is compatible with several mechanisms.
    D. Vary the reference point while holding final-wealth lotteries fixed. (Correct)
    Stable utility over final wealth predicts invariance; reference dependence can change rankings. This test alone does not identify loss aversion.
    E. Assume any aversion to risk is a measure of loss aversion. (Incorrect)
    Risk aversion and loss aversion are distinct.

    Review: Chapter 16 · Chapter 17. Back to question 3

  4. Question 4: C

    A. Ask the buyer to imagine spending the delayed payment. (Incorrect)
    Imagery changes how the future is represented without controlling payment risk.
    B. Increase the delayed amount while leaving its reliability unchanged. (Incorrect)
    This changes the reward trade-off while retaining the reliability difference.
    C. Offer both payment dates with the same credible payment guarantee. (Correct)
    Equalizing payment reliability reduces its confounding with the delay.
    D. Present the immediate rebate after describing the delayed rebate. (Incorrect)
    Presentation order does not equalize payment reliability.
    E. Compare the choice with another buyer's choice at a different seller. (Incorrect)
    Changing buyers and sellers introduces additional differences.

    Review: Chapter 18 · Chapter 20. Back to question 4

  5. Question 5: B

    A. Probability weighting and insurance (Incorrect)
    No probability transformation or transfer of risk is described.
    B. Mental accounting and commitment (Correct)
    The label separates the money mentally; the delay restricts immediate access.
    C. Habituation and social proof (Incorrect)
    The account does not rely on repeated exposure or evidence of others' choices.
    D. Exponential discounting and diversification (Incorrect)
    A labeled access restriction does not specify either a discount function or a portfolio mix.
    E. Loss aversion and numerical anchoring (Incorrect)
    The design specifies neither a framed loss nor a starting numerical estimate.

    Review: Chapter 19 · Chapter 20. Back to question 5

  6. Question 6: A

    A. Imagine a typical day in each itinerary, including travel and waiting. (Correct)
    This brings ordinary experienced time into a comparison dominated by highlights.
    B. Predict the final overall rating without considering the itinerary's daily schedule. (Incorrect)
    A global forecast can leave the same neglected periods unexamined.
    C. Compare the single most enjoyable activity available at each destination. (Incorrect)
    Focusing on the peak repeats the original omission.
    D. Estimate how impressive each itinerary would sound in a story afterward. (Incorrect)
    This emphasizes anticipated memory and social presentation rather than daily experience.
    E. Rank destinations by the intensity of excitement when viewing their advertisements. (Incorrect)
    Immediate excitement need not predict enjoyment throughout the trip.

    Review: Chapter 20 · Chapter 22. Back to question 6

  7. Question 7: D

    A. Present bias for both (Incorrect)
    No immediate-versus-delayed reward comparison is specified.
    B. Sunk-cost reasoning for Maya; cue-linked habit for Leo (Incorrect)
    This reverses the mechanisms supplied in the stem.
    C. Rational updating from service quality for both (Incorrect)
    The stated reasons are cues and past payment, not new quality evidence.
    D. Cue-linked habit for Maya; sunk-cost reasoning for Leo (Correct)
    Their behavior looks similar but the described mechanisms differ.
    E. Ambiguity aversion for both (Incorrect)
    Unknown probabilities are not central to either account.

    Review: Chapter 19 · Chapter 21. Back to question 7

  8. Question 8: B

    A. Count how often the failure story is retold. (Incorrect)
    Retelling measures salience, not the whole performance record.
    B. Compare the full dated outcome record with the retrospective rating. (Correct)
    This makes the sampled history and later summary separately observable.
    C. Ask for the same global rating several more times. (Incorrect)
    Repeated summaries do not recover the outcome distribution.
    D. Delete the final failure because it is unpleasant. (Incorrect)
    A full review should retain relevant failures.
    E. Use the final failure as the only representative observation. (Incorrect)
    This preserves the possible recency distortion.

    Review: Chapter 18 · Chapter 22. Back to question 8

  9. Question 9: A

    A. The delayed lottery; its discounted expected value is €72. (Correct)
    0.9×(0.8×100) = 72, above 70.
    B. The delayed lottery; its discounted expected value is €90. (Incorrect)
    This ignores the 20% chance of no payment.
    C. The options tie; discounting cancels the probability. (Incorrect)
    Probability and discounting both enter multiplicatively.
    D. The certain payment; the lottery is worth €8 today. (Incorrect)
    This uses the 10% discount rate as the weight instead of the 0.9 discount factor.
    E. The certain payment; the lottery is worth €64 today. (Incorrect)
    This applies the 0.8 probability twice and omits discounting.

    Review: Chapter 16 · Chapter 20. Back to question 9

  10. Question 10: B

    A. Expected one-period return is +5%; the sampled path gains 5%. (Incorrect)
    The realized path is determined by multiplication, not the one-period expectation.
    B. Expected one-period return is +5%; the sampled path loses 10%. (Correct)
    The expected multiplier is 1.05; the sampled product is 1.5×0.6 = 0.9.
    C. Expected one-period return is −10%; the sampled path loses 10%. (Incorrect)
    The path loss is correct, but the expected one-period return is positive.
    D. Expected one-period return is +10%; the sampled path gains 10%. (Incorrect)
    Both calculations confuse arithmetic and multiplicative changes.
    E. Expected one-period return is 0%; the sampled path breaks even. (Incorrect)
    The gains and losses are neither equal nor multiplicatively offsetting.

    Review: Chapter 16 · Chapter 18. Back to question 10
