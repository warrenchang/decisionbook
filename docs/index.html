Decision in the Making

The Behavioral Science of Choice, Influence, and Agreement

Visible choices are only the endpoint. This book follows decisions in the making—from attention, interpretation, prediction, and valuation to influence, communication, negotiation, and the design of environments that make better judgment and action possible.
Author

Huanren Warren Zhang

This Version

September 14, 2026

Preface

Five hands rise for one candidate, two for another. A decision is made.

Each member of the hiring committee can explain their vote. They cite the candidates’ qualifications, their interviews, and the needs of the organization. These considerations should guide the choice: what the job requires and which candidate is most likely to meet those requirements.

But their judgments may also reflect influences they have not considered. The order of the interviews may affect how candidates are compared, while the first opinion voiced may guide the discussion. Even how tired or anxious a member feels may influence their judgment without appearing among the reasons they give for their vote. A recent conflict with a colleague, or a successful collaboration many years ago, may shape which qualities they notice and how much weight they give them.

Would the committee have chosen the same person if the interview order had been reversed, or if someone else had spoken first? Explaining why a candidate deserves the job is different from explaining how the committee came to prefer that candidate. To understand the vote, we need to look upstream, at how members interpreted the evidence, formed expectations, and influenced one another before raising their hands.

This is the central question of the book: what shapes a choice before it is made, and how can we use that understanding to choose better?

Follow the decision upstream

Consider what might lie behind the divided vote. Two members could agree that one candidate is more creative and the other more reliable, yet disagree about which quality matters more. Further evidence could help resolve uncertainty about the candidates’ abilities, but it would not by itself settle a difference in priorities. That requires a conversation about what the team needs.

The available options deserve attention too. A vote between two candidates can obscure a third possibility: reopening the search if neither applicant meets the team’s needs.

The decision map in Figure 1 brings these questions together. It follows how we notice and interpret information, construct options, anticipate consequences, and judge how much those consequences matter. Once we choose and commit, the resulting outcomes give us an opportunity to learn.

These activities can overlap or repeat. Considering another option may reveal information we need, while experience can change what attracts our attention next time. The map organizes the questions we need to ask; it does not prescribe a fixed sequence of thought.

The decision map makes alternatives, beliefs, and priorities explicit so that we can evaluate a choice. An important gap in evidence calls for investigation; conflicting priorities call for discussion; an inadequate set of options calls for imagination. The map helps us identify the difficulty and choose a response that addresses it.

The early chapters examine how judgments form and how we can check them. A quick impression may draw on useful experience or give too much weight to a vivid story. Taking more time can help us examine the judgment, but it can also help us defend our first answer. The question is what supports that answer and what could change it.

Decide together

When people decide together, the process must also bring their knowledge into the conversation. A hiring committee can know more than any one of its members, yet fail to use what it knows.

After an influential member endorses a candidate, others may follow because they assume that person has better information. Later endorsements may echo the first judgment without adding independent evidence. Someone with a reservation may stay silent because challenging the emerging agreement feels uncomfortable or disloyal. The group can become more confident while important doubts remain unspoken.

Connection matters because people need to be able to share those doubts. Feeling heard and respected can make it easier to explain a concern, correct a misunderstanding, or consider another perspective. Relationships that allow honest disagreement can therefore support clearer communication, fuller information sharing, and more constructive conflict resolution.

That openness can help the committee understand its disagreement about reliability and creativity. One member may be trying to protect an overloaded team; another may worry that meeting today’s demands leaves no time to develop tomorrow’s ideas. Making these concerns explicit creates room to explore whether redistributing responsibilities could provide reliable coverage while protecting time for new work. If this is impractical, the committee still needs a fair way to weigh those competing needs and decide.

The committee’s decision is only part of the outcome, because the candidate must also decide whether to accept the offer. Each side needs to understand what the other wants and what they can do if no agreement is reached. A candidate may value flexible working hours, while the organization needs reliable coverage at particular times. Agreeing on core hours with flexibility around them could serve both sides better. Listening and sharing priorities can reveal these opportunities to create value. Negotiation then needs to turn them into clear commitments that both parties can fulfill.

These connections explain the book’s movement through choice, influence, and agreement. How we judge affects how we persuade; how we communicate affects what we can learn from one another; and what we learn can change the decisions and agreements available to us.

Ask what makes the choice better

Even a welcome agreement can rest on mistaken expectations about life afterward. The committee may expect a successful appointment; the candidate may anticipate meaningful work, financial security, or more time for family. An attractive offer can promise these things while revealing little about an ordinary working day.

The book also follows choices into the lives that result from them. How do we weigh a possible gain against the risk of loss, or a benefit years away against a demand we face today? Why can a sincere plan give way to a familiar habit?

We will also examine how accurately people anticipate what they will enjoy, how experience changes what they value, and how decisions affect relationships and meaning. Better decisions require us to consider whose life is meant to improve and in what way. When we shape choices for others, that responsibility includes asking whether they can understand the arrangement, question it, and choose differently.

Put understanding to work

The committee can use this understanding to change its next search: agreeing on criteria before meeting candidates, recording individual judgments before discussion, and giving reservations a clear place in the conversation. Each step addresses a different problem and makes the decision easier to examine.

The final part of the book develops this practical approach. It considers how to support useful routines, make choices easier to understand, and improve how organizations gather and evaluate evidence. A proposed change needs a clear purpose, conditions that make it workable, and a way to assess whether it helps.

Assessing a decision requires care, because even the best choice given the information available can have a poor outcome. A route selected after checking traffic can still lead to a delay if an accident occurs after departure. A stock investment can lose value when geopolitical tensions escalate, and a business strategy can lead to losses when an unexpected technological development disrupts the market. To learn from the outcome, we need to consider what information was available when we chose, whether we used it well, and what we have reason to change next time. Recording our expectations before the result gives us something more reliable to consult than hindsight alone.

The same care applies to the evidence behind the book’s recommendations. We will examine what studies actually compared, what they found, and how later research changes the conclusions we can draw. Our recommendations will be based on evidence, with attention to the settings in which they have been tested. A memorable finding can suggest something worth trying without guaranteeing that it will work in every situation.

These questions also guide our use of data and AI. A tool may organize information or improve a prediction, while people still need to decide what counts as success and how the prediction should guide action. The final chapter examines how these tools can contribute to decisions we can explain, evaluate, and improve.

This book grew from teaching behavioral economics and decision making, persuasion, and negotiation. Students wanted to understand why sensible recommendations could fail, well-intended conversations could create distance, and useful agreements could remain out of reach. They also wanted to know what they could do differently next time. The activities throughout the book serve that purpose: you will make predictions, examine cases, compare explanations, and try changes whose consequences you can observe.

Think of a decision you are about to make. Before committing, consider how you came to favor one option and whether your reasons deserve the weight you have given them. There may still be time to question an assumption, hear another perspective, or discover a better possibility.

We should pay attention to our decisions while they are still in the making.
