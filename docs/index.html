Decision in the Making

The Behavioral Science of Choice, Influence, and Agreement

Visible choices are only the endpoint. This book follows decisions in the making—from attention, interpretation, prediction, and valuation to influence, communication, negotiation, and the design of environments that make better judgment and action possible.

Preface: A Decision Is Already in the Making

At 3:17 p.m., the chair of a hiring committee asks for a vote. Five hands rise for one candidate, two for another. The decision appears to happen at that moment.

The committee has reasons. Its members have read the applications, conducted interviews, and discussed what the organization needs. Each can explain why one candidate seems stronger. The result is recorded, an offer is made, and the search is closed.

To understand that vote, however, we would need to arrive much earlier. We would need to hear how the position was defined, see which applications were read first, and notice when an impressive candidate became the standard against which the others were judged. We would need to ask why one interview story kept returning to the discussion while a quieter record of achievement received little attention. When a senior member praised a candidate, did anyone change their mind—or decide to keep a doubt to themselves?

Even a familiar word could matter. The committee agreed that it wanted the best fit, but one member meant competence, another meant shared values, and another meant someone easy to work with. An apparently shared criterion concealed different judgments.

This imagined committee gives us the central question of the book: what has shaped a choice before the moment when someone appears to make it?

Follow the decision upstream

A visible choice is one point in a longer process. Something attracts attention and is given meaning. Possible actions come to mind, their consequences are anticipated, and those consequences are judged worth pursuing or avoiding. A choice then has to be carried out. What follows may teach us something useful, although a result can also strengthen a mistaken explanation.

The map below names these functions so that we can examine them separately. Consider the committee again. Predicting which candidate will perform reliably is different from deciding how much reliability matters relative to creativity. Constructing an additional option—reopening the search, for example—is different from comparing the two candidates already under discussion.

These functions rarely wait politely for one another. Predicting the consequences of an option may reveal that it is infeasible, suggest a better version, or prompt us to imagine another option. What we value affects what we notice, and what we expect can affect how we interpret an ambiguous answer. A decision becomes easier to understand when we can trace those connections.

It also becomes easier to improve. A rational benchmark helps us make the alternatives, beliefs, and values explicit. It gives us a way to ask whether a disagreement concerns what will happen, what matters, or what can be done. The benchmark provides a standard for evaluating a choice; explaining how the choice actually forms requires evidence about human behavior.

From labels to causes

Imagine that the committee’s first favorite eventually wins. We might call the result an anchoring effect, a preference for familiarity, or conformity to the chair. None of those labels, by itself, tells us what happened. The candidate might also have been the strongest applicant.

To distinguish the explanations, we would need observations that bear on them. What did members think before they discussed the candidates? Which evidence changed their judgments? Did the criteria shift to favor the early preference? These questions connect a possible mechanism to something we could examine.

The chapters use this approach throughout. Intuition can make effective use of experience, and habits can make useful actions easier to perform. A shortcut becomes troublesome when the cue it relies on is a poor guide in the current setting. A vivid success story, for example, may draw attention away from the outcomes of many comparable cases. The useful question is when to trust the shortcut and how to check it.

The same discipline applies after a decision. Suppose the new hire performs exceptionally well. That is welcome news, but it does not prove that every step of the selection process was sound. An unsuccessful hire would not establish that every step was mistaken either. Learning requires a record of what was believed before the outcome became known, when uncertainty was still visible.

What is a good decision ultimately for?

A job offer can look attractive during recruitment and feel quite different on an ordinary Tuesday. A purchase can promise lasting pleasure and soon fade into the background. A difficult commitment may be unpleasant at first while contributing to something a person deeply values.

The book therefore follows choices into the lives that result from them. We will distinguish anticipated feelings from lived experience, and both from a person’s judgment that life is going well. Income, health, relationships, autonomy, and meaning can matter in different ways. Improving one does not automatically settle a conflict among them.

Asking what makes a decision better is part of the analysis. A design that increases purchases may serve the seller while making buyers worse off. A successful negotiation may still produce an agreement that is difficult to carry out. Before evaluating a result, we need to know whose objective it serves and which consequences count.

When other minds enter

The hiring committee is already a social setting, and few consequential choices remain within one mind for long. Another person’s action can change what is possible for us; their judgment can become evidence for ours. Cooperation, competition, norms, and market prices all depend on these connections.

The book follows an expanding journey: choice, influence, and agreement. We begin with individual judgment, examine what changes when choices depend on other people, and then turn to deliberate persuasion, communication, and negotiation.

A recommendation reaches an audience that already has beliefs and concerns. To make a persuasive case, we need to understand what listeners are trying to judge and what evidence could reasonably change their minds. A story can help them imagine a consequence, but its vividness does not establish that the consequence is likely.

Conversation lets the audience answer back. When the committee’s members use the word fit differently, a question may do more than another argument. They can explain, check what they have understood, and revise the shared account. Negotiation adds a joint choice: parties with different interests and alternatives seek an arrangement they can accept and implement.

The environment gets a vote

Return to the committee room. The chair could ask everyone to record a judgment before discussion. The group could agree on criteria before meeting candidates, compare the same evidence for each person, and preserve its forecasts for later review. Those changes would alter the conditions under which the next decision forms.

The final part of the book examines this practical work. We can redesign a repeated behavior, make a choice easier to understand and carry out, or change how an organization gathers and evaluates evidence. Each change needs a clear purpose and an observable test. A process that sounds sensible may still fail in practice.

The final chapter returns to the book’s opening distinction between prediction, valuation, and choice, now with AI available to assist the work. Cheaper prediction creates opportunities whose value depends on the rest of the process: what people notice, what they care about, how they compare evidence, and whether they can carry out and learn from a choice. This is why the book ends with data-driven decision-making. It brings the behavioral lessons together in deciding what to delegate to a tool, what to test, and which judgments people must own.

That ambition carries responsibility. The same knowledge can help people pursue their goals or make them easier to exploit. A default may remove an obstacle to a considered intention, while an obscure cancellation process may prevent someone from acting on it. We will keep asking whose goal a design serves, who bears its costs, and whether affected people can understand, question, or leave it.

Evidence, ethics, and responsibility

Behavioral science offers findings with different levels of support. A classroom demonstration can reveal a possibility without telling us how common it is. A study can identify a difference without settling its mechanism. A result that repeats in one setting may change in another.

The chapters will name those boundaries where they matter to the explanation. We will separate examples from evidence, examine competing accounts, and consider what later research has changed. The early evidence guide explains how to read these distinctions; the research appendices develop them for readers who want to design or evaluate studies.

The purpose is to make confidence answerable to evidence. Scientific caution should help us decide what follows from a finding and what remains to be learned.

Notice. Test. Ask. Design. Learn.

This book grew from teaching behavioral economics and decision, persuasion, and negotiation. Students wanted to understand why a sensible recommendation could fail, why a well-intended conversation could create distance, and why people could overlook a useful agreement. They also wanted something they could do differently next time.

You will therefore be invited to make predictions, examine cases, compare explanations, and try small changes. Some exercises produce a forecast; others produce a better question, a conversation plan, or a record that can be checked later. The aim is to leave each chapter with a distinction you can use.

Think again of the moment before the committee votes. The outcome is still uncertain, but there is time to ask what fit means, hear a quiet reservation, or examine an alternative. Better judgment often begins in such moments, while the path can still change.

The decision is already in the making.
