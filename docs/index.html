The Behavioral Science of Choice, Influence, and Agreement

Visible choices are only the endpoint. This book follows decisions in the making—from attention, interpretation, prediction, and valuation to influence, communication, negotiation, and the design of environments that make better judgment and action possible.

Preface: A Decision Is Already in the Making

Five hands rise for one candidate, two for another. A decision is made.

Each member of the hiring committee can explain their vote. They cite the candidates’ qualifications, their interviews, and the needs of the organization. These are the considerations that should guide the choice: what the job requires and which candidate is most likely to meet those requirements.

But their judgments may also reflect influences they have not considered. The order of the interviews may affect how candidates are compared, while the first opinion voiced may guide the discussion. Even how tired or anxious a member feels may influence their judgment without appearing among the reasons they give for their vote. A recent conflict with a colleague, or a successful collaboration many years ago, may also shape which qualities a member notices and how much weight they give them.

Would the committee have chosen the same person if the interview order had been reversed, or if someone else had spoken first? Its members might offer convincing reasons without recognizing how those circumstances helped shape their choice. Explaining why a candidate deserves the job is different from explaining how the committee came to prefer that candidate.

To understand the vote, we therefore need to look upstream, at how members interpreted the evidence, formed expectations, and arrived at a shared decision. Understanding this process also gives us places to intervene while the choice can still change.

This is the central question of the book: what shapes a choice before it is made, and how can we use that understanding to choose better?

Follow the decision upstream

Two committee members might agree that one candidate is more creative and the other more reliable, yet vote differently because they disagree about which quality matters more. Further evidence about the candidates could help resolve uncertainty about their abilities. Resolving a disagreement about priorities requires a different conversation.

The committee must also consider which possibilities remain open. A vote between two candidates can obscure a third option: reopening the search. Before comparing the applicants, members need to consider whether hiring either one is their best available course of action.

The decision map below helps us keep these questions distinct while following their connections. We notice and interpret information, consider possible actions, anticipate their consequences, and judge how much those consequences matter. We then choose and act, encountering results from which we may learn.

These activities can overlap. Considering another option may reveal information we need to gather, while an earlier experience may influence what attracts our attention. Learning from an outcome can change how we approach these tasks next time.

The decision map makes alternatives, beliefs, and priorities explicit so that we can evaluate a choice. It also helps us ask where the process needs attention and what we could change.

Decide together

A hiring committee can bring together knowledge that no single member possesses. Whether it benefits from that knowledge depends on how people share information, examine differences, and reach a decision.

After an influential member endorses a candidate, others may follow because they assume that person knows something they do not. Later endorsements may then echo the first judgment without adding independent evidence. Meanwhile, someone with a reservation may stay silent because challenging the emerging agreement feels uncomfortable or disloyal. The group can become more confident even as relevant information disappears from the discussion.

A useful joint decision process must make room for independent views and examine what supports them. It must also help people work through disagreements about what they want. A member emphasizing reliability may be protecting an overloaded team, while one favoring creativity may be concerned about its future direction.

Once these concerns are clear, the committee can explore whether sharing responsibilities differently would help the team handle its current workload reliably while developing new ideas for the future. If that arrangement is impractical, members need to agree on how much weight to give reliability and creativity, then assess the candidates using those shared priorities.

The committee can offer a job, but the candidate decides whether to accept it. Negotiation gives both sides an opportunity to understand each other’s priorities and find arrangements that serve them better. A candidate may value flexible working hours, while the organization needs reliable coverage at particular times. Agreeing on core hours with flexibility around them could meet both needs. Negotiation can therefore create value by discovering possibilities that improve the agreement for both sides. The parties then need to turn those possibilities into clear commitments they can fulfill.

This is why the book moves through choice, influence, and agreement. We examine how people judge for themselves, how they affect one another’s choices, and how communication and negotiation can help them resolve conflict and act together.

Ask what makes the choice better

Reaching agreement still leaves an important question: what would make this a good decision for the people involved?

The committee may define success as appointing someone who performs well and stays. The candidate may be seeking meaningful work, financial security, or time for family. An attractive offer can promise these things while revealing little about what an ordinary working day will feel like.

The book also follows choices into the lives that result from them. We will ask how accurately people anticipate what they will enjoy, how experience changes what they value, and how they weigh competing priorities. When someone designs a choice for others, we will also ask whose goals it serves and whether those affected can understand and influence it.

Improve the process and learn from the outcome

Return to the committee preparing for its next search. Members may sincerely intend to be more careful, but improvement requires them to decide what they will do differently and why.

They could agree on criteria before meeting candidates, record individual judgments before discussion, and give reservations a clear place in the conversation. These records would help them examine how preferences changed and which evidence mattered. Preserving expectations about the eventual appointment would also create a basis for learning afterward.

The same care is needed in everyday decisions. Choosing a route after checking traffic can still lead to a delay if an accident occurs after departure. Arriving late does not, by itself, show that the choice was poor. To learn from the journey, we need to consider what information was available when we chose, whether we used it well, and what we have reason to change next time.

The final part of the book develops this practical approach. It considers how to support useful routines, make choices easier to understand, and improve how organizations gather and evaluate evidence. Each proposed change needs a clear purpose and a way to assess whether it helps.

The same questions apply when data and AI assist a decision. A tool may organize information or predict an outcome, while people still need to decide what counts as success and how the prediction should guide action. The final chapter brings these responsibilities together.

Throughout the book, our recommendations will remain answerable to evidence. We will examine what studies actually compared, what they found, and how later research changes the conclusions we can draw. A memorable finding can suggest something worth trying without guaranteeing that it will work in every setting.

This book grew from teaching behavioral economics and decision making, persuasion, and negotiation. Students wanted to understand why sensible recommendations could fail, well-intended conversations could create distance, and useful agreements could remain out of reach. They also wanted to know what they could do differently next time.

You will therefore be invited to make predictions, examine cases, compare explanations, and try changes whose consequences you can observe. The aim is to connect understanding with action, and action with learning.

Think of a decision you are about to make. Before committing, consider how you came to favor one option and whether your reasons deserve the weight you have given them. There may still be time to question an assumption, hear another perspective, or discover a better possibility.

We should pay attention to our decisions while they are still in the making.
