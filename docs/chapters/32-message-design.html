32  Message Design

Building an Evidence-Aligned Message

“The first principle is that you must not fool yourself—and you are the easiest person to fool.”

— Richard P. Feynman, “Cargo Cult Science”, Caltech commencement address, 1974

A restaurant owner receives a pitch for a forecasting assistant. The first slide lists algorithms; the second celebrates the development team. By the third, the owner still cannot tell whether the tool will help decide how much food to prepare tomorrow.

Imagine rebuilding the pitch around that decision. Too much preparation wastes ingredients; too little loses sales. The proposed tool might help, but the owner needs to see how it uses the restaurant’s records, what evidence supports its forecasts, and what trying it would cost.

We will use this fictional pitch to build a message that earns attention and gives the audience enough information to decide. A story is optional; a defensible claim and a clear request are essential.

NoteCore Idea

Start with the audience’s decision, explain the problem your proposal addresses, and show what supports the proposed response. Then make the request concrete enough to evaluate. The strength of the message should come from the fit between its claim and evidence, including what remains uncertain.

Learning goals

  • Explain what experiments on clarity and uncertainty reveal about credibility.
  • Use a drafting aid to organize a message without mistaking its structure for evidence.
  • Match the strength of each claim to appropriate evidence, comparison, and uncertainty.
  • Produce and audit a message that remains accurate, dignified, and genuinely rejectable.

What earns credibility?

The restaurant owner needs an understandable account of what the tool can do. Does technical language make that account sound more expert? Oppenheimer (2006) tested a version of this assumption by manipulating the complexity of written texts. Across three experiments, needless complexity lowered readers’ judgments of the author’s intelligence. A further experiment changed the font: difficult-to-read text also lowered those judgments. Readers were partly responding to ease of processing, not simply to the quality of the underlying ideas. Clear writing can help a proposal receive a hearing, but the ease of reading it does not establish its truth.

A second worry is that acknowledging uncertainty will destroy credibility. Van der Bles and colleagues (2020) tested messages that reported numerical estimates alone, added a numerical range, or described uncertainty verbally. Their five experiments involved 5,780 participants, including a field experiment on the BBC News website. Readers noticed the uncertainty when it was disclosed, yet the average loss of trust was small and occurred mainly with verbal uncertainty. Numerical ranges generally carried little trust penalty. These were responses to particular reports, not proof that every admission of uncertainty preserves a relationship over time.

Together, the studies challenge two tempting shortcuts: complexity does not reliably signal expertise, and precision about uncertainty need not undermine trust. For the owner, explain the forecast in familiar units, show how its performance was checked, and distinguish an estimated improvement from a guaranteed result. The rest of this chapter turns those evidential requirements into a draft.

Ask the owner how preparation decisions are currently made and where mistakes become costly. An experienced owner may already forecast informally using bookings, weather, and local events. Beginning with “Restaurants need data-driven decisions” would overlook that knowledge and give the owner a reason to distrust the rest of the pitch.

Find out what makes trying the tool unattractive. The owner may doubt its accuracy, lack clean records, or worry that staff will treat recommendations as orders. Identity can also make a popular choice unattractive: Berger and Heath (2007) found more divergence from others in product domains that participants regarded as identity-signaling. For this owner, adoption might imply surrendering a valued skill. Ask whether that concern is present before treating it as the explanation. Once the concern is clear, choose a response that addresses it. A demonstration can address usability; a pilot may test forecasts; explicit override rules address control. If the product cannot meet an important constraint, the proposal needs work before the message does.

If a story would help, follow a person facing the decision. Picture the owner checking yesterday’s waste against today’s bookings, knowing that a cautious preparation plan could leave popular dishes unavailable. This scene gives the audience a goal and a trade-off. It does not need an invented crisis or a heroic founder.

The obstacle should make the proposed response worth considering. Perhaps the owner has sales records but cannot readily combine them into a daily forecast. That problem suggests a role for the tool. If the real problem is unreliable deliveries, better demand forecasts may help little.

Do not assume the audience’s current belief is a “misbelief” simply because it conflicts with the proposal. The pitch should reveal a limitation that can be investigated, with room for the investigation to vindicate the current practice.

A useful turning point changes what the audience can see. Comparing a forecast with the owner’s existing estimate may reveal a promising improvement, or it may reveal that the tool adds little. Either result should change what the speaker claims.

In a fictional story, label the episode as imagined. In a factual case, report what was actually observed and what alternatives remain.

NoteOptional research note: give curiosity a question

Imagine showing the restaurant owner two days with similar bookings but very different amounts of leftover food. Before presenting the software, ask what might explain the difference. The owner now has something specific to investigate: what did the booking count miss, and could that information improve tomorrow’s preparation?

Loewenstein’s (1994) information-gap account of curiosity explains why a partly understood situation can invite further attention. Curiosity arises when people become aware of a gap between what they know and what they want to know. Simply presenting an unfamiliar subject may leave them without enough understanding to notice a particular gap. A recognizable starting point and an answerable question can give the missing information a place to fit.

For this pitch, use the owner’s experience to make the question meaningful, then show how it could be answered. A comparison of forecasts with actual demand might reveal useful information, or show that the owner’s existing judgment performs just as well. Either result resolves something worth knowing. Curiosity helps invite the investigation. Disclose costs and limitations when they become relevant to the decision, even if withholding them would make the presentation more suspenseful.

A pitch can make the owner care about wasted food and still leave tomorrow’s preparation decision unanswered. The story makes the owner’s stakes understandable, while evidence lets the owner judge how common the problem is and whether the proposed explanation holds. The following five prompts keep that decision visible throughout the draft, with as much narrative as the message needs.

The STORY framework

STORY is a drafting aid, not an experimentally validated persuasion model. It is this book’s mnemonic: Shared reality, Trouble, Opportunity, Reasons, and Yes. “Yes” names the decision being requested; a well-informed “no” remains a legitimate outcome. The tests in Table 32.1 keep each part tied to that decision, from a recognizable opening to a request the audience can evaluate and decline.

For a brief version, try AND–BUT–THEREFORE (ABT): the shared situation, the consequential difficulty, and the proposed response. Use it as a compression test. A narrative index based on counts of connecting words can flag a draft that keeps adding information without establishing a difficulty or consequence. Such a writing heuristic does not measure whether the causal argument is valid or whether an audience understands it. Inspect what the connections actually mean.

Here is an imagined pitch built from those moves.

Shared reality: You already plan tomorrow’s preparation using bookings and experience, and you need enough food without tying money up in leftovers.

Trouble: Suppose you prepare 100 portions and sell 82. The 18 left over reveal a forecasting error, but preparing only 82 tomorrow could create a shortage if demand changes.

Opportunity: We propose comparing your estimate with a forecast built from sales history, bookings, and local events. The useful question is whether it adds information your current approach misses.

Reasons: A working prototype would establish that a forecast can be produced. It would not establish that following it reduces waste. We need forecasts recorded before demand is known and a comparison with your existing estimates.

Yes: Would you consider a four-week trial in which both estimates are recorded, while you keep control of preparation? We would agree on the data, staff time, costs, and criteria for continuing before the trial begins; either side could decide the evidence does not justify adoption.

After drafting, read the message from the owner’s position. Is tomorrow’s preparation decision still visible, or has the pitch drifted back to celebrating the software? Keep a statistic when it helps evaluate the proposal, and remove it when it merely makes the slide look authoritative.

Match evidence to the claim

The owner needs to know whether using the software improves preparation enough to justify its cost. A demonstration that the software runs leaves that question open, just as observed adoption would not by itself show that users benefit. Even an established benefit may cost more to deliver than it is worth. Start by identifying the claim on which the decision depends, then use Table 32.2 to ask what evidence could test it and what that evidence would leave unestablished.

Not every message needs all six claim types. A policy recommendation may hinge on need, causal impact, distribution, and cost; an early prototype may appropriately make only a feasibility claim. For the Reasons row of STORY, record the pivotal claim, its type, the strongest available evidence, the relevant source or comparison, and what remains unestablished. If the available evidence cannot support the necessary claim, weaken the claim, change the requested action, or collect better evidence.

Make the message clear and keep the evidence visible

A clear layout and familiar language help the owner inspect the evidence. They can also make a weak claim feel comfortable: repetition increases familiarity, which can increase perceived truth (Hasher et al., 1977; Fazio et al., 2015). The distinction from Chapter 13 therefore matters when editing: make the comparison easier to check, rather than merely making the conclusion easier to repeat.

For the restaurant owner, show waste in units they can reconstruct—portions or ingredient costs over a stated period—and identify the comparison. Resolve the question that earned attention rather than using suspense to delay an inconvenient limitation.

Return to the owner preparing food for tomorrow. The scene makes a cost understandable, but only data can show how common that cost is or whether the proposed tool reduces it. The aim is informed judgment: retain the human stakes while making the denominator and comparison inspectable (Slovic, 2007; Dahlstrom, 2014). As the donation experiment in Chapter 31 showed, adding statistics can reduce a story’s immediate motivational effect (Small et al., 2007). The braid prioritizes informed judgment even when that reduces immediate persuasion.

A useful draft can move from the scene to evidence, explain the proposed mechanism, and then return to the decision. If there are no pilot results yet, say so and request a trial. If there are results, show who participated, what was compared, and what remains uncertain. The ending should reflect the evidence available, even when that makes the story less tidy.

Check the selected details for an omitted affected group, a rare event made to seem common, or one person’s success presented as something everyone can achieve. The ethical audit below can then locate such omissions in actual sentences.

Adapt the message to the audience’s decision

A message built for the restaurant owner cannot simply be relabeled for a student, patient, or negotiating partner. Each faces a different decision and needs different evidence before acting; carrying over the scene while losing that decision turns adaptation into decoration. STORY travels across teaching, leadership, health, policy, entrepreneurship, and negotiation by preserving the questions that organize the message: start from the stakeholder’s consequential decision, make the trouble specific, state the proposed mechanism, match reasons to claims, and end with an action that can be refused.

In teaching, a case should create the need for a concept and then yield to analysis. In leadership, a change story should acknowledge loss and protect valued identity rather than invent a “burning platform.” In health and policy, a patient or citizen story should be paired with prevalence, effects, alternatives, and uncertainty. In entrepreneurship, the stakeholder—not the product or founder—is usually the protagonist. In negotiation, a short story can explain why an interest matters without pretending that one side’s account is the whole situation.

Across these settings, the important adaptation is to the audience’s decision and available evidence. A compelling scene is useful only if it helps that audience evaluate the proposal.

The following fictional examples show how a message can make two different requests understandable: changing a hiring process and protecting a delivery deadline.

Structured hiring

Persuasive goal: convince managers to use structured hiring interviews.

Information-only version: “Structured interviews have higher validity than unstructured interviews. Comparable questions and job-relevant criteria can reduce inconsistent judgment.”

Fictional story version: A manager defends a confident hiring decision, then discovers that the interview notes record impressions rather than evidence for the skills the job required. Comparing the same job-relevant questions across applicants would leave a more useful record. That is a reason to examine structured interviews; the imagined outcome cannot establish their advantage. Chapter 41 examines research on how interviews affect judgment.

A negotiation deadline

Persuasive goal: explain why a delivery deadline matters without making it sound like an arbitrary demand.

Information-only version: “We need delivery by June 1.”

Fictional story version: Last year, a component arrived four days late. The delay looked small on paper. But the production line had been scheduled tightly, customers had already booked installation dates, and workers were assigned to shifts that depended on the component arriving. By June 5, the line was stopped, customer orders were rescheduled, and the team had to explain a preventable delay. This year, the June 1 date is not a preference; it is the point that keeps the rest of the system from failing. We are willing to discuss price, batch size, or earlier partial delivery, but we need a plan that protects that date.

This story explains an interest without revealing a reservation value. It humanizes the constraint and opens problem-solving rather than simply asserting a position.

Audit the finished message

Once the message is evidence-aligned, make it easier to process: use concrete verbs, legible comparisons, one governing tension, and a visible denominator. Familiar language can provide an entry point and one meaningful surprise can create attention. These are editing choices, not reasons to believe the claim.

Numbers, images, and metaphors should make magnitude or mechanism inspectable. Ask what each representation reveals, what it hides, and whether the audience could reconstruct the underlying quantity. A memorable metaphor may help organize a claim, but its average persuasive advantage is small and context-dependent (Sopory & Dillard, 2002).

A message worth sharing is not necessarily worth believing. People may pass content on because it expresses identity, creates connection, signals competence, helps someone, or supports collective action (Berger & Milkman, 2012; Falk et al., 2013; Baek et al., 2017). Optimize first for accuracy and inspectability, then for transmission.

Check the draft for five recurring failures before choosing its final form:

  • Speaker-first opening: beginning with the organization, product, or research team before establishing the audience’s decision. Start with shared reality and a consequential stake.
  • Decorative trouble: adding a dramatic but that changes the subject without exposing why the current model fails. The trouble must make the proposed explanation necessary to consider.
  • Anecdote as denominator: allowing one vivid case to imply frequency, representativeness, or causal force. Pair the case with the comparison or distribution needed to judge scale.
  • Evidence dump: presenting impressive numbers that do not test the pivotal claim. State what each item of evidence should change and what it cannot establish.
  • Inevitable yes: ending as though understanding obliges agreement. Name the decision, costs, alternatives, uncertainty, and a meaningful way to decline, revise, or stop.

An anecdote may illustrate a concept, and a case may contain a story. Use Table 32.3 to choose the form by the communication task; the labels describe overlapping uses rather than exclusive categories.

Before sharing the draft, use Table 32.4 to check whether its emotional force, treatment of people, and requested action remain proportionate to the evidence. A vivid case needs revision if it leaves the audience with a mistaken impression of how common the problem is.

Practice Lab

Build a one-page STORY brief for one research claim, policy, product, leadership request, or negotiation proposal. The single brief contains five rows: Shared reality, Trouble, Opportunity, Reasons, and Yes. In the Reasons row, identify the pivotal claim, classify it using Table 32.2, give the strongest available evidence and comparison, state the uncertainty, and name what the evidence does not establish. At the bottom, answer three audit questions: Is any case presented as representative without a denominator? Can the audience meaningfully decline or revise the requested action? Which one sentence should be weakened or removed if the evidence is insufficient? Use the completed brief to deliver a 90-second message. Peer feedback identifies one unsupported inferential jump and one concrete repair. Working alone, mark every claim that needs outside evidence, check the pivotal one against its source, and revise the brief before delivering it again.

Take it forward

Read your draft from the audience’s position. Can a reader identify the problem, inspect the evidence behind the pivotal claim, and understand the next decision? Revise any sentence whose force depends on hiding a material fact.

Even a carefully drafted message leaves some meaning for the audience to infer. Chapter 33 follows what happens when another person interprets the message and gives the speaker a chance to correct a misunderstanding.
