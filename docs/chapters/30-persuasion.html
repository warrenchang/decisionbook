30  Persuasion

How Messages Change Beliefs and Attitudes

“If you would win a man to your cause, first convince him that you are his sincere friend.”

— Abraham Lincoln, address to the Springfield Washington Temperance Society, 22 February 1842

Imagine that a company plans to replace its familiar project system with a shared digital platform. Leaders present figures showing time lost to duplicate files. Employees ask who will see their activity data and when they will have time to learn the system. The next presentation contains more efficiency statistics, and the objections remain.

The leaders are answering Will this improve coordination? Their audience is also asking Will this cost me autonomy, and will the promised support arrive? A correct answer to the first question cannot settle the others. To make progress, the proposal itself may need to change.

The employees might comply with a rollout without believing in it, or support the proposal without finding time to use it. Persuasion concerns changes in beliefs and attitudes through communication; compliance, intention, and action are related but distinct outcomes. We will follow this platform proposal from an argument people resist to a decision they can examine.

NoteCore Idea

The same argument can receive different weight depending on its source, the listener’s stakes, and the opportunity to examine it. Experiments on credibility, involvement, and resistance explain why adding facts sometimes changes minds and sometimes misses the question. Use that evidence to build a proposal the audience can evaluate.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish belief and attitude change from agreement to a request and subsequent action.
  • Explain how credibility, involvement, argument quality, and autonomy affect responses to a message.
  • Use research to design and test a proposal that addresses the audience’s actual concerns.

Source, evidence, and stakes

The platform’s efficiency evidence may be sound while employees still have good reasons to resist. They must judge both whether the system works and whether those controlling it will protect their interests. Aristotle’s ethos, logos, and pathos provide a historical vocabulary for source credibility, argument, and emotion (Aristotle, 2007). They organize questions worth asking; they are not a scientifically validated three-part recipe. Research lets us examine when each matters.

In Hovland and Weiss’s (1951) experiment, students read articles attributed to sources of differing credibility. An argument about atomic-powered submarines could be attributed to physicist Robert Oppenheimer or the Soviet newspaper Pravda. Across four topics, the analysis of 61 students who attended all three sessions found greater immediate opinion change under the more credible source, although factual learning did not differ significantly. The source affected acceptance without supplying a better argument.

Credibility involves perceived expertise and trustworthiness, and its effects depend on the audience and context (Pornpitakpan, 2004). A technically expert manager may still be distrusted after broken promises. Credibility can also matter differently over time: a message initially discounted because of its source may gain influence as the content becomes less closely associated with the warning. This sleeper effect occurs under particular conditions; Kumkale and Albarracín’s (2004) meta-analysis does not support the rule that distrusted messages inevitably become persuasive if we wait.

For the platform proposal, a respected project leader who tested the system may be a more credible messenger than the chief technology officer alone. Yet a better messenger cannot substitute for verifiable governance. Employees need to know which data are recorded, who can see them, how the data may be used, and what recourse exists if the commitment is violated.

Evidence must answer the audience’s actual question and match the claim being made. A demonstration can show that a workflow is possible; comparison data can estimate an effect; a case can illustrate a mechanism. Research on two-sided messages distinguishes mentioning opposing arguments from answering them (Hovland et al., 1949; O’Keefe, 1999). O’Keefe’s meta-analysis found that messages acknowledging and refuting objections were more persuasive than one-sided messages on nonadvertising topics. Merely mentioning objections was less persuasive, and advertising studies showed a different pattern. A useful proposal therefore addresses the substance of an objection, rather than adding a token sentence that “some people disagree.”

Emotion gives the stakes personal meaning, but “make it emotional” is not a scientific prescription: fear, anger, and other emotions can affect judgment differently (Lerner et al., 2015). Protection motivation theory and the extended parallel process model distinguish the perceived threat from response efficacy—whether the proposed response works—and self-efficacy—whether the person can carry it out (Rogers, 1975, 1983; Witte, 1992).

Evidence favors including an effective, feasible response, without treating fear as something that usually backfires. Across 248 samples containing 27,372 participants, Tannenbaum and colleagues (2015) found a modest positive average effect of fear appeals on a combined measure of attitudes, intentions, and behavior (\(d=0.29\)). Appeals containing efficacy statements were more effective on average, but those without them did not, on average, reverse the intended effect. This supports giving people a credible route to protection; it does not justify exaggerating a threat or guarantee lasting behavior change from any particular message.

Three diagnostic questions keep the modes aligned:

  1. Source: Why should this audience trust this messenger on this claim?
  2. Evidence: What would a reasonable skeptic need to know, and what result would count against the proposal?
  3. Stakes: What should the audience feel if it understood the consequences accurately?

For the platform, that means showing where duplication occurs, how the proposed workflow is expected to reduce it, what comparison produced the estimate, and which costs were included. The message can make avoidable frustration and pride in competent work salient. It should not manufacture fear of dismissal unless employment risk is real and openly addressed.

Two routes through a message

The separate outcomes in Figure 30.1 keep three tests distinct: whether employees accept the claim, intend to act, and actually act. A detailed workflow comparison gives them material to inspect, but only if they have the time and motivation to use it. Others may initially rely on a colleague’s recommendation. The same presentation must therefore make careful scrutiny possible while giving a hurried reader a trustworthy entry point. Two influential theories explain how motivation and available attention affect this balance.

Petty, Cacioppo, and Goldman (1981) made the stakes concrete for 145 undergraduates: should students have to pass a comprehensive examination before graduating? Some were told the requirement would begin the following year; others were told it would begin in ten years, after they had left. The researchers independently varied argument strength and whether the recommendation came from an expert commission or a high-school class. When the examination could affect the students themselves, argument quality had more influence on their attitudes. When it was distant, source expertise had more influence. The same credentials received different weight when the listener’s reason to scrutinize the proposal changed.

The elaboration likelihood model, developed by Petty and Cacioppo, uses central and peripheral routes as shorthand for the high- and low-elaboration ends of a continuum (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986; Petty & Briñol, 2012). Toward the high-elaboration end, people devote more thought to issue-relevant information. Toward the low-elaboration end, attitudes depend more on processes requiring little issue-relevant thought, such as familiarity, affect, or a simple source cue. Mixed processing is common.

What encourages this thought? Motivation rises when the issue is relevant to a person’s goals; ability depends on knowledge, clear presentation, available time, and freedom from distraction. When both are high, argument quality generally matters more. When either is low, simple cues tend to carry more weight. More thought still does not guarantee a fair assessment: people can devote considerable effort to defending an identity or their initial position.

This does not divide people permanently into careful and superficial audiences. Expertise can be a shortcut, relevant evidence, or a reason to examine an argument more closely; it can also affect confidence in one’s own thoughts (Petty & Briñol, 2012). Its role depends on the task and conditions.

The heuristic-systematic model makes a related distinction between evaluating message content and relying on learned rules such as “experts can be trusted” (Chaiken, 1980; Chaiken et al., 1989). In Chaiken’s first experiment, students read two or six arguments from a communicator portrayed as likable or unlikable. Students expecting to discuss that topic later were more influenced by the number of arguments; those expecting to discuss another topic were more influenced by liking for the communicator. Anticipated discussion changed what received weight. This result does not imply that multiplying weak arguments makes a sound case, or that liking can never matter to an involved audience.

The model’s sufficiency principle proposes that people balance the effort of thinking against reaching a satisfactory level of confidence. A heuristic may suffice when little is at stake; a consequential unresolved question may prompt closer examination. Heuristic and systematic processing can also occur together, rather than forcing an either–or classification.

These models connect persuasion to the rest of the book. Heuristics, fluency, authority, social proof, and affect can influence attitudes under lower elaboration, but they can also alter what people think about, how they interpret evidence, and how confident they are under higher elaboration. The scientific question is therefore not simply “Which route?” It is “How much issue-relevant thinking occurred, what did this variable do within that processing, and what changed—belief, evaluation, intention, or action?”

Attitudes formed or changed through greater elaboration tend, under appropriate conditions, to be more persistent, resistant to counterpersuasion, and predictive of behavior because they are connected to a more developed structure of supporting thoughts. Lower-elaboration change may be more fragile, though repetition, identity, learning, and later experience can stabilize it.

Self-persuasion

An employee who has already heard management’s reasons may have reasons of their own for wanting work to improve. Asking what a worthwhile pilot would need to demonstrate can make those reasons explicit. Self-persuasion refers to attitude change through arguments people generate themselves. It can encourage reflection, but it can also make a favored position easier to defend; Chapter 7 distinguishes self-perception from the pressure to justify a prior commitment.

Müller and colleagues (2009) compared smokers who generated their own reasons against smoking with smokers who read reasons produced by others. Those generating reasons waited longer before lighting a cigarette; a second experiment also found a lower likelihood of smoking within 30 minutes when participants generated additional arguments. This was evidence about immediate smoking behavior, not sustained cessation. It gives a concrete reason to ask people to articulate their concerns rather than assume another prepared argument will be more useful.

A related approach gives people the role of adviser. In a preregistered field experiment, Eskreis-Winkler and colleagues (2019) randomly assigned 1,982 students from seven US high schools to give motivational advice to younger students or to a control condition. The advice activity lasted about eight minutes and covered matters such as study strategies and procrastination. Advice givers subsequently earned higher grades in mathematics and a class they had selected for improvement during that academic quarter. Giving advice might help through reflection, planning, or confidence; the study did not isolate self-persuasion as the cause.

For the platform proposal, invite employees to identify a recurring coordination problem and advise the pilot team on how to solve it. Then examine their proposals together. Their contribution should affect the design: asking for input solely to induce agreement turns an opportunity to learn into a concealed compliance exercise.

Understand the audience’s resistance

Understanding resistance begins by letting the audience describe it. Ask a few employees to explain the proposed change in their own words, identify their strongest objection, and describe what would make a trial worthwhile. An efficiency argument may miss a concern about professional judgment because the speaker and audience disagree about which values deserve attention.

Moral reframing connects a proposal to values the audience already endorses. Feinberg and Willer (2015) randomly assigned 306 US participants to read arguments for maintaining military spending. One emphasized loyalty and authority; another emphasized the military’s opportunities for disadvantaged people. Liberal participants expressed more support after the fairness argument than after the loyalty-and-authority argument, while conservative participants showed no corresponding difference. The outcome was immediate policy support, not a demonstrated change in voting or a permanent conversion. The implication is to investigate what matters to the audience, rather than assume that one’s own strongest reason must be theirs.

For employees concerned about professional judgment, the relevant case for the platform might be that it reduces repeated reporting and leaves more time for skilled work. That argument is defensible only if the proposed system really does so. Moral reframing should expose a relevant benefit, not disguise a cost or assign values to everyone in a demographic group.

Then distinguish agreement from action. Employees may accept the case for coordination yet lack training time. “Support innovation” leaves that obstacle untouched; “test this workflow for six weeks with two hours of training provided” creates a decision they can evaluate. Chapter 32 develops the message-building routine.

Employees who hear “Only people afraid of accountability oppose this system” now have a further reason to object. Their freedom to disagree has become part of the dispute.

Reactance is motivation to restore a freedom perceived as threatened (Brehm, 1966; Brehm & Brehm, 1981). It is one explanation for resistance, alongside reasonable disagreement, distrust, competing priorities, and practical constraints. Calling every objection reactance would simply repeat the original mistake of ignoring its content.

The pressure can be communicated by delivery as well as words. In three experiments, Weinstein, Vansteenkiste, and Paulmann (2020) examined listeners’ responses to controlling and autonomy-supportive speech. A controlling tone increased perceived pressure and self-reported defiance; the final experiment varied words and tone separately and found that both could contribute. These were responses to recorded messages, so they do not establish how much a different voice would change workplace adoption. They do show why a reassuring script may fail when delivered as an order.

An autonomy-supportive message can explain a strong recommendation while acknowledging concerns and identifying real choices. Self-determination theory connects motivation with experiences of autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Deci & Ryan, 2000). For the platform, that means room to propose safeguards and enough training to use the system competently. Where a decision is mandatory, communicate that limit honestly and specify what remains open to input.

Respecting the audience’s judgment also means helping it evaluate arguments encountered later. Inoculation theory proposes that a warning, followed by weakened counterarguments and refutations, can prepare people to resist subsequent persuasion (McGuire, 1964; Banas & Rains, 2010). Its purpose is to prepare an evaluative response, rather than demand loyalty to the original message.

Roozenbeek and colleagues (2022) tested short videos explaining manipulation techniques such as false dilemmas and personal attacks. Across six preregistered randomized studies with 6,464 participants, viewers saw an inoculation video or a neutral video and then evaluated unfamiliar social-media posts. The training improved recognition of manipulation, with less consistent effects on trustworthiness judgments and willingness to share. A YouTube field study also found better recognition within 24 hours. Recognizing a technique is useful, but these results do not establish permanent protection or less actual sharing of misinformation.

For the platform discussion, a claim such as “Either we adopt this system or we reject innovation” is a false dilemma: other systems, revised safeguards, and a limited pilot remain possible. Explaining that move prepares employees to evaluate later messages without telling them which proposal they must favor.

Corrections also deserve a realistic expectation. A broad effect in which corrections routinely strengthen false beliefs has proved elusive (Wood & Porter, 2019), although a corrected claim can continue to influence reasoning. Clear corrections that offer an alternative explanation can help people replace the account they previously used (Lewandowsky et al., 2012).

When a person is weighing reasons both for and against change, a conversation may be more useful than another rebuttal. Clinical motivational interviewing offers an example of eliciting those reasons while respecting autonomy (Miller & Rollnick, 2013). Its conversational stance can inform workplace discussions without making a management meeting equivalent to a clinical intervention.

The platform pilot should preserve meaningful choice where possible: teams can compare two workflows, propose safeguards, and examine results before scale-up.

Calling the pilot “voluntary” is insufficient if refusing the manager’s request could affect promotion. Specify what saying no would mean, as well as the proposal’s costs, uncertainty, and purpose. The actual freedom to decline must support the reassuring phrase in the presentation.

From information transfer to model updating

The culture chapter showed why the same action can carry different meanings. The platform proposal reveals the consequence for persuasion: leaders see coordination, while employees also see possible surveillance. Each side is using a different model, or working explanation of how the system will affect their work. More facts help when they answer a question within that explanation or give a reason to revise it. They help less when the speaker keeps answering a question the audience has already set aside.

Facts do not speak for themselves. In panel A of Figure 30.2, the observations are visible, but their meaning remains open. Is the outcome increasing steadily, accelerating, or beginning to level off? Panels B–D place different curves over exactly the same points, on exactly the same scales. Each curve makes a different story salient without changing the observations.

Evidence constrains models; assumptions help select among them. The curves are fitted by minimizing squared errors, but each fit assumes a particular trend shape.1 The three stories are not equally credible merely because we can fit them. We need to examine how well each accounts for the observations, what mechanism could produce its shape, and what further evidence would distinguish the alternatives. For the platform proposal, the same rising adoption figures might be presented as steady progress, gathering momentum, or early enthusiasm approaching its limit. A persuasive message should explain why its interpretation deserves more weight and what result would count against it.

A useful starting point is therefore to identify what the audience expects and what evidence could change that expectation. Predictive processing offers an analogy from perception and action, where information is related to predictions (Clark, 2013). It does not establish a general theory of persuasion. The sequence in Table 30.2 and the barrier matrix that follows are practical syntheses for examining a proposal; the preceding studies test particular processes that can influence its reception. The complete sequence is a drafting aid, not a tested theory of persuasion.

TipBring one message to redesign

Choose a product, policy, behavior-change request, difficult conversation, or research claim. In six lines, write: audience; desired response; current model; main barrier; credible evidence; constraint. Then write one observation that would genuinely discriminate the old model from your proposed one. If you cannot state that test, you may have a preference rather than a persuasive explanation.

Build a proposal the audience can evaluate

The original message said, in effect, “The evidence shows that this platform is efficient; therefore use it.” The redesigned proposal follows the audience’s model instead of ignoring it.

First, it begins in shared reality: teams are losing time to duplicate files, conflicting versions, and invisible handoffs. Second, it names the concern fairly: a shared system can improve coordination and create surveillance if access rules are vague. Third, it offers evidence matched to the claims: workflow data for duplication, a live demonstration for usability, written governance for data use, and a limited pilot for predicted time savings. Fourth, it makes identity and value explicit: the aim is to protect professional time and judgment, not replace them. Finally, it proposes an actionable and revisable step: two volunteer teams test the system for six weeks against predeclared measures, with training time, an independent feedback channel, and a decision rule for stopping, revising, or scaling.

The account of wasted time should illuminate an observed problem; invented threats of dismissal would distort the choice. Check the messenger, example, evidence, and requested action against the proposal’s stated purpose. If their effectiveness depends on hiding a cost or manufacturing an obligation, revise them.

Ethos, logos, and pathos now support the same update. A trusted project leader and a technical specialist share the message. The causal argument is inspectable. The emotional stakes—avoidable frustration, competent work, and fear of misuse—fit the situation. Motivation and ability are supported by relevance, demonstration, training, and time. Resistance is not treated as a defect; it helps specify the test.

A defensible proposal gives people reasons they can examine, along with room to question or refuse it. The pilot gives both sides evidence for accepting, revising, or rejecting the proposal. Message Design turns this diagnosis into a usable draft.

Practice Lab

Choose one organizational change that has met resistance. Produce a one-page persuasion brief containing: the desired response; the audience’s current model; a completed barrier matrix; one claim-evidence-warrant chain; the messenger and why that source deserves weight; the appropriate emotional stake; a feasible next action; and one observation that would make you revise or withdraw the proposal. End with the transparency test: would you still use this design if the audience could see the brief?

Take it forward

Return to the platform proposal. More efficiency statistics will help only if uncertainty about efficiency is what holds the audience back. A useful proposal lets employees examine the consequences they care about and gives leaders a reason to revise their plan when the objections are sound. The next chapter asks when a story can make those consequences easier to understand, and when it makes the proposal feel better supported than it is.
