21  Habits, Wanting, and Self-Control

The past can cue action before the present has finished deciding

“Habits are learned dispositions to repeat past responses.”

— Wendy Wood and David T. Neal, “A New Look at Habits and the Habit–Goal Interface”, 2007, p. 845

Imagine a student sitting down to write a term paper. The document is open, and the phone lies beside the keyboard. Halfway through the first sentence, the student hesitates over a word. A moment later, the phone is unlocked and social media is open.

Nothing interesting has arrived. The student puts the phone down, annoyed, and promises to leave it alone. A few minutes later, another difficult passage brings another check.

The intention to work was sincere and checking the phone was barely enjoyable, yet the same action keeps interrupting the goal. To understand why, we need to look at what happened before the screen lit up—and at what checking accomplished in those first few seconds.

NoteCore Idea

Repetition can teach a familiar situation to trigger a response with little deliberation. This makes useful routines easier to carry out, but it can also keep an unwanted action going. Changing the pattern begins with understanding its cues and consequences, then making a better response easier to repeat.

Learning goals

  • Explain how a repeated action can become a habit, and why frequency alone is insufficient to identify one.
  • Distinguish a habitual response from wanting an outcome and enjoying it.
  • Explain how reward cues can motivate effort, and why a near miss can encourage another attempt without improving its odds.
  • Use observations of cues, urges, and consequences to distinguish possible explanations of a recurring action.

Before reading on, recall one ordinary action you sometimes find yourself doing before you meant to. Keep that episode in mind as we continue our discussion.

What is a habit?

The first few times the student checked his phone while studying, there may have been a clear reason: waiting for a reply or taking a break. Finding new posts from friends satisfied his curiosity, making phone checking rewarding and giving him a reason to return to it during later breaks. As he repeated the action in the same setting, the pauses themselves could begin to bring checking to mind, even when no interesting posts appeared. A difficult sentence, boredom with the task, or the wish to take a rest could then prompt him to reach for the phone without first deciding whether checking would be worthwhile.

A habit is a learned association through which a context cue retrieves a response, while automaticity describes how readily that response starts with little deliberate guidance. Research on everyday routines emphasizes how repeating an action in a recurring context can make familiar cues prompt that action with less need for a fresh decision (Wood et al., 2002; Wood & Neal, 2007).

Frequency alone does not tell us whether an action is habitual. A student might check a course website every day because the instructor has promised an important update, with each visit following a fresh judgment that the information is needed. The same number of visits could instead result from opening the site whenever the laptop starts, even after the announcement period ends. The visible action is similar; what brings it about differs.

Following a goal or following a cue

Goal-directed control uses the expected consequences of an action and their current value: checking makes sense because the reply matters now. Habitual control draws more directly on a learned response to the setting: this is where checking usually happens. These forms of control can work together, as when a person deliberately sits down to study and then relies on familiar routines to get started.

The distinction concerns what guides the response, rather than whether someone is fully conscious. Goal-directed actions need not involve slow, explicit reasoning, and people can notice a habit while performing it. The research notes at the end explain how different methods test these distinctions.

There is considerable value in having useful actions become easy. If sitting down at the desk reliably brings up yesterday’s notes and the next unfinished task, the student does less negotiating with himself before work begins. Good habits help turn knowledge into practice, although a response learned in the past can remain available after its usefulness has changed.

How habits form

We can follow the student’s phone checking through four elements: a cue, an impulse, an action, and an outcome. The pause in writing is the cue; readiness to reach for the phone is the impulse; checking social media is the action; and a moment of distraction, an interesting post, or disappointment is the outcome. Here, impulse means readiness to act, which need not be consciously felt: sometimes the hand is already moving before the student notices.

The dashed arrow in Figure 21.1 represents learning across repetitions, as the cue becomes associated with the action (Wood & Neal, 2007). Reward or relief can encourage that repetition, but neither is required every time the habit is performed. The association is not defined by how pleasurable the outcome is (Wood & Rünger, 2016), which helps explain why the student can keep checking social media without finding anything interesting.

A learning curve, not a deadline

The same process can support a routine someone wants to build. Consider a person who plans to walk for ten minutes after lunch: although the walk initially needs deliberate preparation, repeating it after the same event can make finishing lunch a more reliable prompt to put on walking shoes.

Lally and colleagues (2010) followed people trying to establish everyday behaviors in a consistent context. The schematic curve in Figure 21.2 represents diminishing gains in reported automaticity. The median modeled time to reach 95% of the plateau was 66 days, with estimates from 18 to 254 days among participants whose data fitted the curve well.1

The useful question is whether the chosen action is becoming easier to start when its cue occurs. A brief walk after lunch gives that question a concrete answer; a resolution to become healthier does not yet specify what to repeat.

Why an unrewarding routine can persist

If a habit was learned through useful or satisfying actions, why does disappointing experience fail to stop it? Two possibilities deserve attention. A familiar setting may still retrieve the practiced response. Or the action may deliver a small, immediate benefit that is easy to overlook when judging the whole episode.

The popcorn people kept eating

Neal and colleagues (2011) tested the first possibility with an unappealing snack. Cinema visitors received fresh popcorn or popcorn that was a week old. People with weaker cinema-popcorn habits ate less when the popcorn was stale. Those with stronger habits ate similar amounts of fresh and stale popcorn in the cinema, despite liking the stale popcorn less.

The researchers also changed the conditions of eating. In one experiment, the familiar cinema context mattered; in another, using the nondominant hand disrupted automatic eating. Under these altered conditions, consumption became more responsive to the popcorn’s appeal. Habit strength was measured from prior behavior, while the researchers manipulated the immediate conditions.

The study shows how a practiced response can persist despite a disappointing outcome, and how disrupting its familiar execution can restore sensitivity to that outcome. For the student, it suggests looking at the repeated setting and movement, as well as the decision to check.

What checking accomplishes

Our student’s social media feed may contain nothing enjoyable, yet opening it briefly removes the difficulty of writing. While that relief is immediate, the costs of the unfinished term paper and lost concentration become apparent later. Judged over an afternoon, checking was unhelpful; judged over the first few seconds, it provided an escape.

To investigate that explanation, ask what changes immediately after checking: does uncertainty fall, does discomfort recede, or does something enjoyable appear? A student waiting for necessary information might give a very different answer from one avoiding a difficult task.

An occasional interesting post adds another reason to check, since rewards that arrive only sometimes can help sustain responding even across unrewarded attempts. In this example, occasional rewards and relief from the task are two possible contributors; observing what follows the checks can help us investigate them.

Learning theory distinguishes these consequences by what they do to future behavior. If receiving interesting content makes checking more likely, that is positive reinforcement; if escaping discomfort makes checking more likely, that is negative reinforcement. Both strengthen responding. Negative refers to removing an aversive condition, whereas punishment concerns consequences that reduce a response (Skinner, 1953). These are hypotheses about what sustains the student’s checking, not conclusions we can draw from frequency alone.

Wanting can outlast liking

The student expects little enjoyment from another check, yet still feels pulled toward the phone. If pleasure explained the entire attraction, that pull would be difficult to understand. Separating wanting, or motivational attraction, from liking, or experienced pleasure, gives us a way to examine what keeps the behavior appealing. Research distinguishes these components of reward even though they often occur together (Berridge & Robinson, 1998).

Feeling drawn to check despite little enjoyment is different from reaching automatically without noticing a desire. Habit and wanting can contribute to the same episode, but neither establishes the other. A craving may include a conscious expectation of relief or pleasure; motivational pull need not depend on an explicit forecast.

Incentive salience names the process through which reward-associated cues become motivationally attractive: they can draw approach even when pleasure is modest. It is a more specific research account of “wanting” than the everyday use of that word for any conscious wish (Berridge & Robinson, 1998).

The contrast between wanting and liking in Figure 21.3 suggests what the student could record: what checking seemed to offer, what happened immediately, and how it affected the work afterward. Comparing these records can help him choose a change that addresses what the checks actually provide.

Addiction can involve impaired control and continued use despite harm, with craving, withdrawal, and wider health problems. Theories of incentive sensitization and addiction help explain persistent pursuit of drugs, but frequent phone checking alone does not establish a clinical disorder (Robinson & Berridge, 1993; Koob & Volkow, 2016).

From expecting a reward to working for it

The attraction can begin before anything rewarding happens. In classic monkey experiments, an unexpected juice reward briefly increased the firing of many dopamine neurons. After a visual cue reliably predicted the juice, the brief response appeared at the cue, with little additional response when the expected juice arrived. If the juice was omitted, firing fell below its usual level at the expected time. This reward prediction error signals a difference between expectation and outcome; it is not a reading of how pleasurable the juice tastes (Schultz et al., 1997).

Learning what predicts a reward is only part of the story: obtaining it may require work. Salamone and colleagues (1991) gave rats a choice between pressing a lever for preferred food and eating less-preferred chow freely available nearby. Reducing dopamine signaling in the nucleus accumbens, a brain region involved in motivation, shifted behavior toward the freely available chow. The rats still sought and ate food, but allocated less behavior to the more demanding route. The result helps distinguish pursuing a reward from consuming it.

Dopamine therefore contributes both to learning about rewards and to mobilizing effort to obtain them. These functions need not share one signal: Mohebi and colleagues (2019) found that dopamine release in the accumbens tracked changing reward expectations even when the firing of the recorded dopamine cells did not change correspondingly. Calling dopamine the “happiness of pursuit” would still confuse motivation with pleasure; an insistent urge can be unpleasant.

For our student, this distinction suggests examining both what the next check promises and what continuing the paper requires. The paper also offers a valued outcome, but its benefits are distant and the effort is immediate. Making the next writing step clearer may help connect that effort to a result worth pursuing. Chapter 20 examines how a delayed benefit can acquire more weight in the present.

Why “maybe this time” can keep us going

What changes when a cue promises only a chance of reward? Fiorillo, Tobler, and Schultz (2003) trained two monkeys with visual cues predicting different probabilities of juice after a two-second delay. A gradual increase in dopamine-neuron activity during the wait was greatest at a 50% reward probability, where receiving and missing the reward were equally likely. This was distinct from the brief cue response, which increased with reward probability. The finding concerns a particular signal during anticipation; it does not show that a 50% chance maximizes pleasure, effort, or addiction.

A slot machine adds another complication: a loss can look like progress. Two matching symbols stop on the payline, while the third lands just above it. The player has won nothing, but the display invites the thought, “Almost.” In a simplified two-reel experiment with 40 volunteers, near misses were rated less pleasant than full misses, yet increased the reported desire to continue when players had selected their own play symbol (Clark et al., 2009). Here, stronger motivation accompanied a worse experience.

In a skill task, getting closer to a target may provide useful feedback. On a slot machine with independent spins, a near miss does not improve the probability of winning next time. Treating resemblance to success as evidence of progress can therefore sustain costly pursuit. The research note below separates this behavioral finding from stronger claims about dopamine and gambling disorder.

What the explanation suggests changing

Having already tried making a stronger promise, the student could now examine the conditions under which it has to be carried out. Each explanation suggests a different point of intervention: the setting that retrieves a response, the benefit that keeps it attractive, or the urge that precedes it. Here we follow those implications in the writing episode; Chapter 39 develops a complete plan for selecting, testing, and maintaining a change.

People reporting higher self-control also reported stronger beneficial routines in Galla and Duckworth’s (2015) studies. This association suggests looking beyond acts of resistance. Duckworth and colleagues (2016) develop the complementary argument that choosing and modifying situations can make self-control easier by intervening before an impulse becomes strong. What could the student arrange before sitting down to work?

Find the cues that arrive together

The phone is only one part of the student’s situation: it lies within reach, the difficult sentence creates a pause, and the social media app offers easy access to something that has previously brought news or relief. A cue ecology is a practical label for inspecting how several cues and enabling conditions converge, rather than assuming there is always one trigger to remove.

For the student’s next writing session, three observations would be a useful start: where the phone sits, what happens just before the hand moves, and what checking changes in the moment. Looking only for notifications could miss the role of pauses and boredom; if difficult passages repeatedly precede checking, the task itself also needs attention.

The first changes could be modest: place the phone out of reach, close social media on the laptop, and leave a note naming the next step in the term paper. The student can adjust these changes around any messages that require immediate attention. Limited Attention examines the earlier question of which signals enter awareness; here the aim is to change the practiced response that follows.

Give the pause a different response

Removing access to the phone leaves the difficult sentence in place, so the student also needs something feasible to do at that moment. An if–then plan might be: “If I get stuck, I write a rough version or mark the missing point, then continue to the next sentence.” A planned break can provide rest without turning every hesitation into an open-ended search for new posts.

The replacement should address what the old action was accomplishing when that function can be identified. If checking relieved task uncertainty, making the next step clearer is worth trying. If fatigue is the problem, an actual break may help more than demanding continued work. An if–then plan helps specify a response; repeated performance in context is still needed before it can become habitual.

This reasoning can extend beyond the phone. Imagine a manager whose defensive replies have become reliably cued by a colleague’s questioning tone. The manager might rehearse asking for one concrete example before explaining. The useful response is specific enough to practice at the moment when the familiar reaction begins.

Make room for an urge

Even after the setting changes, the student may still want to check. A brief pause can be used to notice what is happening: “I feel restless; I want to escape this sentence.” The immediate task is to choose the next action while the feeling is present. Relief need not arrive first.

Urge surfing is one way of practicing this pause: observe the changing sensations of an urge without immediately acting on it. The aim is to change the response to the urge, rather than require it to disappear first (Bowen & Marlatt, 2009). The optional BRAIN practice below offers a related structured observation exercise.

A lapse does not erase learning

If the student checks again after several successful writing sessions, the old response may still be available when familiar cues recur. That lapse does not by itself show that nothing was learned. What matters is whether the new response becomes easier to retrieve across further opportunities.

In Lally and colleagues’ (2010) study, missing one opportunity to perform the new behavior did not materially disrupt the formation process. A useful shorthand is no strengthening, but no reset: the missed occasion supplied no new repetition, but one miss need not erase earlier progress.

A successful afternoon and a well-established habit are different achievements. To distinguish them, observe relevant opportunities over time and whether the new response starts with less deliberation. Chapter 39 turns these observations into measures of maintenance and a practical recovery plan.

NoteWhere this chapter enters the loop

Past learning can make a familiar setting retrieve an action with little fresh comparison of its consequences. The decision map helps us ask how that learned response interacts with what we predict, value, and choose; it does not require each function to occur separately before every action. Changing the setting, rehearsing a response, and observing its consequences can help build a different pattern for the next encounter.

Practice Lab

Return to the mild, everyday behavior you recalled at the beginning. Build an observation record for two or three episodes; alternatively, compare the student’s phone checking while awaiting a necessary message with checking during a difficult sentence.

  1. Reconstruct the sequence. What happened just before the action, what did the person do, and what changed immediately afterward?
  2. Separate attraction from pleasure. What did the action seem to offer, and what did it actually provide? Record an urge only if one was noticed.
  3. Compare explanations. Did a familiar setting retrieve the response, did the action serve a currently valued goal, or could both contribute? Frequency alone cannot decide this.
  4. Name a distinguishing observation. Would the action continue when the expected benefit was unavailable, or change when the setting changed? Explain what either result would suggest and what it would leave uncertain.

Conclude with one explanation the observations support and one alternative they cannot yet exclude. This record supplies the diagnosis from which Chapter 39’s behavior-design studio builds a change plan.

Take it forward

Picture the next writing session, with the phone in a bag and the document open beside a note: “Explain the first result.” When the student reaches a difficult sentence, he leaves a rough phrase and continues. Perhaps the urge to check is still there, but the goal is to make returning to the work a response the setting increasingly supports.

  • Use this when an ordinary repeated action starts with little deliberation or continues despite disappointing consequences.
  • Do not infer habit, weak character, or addiction from frequency alone.
  • Try this next by comparing the cues, expected benefit, and actual consequence across a few episodes.

Making an action reliable leaves another question: is it helping to build the life the person wants? Deciding for a Better Life takes up that question. Behavior Design later develops the practical tools for changing a routine.

Optional research and practice notes

NoteResearch note: what counts as evidence of a habit?

The everyday question “Was that a habit?” has several research counterparts. Frequency records how often an action occurs. Automaticity concerns how readily the response is retrieved and performed with little deliberation. Outcome-devaluation tests examine whether responding persists when its consequence has lost value, a stricter operational test of habitual control in some laboratory paradigms. These measures are related but not interchangeable (Wood & Neal, 2007; Wood & Rünger, 2016).

Computational models add a different distinction. Model-based algorithms use a representation of transitions to evaluate how actions lead to outcomes. Model-free algorithms learn values or policies from experience without using that transition model for planning (Sutton & Barto, 2018). These approaches help formalize control, but a model-free fit is not a diagnosis of an everyday habit. Increased automaticity alone does not demonstrate insensitivity to outcome value.

NoteResearch note: unexpected outcomes and dopamine

Panels A–C of Figure 21.4 compare the prediction-error patterns introduced above: an unexpected reward before cue learning, a learned predictive cue, and an omitted expected reward (Schultz et al., 1997). A small response to an expected reward means little change from baseline, not an absence of dopamine or pleasure. Neuronal firing, dopamine release, and experienced pleasure are different measurements (Berridge & Robinson, 1998; Mohebi et al., 2019).

Panel D adds an unexpected delay. The reward fails to arrive when predicted, producing a negative error; its later arrival can then produce a positive error (Hollerman & Schultz, 1998). This is different from knowing in advance that a reward will take longer. The dip depends on a violated prediction, not simply on having to wait.

Figure 21.5 adds the interval before an uncertain outcome. The solid line represents a certain reward; the dashed line represents a 50% chance of the same reward at the same time. Under uncertainty, a sustained anticipatory response can grow during the wait. Both possible outcomes share that anticipatory history: receiving reward produces a positive error, while omission produces a negative error. The branches separate only when the outcome becomes known (Fiorillo et al., 2003).

The uncertainty-related rise is not a general work signal. It occurred during a waiting interval and does not show that working longer, working harder, or making rewards unpredictable always increases motivation.

NoteResearch Lens: what near-miss studies actually measure

In an fMRI study of 22 people, Habib and Dixon (2010) found greater overlap between responses to near misses and wins in the group classified as pathological gamblers. That is evidence about a blood-oxygenation signal associated with brain activity, not a direct measurement of dopamine release. Near-miss responses also occur in people without gambling disorder (Clark et al., 2009).

Sescousse and colleagues (2016) found stronger striatal responses to near misses in 22 pathological gamblers than in 22 controls. However, their dopamine-receptor-blocking drug manipulation did not reliably alter those responses. This result leaves the specific dopamine mechanism unresolved; it establishes neither a dopamine surge nor the absence of any dopamine contribution.

Uncertain rewards should also be distinguished from ambiguity, where the probabilities themselves are unclear. Chapter 16 examines why imaging evidence does not support a simple rule that known risk excites the reward system while ambiguity shuts it down.
