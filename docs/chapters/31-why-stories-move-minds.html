31  Why Stories Move Minds

Narrative turns information into simulated meaning

“We organize our experience and our memory of human happenings mainly in the form of narrative.”

— Jerome Bruner, “The Narrative Construction of Reality”, 1991, p. 4

Sara, a fictional team leader, reads an anonymous comment about her meetings: “We wish we could tell you bad news before it becomes an emergency.” She had thought solving problems quietly protected her team. Now she wonders whether her habit of arriving with an answer has made people reluctant to bring her a question.

Compare that scene with the statement “Leaders should encourage upward feedback.” The statement is clear, but the scene gives us something to investigate: why might a capable, well-intentioned leader make it difficult to speak? Following Sara can make the problem easier to imagine. It can also make her explanation feel true before we have tested it.

This chapter asks what a story adds to a message, and when the addition helps people judge well.

NoteCore Idea

A story gives us someone to follow through a consequential situation. It can help us imagine a decision, understand a perspective, and remember an idea. Those benefits also create a risk: a convincing account of one person can feel like evidence about everyone. Choose a story for a clear explanatory purpose, and supply the comparisons needed to judge its wider significance.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish a story from information, an example, and a vivid anecdote.
  • Explain how stories influence attention, simulation, identity, emotion, memory, and resistance.
  • Diagnose when a story adds decision-relevant meaning, when it adds distortion, and what evidence must accompany it.

What is a story?

For this chapter, a story follows someone pursuing a goal through events that have consequences. Look for a protagonist, goal, obstacle, stakes, choice, and change. Sara wants to protect her team; a comment raises doubts about how she has tried to do it.

An example can clarify a concept without developing an unfolding situation: asking each team member for a concern illustrates upward feedback. An anecdote is a particular case, which may or may not develop into a story.

Bruner (1986, 1990) distinguished narrative thinking, organized around agents and consequences, from reasoning through categories and general principles. Both can help us understand a problem. Fisher (1984, 1987) likewise distinguished a story’s coherence from its fit with an audience’s experiences and values. These accounts explain why a story may feel meaningful; neither coherence nor familiarity establishes that its causal account is correct.

Practitioner writers such as Lisa Cron and Will Storr offer useful craft questions: What does the protagonist want? What changes? Which belief becomes less workable?

Information is not yet narrative

A report might say, “The team introduced anonymous feedback and monthly check-ins.” To turn that information into a story, we need a person with a reason to care and events that alter the situation.

In our fictional continuation, Sara initially dismisses the comment as unfair. Then she rereads the last month’s meeting notes and finds that nearly every proposed solution was her own. At the next meeting, she waits for colleagues to describe the problem before offering an answer. One raises a delivery risk that has not appeared on the dashboard.

The story makes the tension between helping and taking over visible. It also embeds a hypothesis: Sara’s meeting style discouraged useful information. The scene makes that hypothesis easy to examine.

Story as model updating

The previous chapter treated a model as an explanation of what is happening and what to expect. A story can make a change in that explanation visible: Sara begins by equating quick solutions with helpful leadership, encounters information that troubles this view, and tries another way of running a meeting.

The five-part route in Figure 31.1 turns this change of explanation into a writing template.

When using it, ask what the protagonist believed, what observation challenged that belief, and what further evidence would justify the proposed change. The final question prevents the turning point from doing the work of a scientific test.

Why facts alone can fail

“Invite more participation” gives Sara a clear instruction. Yet it leaves out the difficulty that made the instruction necessary: her effort to protect the team by supplying answers may be keeping problems out of view. Unless that conflict becomes visible, readers can agree with the rule without recognizing the behavior it needs to change.

Following Sara’s hesitation lets readers examine why a choice is difficult, whom it affects, and how its consequences could unfold. A story earns its place when it supplies this missing understanding; a direct factual statement serves the reader when the decision is already clear.

Four mechanisms of narrative persuasion

A reader may follow Sara closely and still leave without understanding how to change a meeting. Another may remember her embarrassment while forgetting the information that challenged her belief. Holding attention, making a choice understandable, and leaving a useful lesson are different jobs. To design the story, identify which job the message needs. Research and theory suggest several overlapping ways stories can affect readers, including absorption in the narrative, identification with a character, and imagining consequences (Green & Brock, 2000; Cohen, 2001; van Laer et al., 2014). The four families below organize these mechanisms.

1. Attention and curiosity

A story begins when something important is unresolved. Someone wants something, but the outcome is uncertain. This creates a gap the audience wants to close. Will the protagonist succeed? What will they do? What will happen next? What does this reveal?

Attention matters because people cannot process everything. In an information-rich world, the first persuasive problem is not only accuracy. It is relevance. A message that does not create a reason to attend may disappear before it is judged. A story opening should therefore begin near disruption, not background. “Our organization was founded in 1986 and has always valued innovation” is rarely a strong opening. “On paper, the new system was perfect. In practice, no one used it” creates a question.

Transportation

Narrative transportation refers to mental absorption into a story world. When transported, people devote attention, imagery, and emotion to the unfolding narrative. They are not merely evaluating claims from the outside; they are experiencing a situation from within. Green and Brock’s work made transportation a central concept in narrative persuasion, and later research, including meta-analytic work by van Laer and colleagues, supports its importance.

Transportation matters because persuasion often fails when people remain outside the message, evaluating it defensively. A transported audience temporarily enters the logic of the story. They process the claim through simulation as well as detached critique. In teaching, this suggests that opening with a problem situation can be more effective than opening with a definition. The definition becomes meaningful after the learner has entered the problem.

Transportation also creates risk. A compelling story can smuggle assumptions into experience. A political film can make a misleading causal story feel true. A corporate change story can make a strategy feel inevitable while hiding alternatives. Ethical storytellers must ask: What world am I inviting the audience to enter? What assumptions does that world make? Whose perspective is centered? Whose perspective is missing? What will feel true after the story ends?

2. Identity and self-reference

Identification

From outside the meeting, it is easy to tell Sara to stop supplying every answer. From her position, holding back means changing the very behavior she has regarded as helpful leadership. Readers need access to that tension to understand what the proposed change asks of her. Identification occurs when audience members imaginatively take the perspective of a character, share goals or emotions, and experience events partly from inside the character’s situation. Identification differs from liking. You may identify with a character you do not entirely like because you understand their motives or feel their struggle from within.

Following a character can shift the question from “What do I think of this person?” toward “What would this situation be like from their position?” Sara need not be wholly admirable for readers to understand her wish to appear competent.

A useful character has intelligible reasons and meaningful constraints. Showing why speaking up risks embarrassment, for example, gives an audience more to consider than labeling a colleague timid. Another character may experience the same meeting differently.

Self-reference

A reader may connect Sara’s predicament to a meeting they have led or attended. That connection gives the abstract issue a personal reference point. It can also narrow interpretation: a leader who recognizes only their own good intentions may miss how the meeting feels to a junior colleague.

Use identification to expand the perspectives available for judgment. After following Sara, ask what the quiet team member wanted, what made speaking costly, and how the same exchange looked from that seat.

3. Emotion, memory, and reduced resistance

Stories persuade because they connect information to value. Emotion is not merely decoration added to cognition. It helps prioritize attention, encode memory, guide action, and signal what matters. In a story, emotion arises from stakes: someone wants something, something could be lost, and the outcome is uncertain. Without stakes, there is little reason to care.

A common mistake in academic and managerial communication is to remove all emotion in the name of objectivity. Objectivity is important for evidence, but persuasion requires significance. A story can preserve accuracy while making significance visible. “The project was delayed by six months” is information. The meaning becomes clearer when we understand who was affected: a small supplier that invested in new equipment, expected a contract, lost revenue, laid off workers, and became reluctant to join future innovation partnerships.

Stories can also improve retrieval when their causal and emotional structure organizes otherwise disconnected details. People may remember what someone wanted, what blocked them, what they chose, and what happened. The story can provide a retrieval path for the principle, although vividness may also preserve an unrepresentative case better than the qualifying evidence.

Reduced resistance

A teacher may use a story to help students enter a difficult choice before defending a position. The same absorption that makes the choice accessible can leave less attention for questioning its assumptions. The teaching task is therefore to invite participation and then reopen the account to examination. Green and Brock (2000) linked transportation with story-consistent beliefs and evaluations across their experiments.

For a teacher, the opportunity is to let students encounter a difficult choice before discussing the principle. The responsibility is to reopen scrutiny afterward: which assumptions made the ending seem reasonable, and what would happen if they were wrong?

4. Simulation and causal understanding

Students may readily endorse “report unfavorable results” or “look for shared interests” without seeing why either becomes difficult in practice. A favor can create felt obligation; an urgent demand can hide the interest that would make a trade possible. If teaching stops at the rule, the obstacle remains unexamined. Stories let students rehearse the decision before facing its consequences: What do we know? What is uncertain? What are the options? What are the risks? What would I do?

Mental simulation helps people understand causal structure. Instead of passively receiving advice, they rehearse consequences. This makes story especially useful for risk communication, ethics training, leadership development, entrepreneurship, public policy, and negotiation.

An ethics lecture that says, “Conflicts of interest are dangerous” may be less effective than a story in which a researcher accepts a small favor, then a larger favor, and gradually finds it harder to report unfavorable results. The story simulates moral drift. A negotiation lecture that says, “Positions hide interests” may be less effective than a story in which two teams fight over a deadline until they discover that one side needs symbolic reassurance and the other needs operational flexibility.

Causal stories and causal evidence

Stories persuade because they organize causality. They show how one thing led to another: because the protagonist believed X, they did Y; because they did Y, consequence Z followed; because Z happened, the old belief had to be revised. A coherent sequence can help a reader organize scattered evidence, while also making a missing link easy to overlook.

Organizations do something similar after success or failure. They construct stories: the market changed, leadership failed, customers were not ready, the technology was immature, incentives were wrong, or the team lacked discipline. The story determines what the organization learns. If the story is wrong, the organization learns the wrong lesson.

This creates a discipline: distinguish causal story from causal evidence. What mechanism does the story imply? What evidence supports that mechanism? What alternative story could explain the same events? What base rates or comparison cases are missing? What role did luck play? What structural factors are hidden by focusing on one character?

NoteResearch Lens: following the same story

Naturalistic neuroscience has found time-locked similarities in brain activity when people encounter the same films or stories, and speaker–listener coupling has been associated with comprehension (Hasson et al., 2004, 2008; Stephens et al., 2010). Selection and sequence can guide audience attention.1

The power—and danger—of one

Stories often persuade through one face, one name, one family, one patient, one customer, one employee. This can be morally important because large numbers can become abstract. A single person can restore emotional reality. In donation experiments, presenting an identifiable person has sometimes elicited more giving than presenting statistical information about need (Small et al., 2007).

But the same mechanism can distort judgment. One vivid victim may receive attention while many unseen victims are neglected. A rare disease with a compelling story may attract disproportionate funding. A dramatic crime may shape policy more than widespread harms. A single customer complaint may dominate managerial attention even when broader data point elsewhere.

The ethical challenge is to connect story and scale. A good humanitarian message may begin with one person’s story, then connect it to accurate statistics: this is one person, and the evidence shows how common or rare this pattern is. The story creates attention and care; the statistic creates proportion. Together they support responsible action.

Practice Lab

Select one existing message that uses a story—or one abstract claim for which a story has been proposed. Complete a one-page narrative diagnosis:

  1. Decision: What belief or choice should the message help the audience examine?
  2. Narrative job: Which one function is most needed—attention, simulation, identity and self-reference, or emotion and memory?
  3. Form: Is a story necessary, or would a direct statement, example, comparison, or case make the point with less distortion?
  4. Evidence companion: If a story is retained, name the denominator or scale estimate, relevant comparison, evidence for the proposed mechanism, strongest alternative account, and one affected perspective the story leaves out.
  5. Verdict: Retain, replace, or omit the story, and give the reason in one sentence.

The output is the diagnosis, not a polished story. Chapter 32 provides the construction workflow if the verdict is to retain one.

Take it forward

A story can make an unfamiliar problem worth attending to, but its vividness cannot tell us how common the problem is. When you retell a case, carry that distinction with it: explain what the story illustrates and which broader claim still needs evidence.
