28  Social Influence

Compliance, Authority, and Group Decisions

“Power tends to corrupt and absolute power corrupts absolutely.”

— Lord Acton, letter to Mandell Creighton, 5 April 1887

Lena, a test engineer, emails seven colleagues after a component fails its safety check for the second time that week. The senior manager, Sam, replies, “It is probably fine,” and nobody adds a comment. By noon, the issue has no named owner. Does the silence mean that the others checked the evidence and agreed, that they are reluctant to challenge the manager, or that each expects someone else to investigate?

Those explanations call for different remedies. An invitation to speak may help with withheld doubts, but it will not assign an inspection to anyone. Other people can influence what we believe, what we say, and what we do, without those changes matching one another. We begin with everyday requests, where this distinction is easy to miss, before returning to authority and shared responsibility.

NoteCore Idea

Someone can accept a request without accepting its merits, just as a team can appear unanimous while its members privately disagree. To understand social influence, ask what changed in the person’s information, relationships, or cost of saying no. Different influences require different safeguards.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish changes in private judgment from conformity, compliance with a request, and obedience to an instruction.
  • Use experimental evidence to explain how favors, commitments, scarcity, and liking can influence a response.
  • Diagnose whether silence reflects withheld doubts, unshared evidence, mistaken beliefs about colleagues, or uncertainty about who should act.
  • Redesign one group decision or escalation process so evidence, ownership, dissent, and a safe path to correction are explicit.

How a request becomes harder to refuse

Social influence includes changes in judgment and behavior arising from other people’s words, actions, or presence. Conformity brings a response into line with a group; compliance means agreeing to a request; obedience means following an authoritative instruction. Persuasion concerns attempts to change beliefs or attitudes through communication. These processes can overlap, but observing one does not establish the others (Cialdini & Goldstein, 2004). An employee can comply because refusing feels disloyal while still believing the proposal is mistaken.

Social Norms and Conformity examined how others supply information and expectations about appropriate behavior. Here the question is how the interaction itself can change a response. A reason, a gift, or an earlier promise can make a request seem different even when its practical costs remain the same.

Langer, Blank, and Chanowitz (1978) approached people about to use a photocopier. For a small copying request, 60% agreed without a reason, compared with 93% when the requester added the circular explanation “because I have to make copies.” With a larger request, that uninformative explanation lost its advantage: both conditions produced 24% compliance. The result concerns a routine, low-cost interaction, not a universal power of the word because. It also measures permission to go ahead, not a change in beliefs about whose copying mattered more.

A favor can create a different pressure. In Regan’s (1971) experiment, male university students evaluated art alongside someone who was actually working with the researcher. During a break, that person sometimes brought the participant a soft drink; in another condition, the experimenter supplied it. Later, the apparent fellow participant asked the student to buy raffle tickets. When the requester had supplied the drink, 58% bought more than one ticket, compared with 25% across the two control conditions. Receiving a drink from someone else did not produce the same increase. The requester’s pleasant or unpleasant manner changed liking, but its effect on purchases was weaker and not statistically significant. The pattern supports an obligation to reciprocate rather than simply a better mood or greater liking.

The practical distinction is between appreciating a favor and deciding that the next request is worthwhile. A colleague’s help can sustain a cooperative relationship without making every later proposal a good one. Chapter 25 examines reciprocity as a motive for cooperation; here it helps explain how one person gains compliance from another.

The sequence of requests also matters. In the foot-in-the-door technique, a small request precedes a larger one. Freedman and Fraser (1966) first approached householders about displaying a small road-safety sign. Later, a different requester sought permission to place a large, unattractive driving-safety sign on their lawn. In that condition, 76% agreed, compared with about 17% approached only with the large request. The two comparison groups contained 25 and 24 householders. An initial action can affect self-perception, commitment, or expectations about the interaction; research supports several processes rather than one universal explanation (Burger, 1999).

The door-in-the-face technique reverses the order. Cialdini and colleagues (1975) first asked students to volunteer for two hours a week for at least two years. After they refused, the requester proposed a two-hour zoo outing with young people from a detention center. In the first experiment, 50% expressed willingness to help after this retreat, compared with about 17% asked only about the outing. A perceived concession can invite reciprocation, although willingness to volunteer is not the same as attending. Chapter 36 applies the distinction between a concession and the value of an offer to bargaining.

In low-balling, the action stays the same while an important cost is disclosed after commitment. Cialdini and colleagues (1978) invited students to an experiment, revealing its 7 a.m. start either before or after asking for an initial decision. Attendance was 53% when the time came later and 24% when it was disclosed at the outset. The later disclosure gave the same appointment a different decision history. A prior “yes” can make withdrawal harder even when no useful new reason for attending has appeared.

These studies show why an earlier interaction belongs in the explanation of a later choice. They do not establish that every small request escalates, every concession must be repaid, or every initial commitment should be abandoned. Before continuing, ask whether the new terms serve the original purpose and whether the costs were visible when the commitment was made.

Scarcity, liking, and shared identity

A request need not create an obligation to influence a choice. It can also change how desirable an option feels. In their first experiment, Worchel, Lee, and Adewole (1975) asked participants to evaluate cookies presented in jars containing either two or ten. Scarce cookies were rated as more desirable, and a reduction attributed to other people’s demand increased desirability more than an accidental reduction. The study measured evaluations of the same product under different supply cues, not a general increase in purchases or actual product quality. “Only two left” can therefore make demand or a lost opportunity salient without establishing that the item is worth buying.

Liking and perceived similarity can affect a request even without a long relationship. Across three experiments, Burger and colleagues (2001) found greater compliance after brief interaction, mere exposure to the requester, or information suggesting similarity. These are particular experimental conditions, not evidence that warmth invariably overrides judgment. Fluency and Familiarity explains how repeated exposure can change a feeling of familiarity, while Connection examines the more demanding work of understanding another person.

Shared identity goes beyond liking an individual. A request from “one of us” may invoke mutual obligation or make refusal feel disloyal. This influence is often called unity in practical accounts of influence; its scientific foundations lie in research on social identity and group membership. The group-membership experiments in Chapter 25 show how even arbitrary divisions can affect whose interests receive weight. Culture and Identity examines how different identities become relevant to a choice. Group membership can organize trust and cooperation, but does not establish the truth of a claim or the merits of a request.

Lena’s safety email now presents a more specific problem. Her manager’s reassurance could contain useful expertise, but it could also make challenge socially costly. The next step is to examine what authority contributes to the decision.

Authority and responsibility

The manager may know more than the engineer: perhaps the component was tested yesterday, or the apparent fault is normal under these conditions. Deferring to relevant expertise can save time and coordinate action. Yet a managerial title alone does not establish that this evidence exists. If the engineer stops asking because disagreement seems disloyal, authority has changed the decision through the social cost of speaking.

How far can an authoritative instruction carry someone past their own concern? Milgram studied a situation in which following instructions appeared to cause another person pain.

In the condition Milgram reported in 1963, forty men sat at a board of switches labelled from 15 to 450 volts. Each wrong answer from a learner in another room was to receive a stronger shock. At 300 volts, the learner pounded on the wall and stopped answering; the experimenter instructed the participant to count silence as an error. All forty reached 300 volts, five stopped there, and twenty-six reached 450. The learner was an actor and the shocks were simulated, but participants had been told they were administering real shocks.

What that continuation means remains contested. Milgram reported visible distress, yet later archival analysis found that belief in the setup varied and that skepticism may itself have increased continuation in some variants (Perry et al., 2020). Institutional trust, uncertainty about harm, gradual escalation, pressure to finish, and identification with the scientific project offer competing or interacting explanations (Haslam & Reicher, 2017). The wider series changed the situation and obtained different patterns (Milgram, 1974); the 26-of-40 result is evidence about one particular condition, not a population-wide obedience rate.

Burger (2009) tested a modified version of a different Milgram condition, stopping after the learner’s protest at 150 volts. In his baseline group, 28 of 40 participants began the next task item and had to be stopped by the experimenter. Its earlier cutoff and screening procedures also underline why the original distressing protocol cannot simply be repeated under present ethical standards.

These studies make the conditions of deference worth examining. Lena may reasonably leave a wider safety assessment to management, provided the concern reaches someone who can assess it and accepts responsibility for responding. Before closing the issue, ask what evidence supports the reassurance and who checked it. A credential or title can help locate expertise; it cannot replace that connection between reasons and accountable action.

Groupthink: pressure to agree and limits of the theory

Suppose one of Lena’s colleagues privately shares her concern but says nothing after Sam’s reassurance. If everyone reads that silence as agreement, the group gains apparent confidence without gaining evidence. A cohesive team can coordinate quickly, yet protecting that agreement can make an objection feel like a personal betrayal. Janis (1982) made groupthink a familiar account of how pressure for concurrence could narrow the appraisal of evidence and alternatives in policy groups. In his model, antecedent conditions could produce self-censorship, pressure on dissenters, and an illusion of unanimity, followed by defective decision processes. The framework remains a useful warning that agreement can be socially produced rather than evidentially earned.

It is also contested. Aldag and Fuller (1993) found that research did not convincingly validate the complete syndrome or a simple path from its proposed characteristics to poor outcomes. Esser’s (1998) review found mixed results and continuing heuristic value, while emphasizing theoretical and methodological problems. The responsible use of the term is therefore as an umbrella that prompts diagnosis, not as a diagnosis supplied by hindsight.

A bad result can follow from uncertainty, missing expertise, poor incentives, implementation failure, or chance. Unanimity can reflect genuinely convergent evidence or shared values. A meta-analysis found no significant overall effect of cohesion on decision quality; poorer decisions appeared under additional conditions such as directive leadership, and the result depended partly on what “cohesion” meant (Mullen et al., 1994).

A safeguard earns its place by revealing evidence, disagreement, or responsibility that the group previously lacked. Psychological safety—a shared belief that interpersonal risk taking is safe—was associated with learning behavior in 51 work teams (Edmondson, 1999). That association gives a reason to examine the cost of speaking. A colleague who raises a concern needs to see it considered, with a response that makes the next concern possible to raise.

The informational and normative routes in Social Norms and Conformity: When Other People Become Evidence can contribute to apparent consensus without being identical to groupthink. Decision Hygiene carries the safeguards forward as a repeatable process for independent evidence, structured disagreement, explicit authority, and learning.

When the group knows more than the discussion reveals

There is another possibility in the safety thread: nobody is hiding a doubt, but each person holds only part of the relevant evidence. One has the new test result, another remembers a similar fault, and Sam has yesterday’s reassuring supplier report. A meeting can repeat that shared report while leaving the two warning signs unconnected.

Stasser and Titus (1985) studied this problem by distributing information about candidates among group members. Some facts were shared; others were known to only one member. In the hidden-profile condition, combining the unshared facts could reveal an alternative that each person’s initial information made less attractive. Discussion tended to favor shared and preference-consistent information, so having the relevant facts somewhere in the group did not ensure that they shaped its choice.

For Lena’s team, an evidence round should precede advocacy: what did each person observe that others may not know? Record the observations together before asking for a vote. This addresses shared-information bias. A private poll alone would preserve initial judgments while leaving the missing evidence unpooled.

Bystanders and pluralistic ignorance

Even when people agree that a problem is serious, action can still stall. The safety email has several recipients, which seems to give it several chances of being handled, yet each may wait for someone else. The next problem is how shared awareness becomes—or fails to become—personal responsibility.

Darley and Latané (1968) placed participants alone in cubicles for what appeared to be a group discussion over an intercom. A recorded voice then appeared to suffer a seizure. Among the thirteen participants who believed they were the only other person listening, 85% sought help before that speaker’s allotted turn ended; among thirteen who believed four additional listeners were present, 31% did. Those percentages describe reporting within a particular time window. By the study’s six-minute cutoff, the corresponding proportions were 100% and 62%.

The experiment changed how many other potential helpers a participant believed were present. It measured an individual’s response, not the probability that at least one member of a real crowd would help. That distinction matters when applying the bystander effect outside the laboratory.

Across more than 7,700 participants and 105 independent effect sizes, Fischer and colleagues (2011) estimated an overall reduction in individual helping equivalent to 0.35 standard deviations (Hedges’ g of −0.35) when others were present. The reduction was smaller in dangerous emergencies, and some conditions showed no reduction or a positive effect when bystanders could provide physical support. Other people can spread responsibility, but they can also make intervention feasible.

Latané and Darley (1970) described helping as a sequence: notice the event, interpret it as an emergency, accept responsibility, know how to help, and decide to act. A failure at any step can prevent intervention.

Other people can interfere at several stages. If no one else reacts, the situation may seem less serious. This is pluralistic ignorance: people privately feel concern but infer from others’ calm behavior that nothing is wrong. If many people are present, responsibility diffuses: “Someone else will act.” If action might be embarrassing, people hesitate.

The same gap can appear in a classroom. Several students privately feel confused, yet each takes the absence of questions as evidence that everyone else understands. Asking separately what students understand and how well they think their classmates understand would reveal the gap more directly than observing silence. In a different setting, Prentice and Miller (1993) found that students misperceived their peers’ comfort with campus drinking norms.

An organization can make a similar comparison between private views and beliefs about colleagues’ views. Anonymous input may uncover concerns that a public “Any objections?” misses, although silence can also reflect fatigue, uncertainty, or lack of relevant information. The purpose is to discover what people think before deciding why they did not speak.

The unanswered safety email raises a separate possibility: all seven recipients may agree that the issue deserves attention, while each expects another person to act. Here the missing information concerns ownership. Naming an investigator, confirming receipt, setting a response time, and specifying a backup if the investigation stalls would address that gap. These arrangements still leave room to question the manager’s reassurance and to obtain any evidence the group lacks.

Match the safeguard to the missing step

An invitation to speak may uncover a withheld doubt, but it still leaves Lena’s inspection unassigned. Naming an inspector solves that ownership problem without supplying evidence the inspector does not hold. An anonymous poll can expose mistaken beliefs about colleagues’ views, but it cannot create expertise absent from the group. A mismatched safeguard can leave everyone feeling that the matter has been handled while the original gap remains.

NoteDiagnose the pathway

A favor, a title, or apparent agreement can change behavior without resolving the underlying question. Expertise can guide judgment, but an instruction does not transfer accountability away from the actor or the institution that designed the role. Persuasion takes up the related question of how communication changes beliefs and attitudes.

Practice Lab

Choose a request you accepted despite reservations, or use the safety-email example. First distinguish what you privately believed from what you said or did. Then apply the questions below.

  1. Name the most plausible influence cue or group pathway and the observation that supports it. Would the response have differed without the favor, prior commitment, title, or apparent agreement?
  2. Identify the missing check: information about the request, freedom to reconsider, a chance to disagree, or an owner for the next action. For the safety case, specify who investigates, how receipt is confirmed, and when the issue escalates.
  3. Write a sentence a junior colleague could use before proving wrongdoing: “I may be missing something, but can we check the test results before closing this?”
  4. State what you expect the redesign to reveal or change, and what would make you revise the diagnosis.

Take it forward

The safety email can now end differently: “Lena will inspect the component by 3 p.m. and post the evidence here. Until then, we will follow the existing safety procedure; if the inspection cannot be completed, Sam will escalate it.” Colleagues can still challenge the assessment, but they no longer need to guess who owns the next step.

The same invitation to question Sam may feel routine to one colleague and disrespectful or risky to another. Authority, silence, and dissent acquire meaning within relationships and local expectations. Culture and Identity therefore asks what the same observable action means here.
