32  Building an Evidence-Aligned Message

Use narrative to carry evidence, not to replace it

“The first principle is that you must not fool yourself—and you are the easiest person to fool.”

— Richard P. Feynman, “Cargo Cult Science”, 1974, p. 12

A restaurant owner receives a pitch for a forecasting assistant. The first slide lists algorithms; the second celebrates the development team. By the third, the owner still cannot tell whether the tool will help decide how much food to prepare tomorrow.

Imagine rebuilding the pitch around that decision. Too much preparation wastes ingredients; too little loses sales. The proposed tool might help, but the owner needs to see how it uses the restaurant’s records, what evidence supports its forecasts, and what trying it would cost.

We will use this fictional pitch to build a message that earns attention and gives the audience enough information to decide. A story is optional; a defensible claim and a clear request are essential.

NoteCore Idea

Start with the audience’s decision, explain the problem your proposal addresses, and show what supports the proposed response. Then make the request concrete enough to evaluate. The strength of the message should come from the fit between its claim and evidence, including what remains uncertain.

Learning goals

  • Build one message with STORY as the primary workflow; use AND–BUT–THEREFORE only as an optional compression test.
  • Match the strength of each claim to appropriate evidence, comparison, and uncertainty.
  • Produce and audit a message that remains accurate, dignified, and genuinely rejectable.

Design the change

Begin with the audience’s current story

Ask the owner how preparation decisions are currently made and where mistakes become costly. An experienced owner may already forecast informally using bookings, weather, and local events. Beginning with “Restaurants need data-driven decisions” would overlook that knowledge and give the owner a reason to distrust the rest of the pitch.

Hidden resistance and protected identity

Find out what makes trying the tool unattractive. The owner may doubt its accuracy, lack clean records, or worry that staff will treat recommendations as orders. These are different problems. A demonstration can address usability; a pilot may test forecasts; explicit override rules address control. If the proposed product cannot meet an important constraint, improve the proposal before polishing the message.

Protagonist, goal, and stakes

If a story would help, follow a person facing the decision. Picture the owner checking yesterday’s waste against today’s bookings, knowing that a cautious preparation plan could leave popular dishes unavailable. This scene gives the audience a goal and a trade-off. It does not need an invented crisis or a heroic founder.

Conflict, obstacle, and misbelief

The obstacle should make the proposed response worth considering. Perhaps the owner has sales records but cannot readily combine them into a daily forecast. That problem suggests a role for the tool. If the real problem is unreliable deliveries, better demand forecasts may help little.

Do not assume the audience’s current belief is a “misbelief” simply because it conflicts with the proposal. The pitch should reveal a limitation that can be investigated, with room for the investigation to vindicate the current practice.

Turning point, evidence, and change

A useful turning point changes what the audience can see. Comparing a forecast with the owner’s existing estimate may reveal a promising improvement, or it may reveal that the tool adds little. Either result should change what the speaker claims.

In a fictional story, label the episode as imagined. In a factual case, report what was actually observed and what alternatives remain. An illustrative ending cannot establish demand, effectiveness, or savings; those claims need their own evidence.

NoteOptional research note: give curiosity a question

Imagine showing the restaurant owner two days with similar bookings but very different amounts of leftover food. Before presenting the software, ask what might explain the difference. The owner now has something specific to investigate: what did the booking count miss, and could that information improve tomorrow’s preparation?

Loewenstein’s (1994) information-gap account of curiosity explains why a partly understood situation can invite further attention. Curiosity arises when people become aware of a gap between what they know and what they want to know. Simply presenting an unfamiliar subject may leave them without enough understanding to notice a particular gap. A recognizable starting point and an answerable question can give the missing information a place to fit. This is an account of how curiosity can arise, rather than a guarantee that every unanswered question will hold attention.

For this pitch, use the owner’s experience to make the question meaningful, then show how it could be answered. A comparison of forecasts with actual demand might reveal useful information, or show that the owner’s existing judgment performs just as well. Either result resolves something worth knowing. Curiosity helps invite the investigation; it does not establish that the proposed explanation is true. Disclose costs and limitations when they become relevant to the decision, even if withholding them would make the presentation more suspenseful.

Build the message with one primary workflow

The following five prompts keep the audience’s decision, explanation, evidence, and requested action together. Use them as a drafting aid, adapting the amount of narrative to the message’s purpose.

The STORY framework

STORY is this book’s practical mnemonic: Shared reality, Trouble, Opportunity, Reasons, and Yes. “Yes” names the decision being requested; a well-informed “no” remains a legitimate outcome.

For a brief version, try AND–BUT–THEREFORE: the shared situation, the consequential difficulty, and the proposed response. This is an optional compression test. Neither it nor STORY is a validated psychological law, and a message need not force every audience into the same narrative sequence.

For an illustrative restaurant-forecasting pitch, the moves could be: S—independent restaurants already use point-of-sale and ordering tools; T—owners still have to guess daily preparation, so overestimates waste food and underestimates lose sales; O—a forecasting assistant turns existing sales history into preparation recommendations; R—report only verified pilot behavior, comparisons, uncertainty, and limits; Y—invite a defined number of eligible restaurants to a time-bounded pilot with stated success criteria. The sequence is reusable; any traction number must be real, sourced, and appropriate to the claim.

After drafting, read the message from the owner’s position. Is tomorrow’s preparation decision still visible, or has the pitch drifted back to celebrating the software? Keep a statistic when it helps evaluate the proposal, and remove it when it merely makes the slide look authoritative.

Match evidence to the claim

Evidence does not form one universal ladder. An interview, prototype, behavior record, impact estimate, and cost model answer different questions. A prototype can establish technical feasibility without showing demand. Observed adoption can establish use without showing that the intervention caused a beneficial outcome. A causal effect can be real while the delivery model remains unaffordable. The task is therefore to identify the pivotal claim for the audience’s decision and match it to evidence capable of testing that claim.

Not every message needs all six claim types. A policy recommendation may hinge on need, causal impact, distribution, and cost; an early prototype may appropriately make only a feasibility claim. For the Reasons row of STORY, record the pivotal claim, its type, the strongest available evidence, the relevant source or comparison, and what remains unestablished. If the available evidence cannot support the necessary claim, weaken the claim, change the requested action, or collect better evidence.

Fluency: power and danger

A clear layout and familiar language make the pitch easier to inspect. Repetition can also make a claim feel truer through familiarity (Hasher et al., 1977; Fazio et al., 2015), so ease of reading must not become the reason to believe it. Chapter 13 develops this distinction.

For the restaurant owner, show waste in units they can reconstruct—portions or ingredient costs over a stated period—and identify the comparison. Resolve the question that earned attention rather than using suspense to delay an inconvenient limitation.

The story-evidence braid

Return to the owner preparing food for tomorrow. The scene makes a cost understandable, but only data can show how common that cost is or whether the proposed tool reduces it. Pairing a case with an appropriate denominator and comparison helps keep its emotional force in proportion (Small et al., 2007; Slovic, 2007; Dahlstrom, 2014).

A useful draft can move from the scene to evidence, explain the proposed mechanism, and then return to the decision. If there are no pilot results yet, say so and request a trial. If there are results, show who participated, what was compared, and what remains uncertain. The ending should reflect the evidence available, even when that makes the story less tidy.

Adapt the message without changing its discipline

STORY travels across teaching, leadership, health, policy, entrepreneurship, and negotiation because all involve a person or group deciding whether to revise a model. The surface changes; the discipline does not. Start from the stakeholder’s consequential decision, make the trouble specific, state the proposed mechanism, match reasons to claims, and end with an action that can be refused.

In teaching, a case should create the need for a concept and then yield to analysis. In leadership, a change story should acknowledge loss and protect valued identity rather than invent a “burning platform.” In health and policy, a patient or citizen story should be paired with prevalence, effects, alternatives, and uncertainty. In entrepreneurship, the stakeholder—not the product or founder—is usually the protagonist. In negotiation, a short story can explain why an interest matters without pretending that one side’s account is the whole situation.

The message fails when adaptation becomes a new catalogue of tricks. Keep the same questions: What model is being updated? What evidence warrants that update? What action follows? What could the audience still reasonably reject?

Ethics and common failure modes

Ethical storytelling

A story inevitably selects details. Check whether that selection hides an affected group, makes a rare event seem common, or turns one person’s success into a claim about what everyone can achieve. The ethical audit below is useful after drafting, when these omissions can be located in actual sentences.

Common mistakes

  • Speaker-first opening: beginning with the organization, product, or research team before establishing the audience’s decision. Start with shared reality and a consequential stake.
  • Decorative trouble: adding a dramatic but that changes the subject without exposing why the current model fails. The trouble must make the proposed explanation necessary to consider.
  • Anecdote as denominator: allowing one vivid case to imply frequency, representativeness, or causal force. Pair the case with the comparison or distribution needed to judge scale.
  • Evidence dump: presenting impressive numbers that do not test the pivotal claim. State what each item of evidence should change and what it cannot establish.
  • Inevitable yes: ending as though understanding obliges agreement. Name the decision, costs, alternatives, uncertainty, and a meaningful way to decline, revise, or stop.

Worked transformations

Worked example: structured hiring

Persuasive goal: convince managers to use structured hiring interviews.

Information-only version: “Structured interviews have higher validity than unstructured interviews. Comparable questions and job-relevant criteria can reduce inconsistent judgment.”

Fictional story version: Maria had hired dozens of people and believed she was good at reading candidates. In one interview, a candidate from a familiar university spoke confidently about teamwork and leadership. Maria felt an immediate connection. Another candidate was quieter, less polished, and from an institution Maria did not know well. The team hired the first candidate. Six months later, performance problems appeared: missed deadlines, poor collaboration, and resistance to feedback. During a later review, Maria reread the interview notes and realized there were almost none. She had recorded impressions, not evidence. When the company introduced structured interviews, she resisted at first. They felt mechanical. But after using the same job-relevant questions and scoring evidence separately, she noticed something uncomfortable: her “intuition” had often been confidence responding to confidence. The structure did not replace her judgment. It gave her judgment something better to work with.

This story begins inside the manager’s existing belief: “I can read people.” It introduces a problem: a confident choice is followed by poor performance. It creates a turning point: rereading the notes reveals the absence of evidence. It offers a new model: structure improves judgment rather than replacing it. The example makes the reason for structure understandable. It does not establish that confidence caused the hiring failure or that structured interviews would have prevented it; those claims require comparative research, discussed in Chapter 41.

Worked example: negotiation deadline

Persuasive goal: explain why a delivery deadline matters without making it sound like an arbitrary demand.

Information-only version: “We need delivery by June 1.”

Fictional story version: Last year, a component arrived four days late. The delay looked small on paper. But the production line had been scheduled tightly, customers had already booked installation dates, and workers were assigned to shifts that depended on the component arriving. By June 5, the line was stopped, customer orders were rescheduled, and the team had to explain a preventable delay. This year, the June 1 date is not a preference; it is the point that keeps the rest of the system from failing. We are willing to discuss price, batch size, or earlier partial delivery, but we need a plan that protects that date.

If these events were real and accurately reported, this story could explain an interest without revealing a reservation value. It humanizes the constraint and opens problem-solving rather than simply asserting a position.

Edit for clarity without confusing fluency with truth

Once the message is evidence-aligned, make it easier to process: use concrete verbs, legible comparisons, one governing tension, and a visible denominator. Familiar language can provide an entry point and one meaningful surprise can create attention. These are editing choices, not reasons to believe the claim.

Numbers, images, and metaphors should make magnitude or mechanism inspectable. Ask what each representation reveals, what it hides, and whether the audience could reconstruct the underlying quantity. A memorable metaphor may help organize a claim, but its average persuasive advantage is small and context-dependent (Sopory & Dillard, 2002).

A message worth sharing is not necessarily worth believing. People may pass content on because it expresses identity, creates connection, signals competence, helps someone, or supports collective action (Berger & Milkman, 2012; Falk et al., 2013; Baek et al., 2017). Optimize first for accuracy and inspectability, then for transmission.

CautionEvidence Boundary

STORY and ABT are practical writing tools, not validated psychological laws. A memorable structure cannot rescue false evidence, a hidden conflict of interest, an unrepresentative case, or a recommendation that removes meaningful choice. Fluency, emotional response, agreement, and sharing are message outcomes—not proof that the model is correct.

Practice Lab

Build a one-page STORY brief for one research claim, policy, product, leadership request, or negotiation proposal. The single brief contains five rows: Shared reality, Trouble, Opportunity, Reasons, and Yes. In the Reasons row, identify the pivotal claim, classify it using Table 32.2, give the strongest available evidence and comparison, state the uncertainty, and name what the evidence does not establish. At the bottom, answer three audit questions: Is any case presented as representative without a denominator? Can the audience meaningfully decline or revise the requested action? Which one sentence should be weakened or removed if the evidence is insufficient? Use the completed brief to deliver a 90-second message. Peer feedback identifies one unsupported inferential jump and one concrete repair.

Take it forward

Read your draft from the audience’s position. Can a reader identify the problem, inspect the evidence behind the pivotal claim, and understand the next decision? If the story works only while a qualification is hidden, revise the story before polishing the language.

The message is now ready to leave the page, but meaning will not travel intact. Chapter 33 follows what happens when another person interprets, tests, and corrects it.
