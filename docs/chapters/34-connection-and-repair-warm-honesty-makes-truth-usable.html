34  Connection and Repair

Warm Honesty Makes Truth Usable

“Listening is where love begins: listening to ourselves and then to our neighbors.”

— Fred Rogers, The World According to Mister Rogers, 2003, p. 93

A project manager receives a colleague’s section after the client deadline and writes, “This is unacceptable. We need to discuss your reliability.” The colleague replies, “You never told me the client changed the date.”

The manager checks the email thread. The new date was discussed with the client, but the colleague was never copied in. There is still a delivery problem to solve, and now there is an unfair accusation to repair. Explaining the original intention will not do both jobs.

We will follow this fictional exchange through listening, a more accurate account of what happened, and a response that accepts responsibility without losing sight of the work.

NoteCore Idea

A difficult conversation can involve a practical problem, hurt feelings, and a threat to someone’s standing at the same time. Listen for what needs attention, describe the behavior and its impact clearly, and ask before inferring motive. When harm has occurred, repair requires responsibility and a credible change in what happens next.

Learning goals

  • Explain how relationship quality and expectations shape the decision to connect.
  • Diagnose whether a difficult conversation calls for practical help, emotional recognition, or identity repair.
  • Use follow-up questions, visible listening, appreciation, and shared activities to build connection.
  • Produce a warm-honesty opening that separates observable behavior, experienced impact, inferred intent, and a testable request.
  • Complete a repair sequence that acknowledges harm, accepts responsibility, and specifies changed behavior.

Why relationship quality matters

Social connection includes both having supportive relationships and experiencing others as responsive, caring, and meaningful in one’s life. These ties give people somewhere to take distress, ask for help, exchange information, and develop ideas. Choosing solitude can also be restorative; it differs from feeling isolated or lacking wanted support. Chapter 22 distinguishes these experiences. The importance of connection extends beyond a pleasant conversation: a meta-analysis of 148 studies involving 308,849 participants found that stronger social relationships were associated with 50% greater odds of survival over the studies’ follow-up periods (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010). This association does not establish the health effect of a particular conversational practice. It places relationship quality among the consequential outcomes of how we organize our lives.

Supportive and ambivalent relationships

A supportive colleague can disagree while remaining dependable. An ambivalent relationship combines positive and negative experiences: the person who helps you through a crisis also belittles you in public, or offers encouragement that can unexpectedly become criticism. The helpful side makes the relationship valuable and hard to leave; the harmful side makes the next encounter difficult to anticipate.

Holt-Lunstad and colleagues (2007) studied 107 adults interacting with a supportive or ambivalent friend. Discussing a negative event with an ambivalent friend was associated with greater systolic blood-pressure reactivity. Counting contacts therefore misses something that matters: whether contact offers reliable support or requires vigilance. For the manager in our case, a friendly invitation after an unfair accusation may still leave the colleague unsure which response to expect. Consistent conduct and honored boundaries make the invitation more credible.

Social pain and the stakes of a conversation

Social pain is the distress associated with rejection, exclusion, humiliation, or threatened belonging. Being left off an email can raise a practical information problem and a personal question: “Am I still part of this team?” Eisenberger and colleagues (2003) found that exclusion during a virtual ball-tossing game recruited brain regions also implicated in the distress of physical pain. Overlap does not make the experiences identical, but the finding helps explain why social injury deserves attention.

In the project exchange, the colleague must defend both a factual account and a reputation for competence. A reply that addresses only the timetable leaves that second concern untouched. To choose a useful response, first establish what is at stake for this person.

1. Understand accurately

Diagnose the conversation before answering it

The manager still needs a delivery plan, while the colleague needs the accusation about reliability corrected. Moving straight to scheduling may look efficient to the manager and dismissive to the colleague; discussing only the hurt leaves the work unresolved. The first task is to identify what kind of response the conversation needs at this moment. The same sentence can carry at least three kinds of need. A practical conversation asks what to do. An emotional conversation asks for recognition, regulation, or company. A social or identity conversation asks what the event says about respect, belonging, competence, fairness, or the relationship (Duhigg, 2024; Stone et al., 2010). The three layers in Table 34.1 connect possible needs to opening questions and the mismatches to avoid. Advice fails when the speaker is asking to be understood; validation frustrates when an urgent plan is needed.

The same sentence in Figure 34.1 permits all three readings, so ask what kind of help is useful now rather than choosing a response from the words alone. A conversation may move between layers as the answer changes.

NoteWatch for the mismatch

Predict first

It’s Not About the Nail is a comic illustration of this mismatch. Before watching, predict what kind of help each person thinks the conversation requires. While watching, note the sentence where practical problem-solving and emotional recognition diverge. Afterward, write a response that both validates and asks permission before solving.

The manager cannot identify the colleague’s immediate need by deciding privately which conversation to have. A question gives the colleague a voice in that decision. Research on ordinary conversations suggests another benefit: Huang and colleagues (2017) found that people who ask more questions, especially follow-up questions, are better liked by their partners. Building on an answer can show that it received attention.

Use the answer to decide what to ask next. The ladder in Table 34.2 moves from what happened to why it mattered:

Depth does not mean forcing intimacy. It means allowing the conversation to move beyond labels into experience, meaning, emotion, values, needs, or identity when appropriate. Follow the last answer rather than reciting a prepared list. Ask for specificity, label emotion tentatively, invite correction, and move only one level deeper at a time.

In the project exchange, “What happened?” opens the sequence of events; “What did my message imply to you?” opens its meaning. The second question is useful only if the manager is prepared to hear an answer that changes the account.

Kardas and colleagues (2022) found that people overestimated the awkwardness of conversations and underestimated the connection they would feel, especially when discussing deeper topics. Participants also reported wanting more intimacy than their typical conversations with strangers provided. Underestimating the other person’s interest helped keep conversations shallow. Choosing small talk can therefore reflect a pessimistic forecast about one’s partner rather than a preference for distance. Preface a personal question with control: “May I ask something a little more personal? It is completely fine to pass.” A deep question is an invitation, not a demand.

Closeness depends on more than question depth. In a structured experiment, gradually escalating reciprocal self-disclosure produced greater immediate feelings of closeness between strangers than comparable small-talk tasks (Aron et al., 1997). In ongoing relationships, disclosure was more closely connected to intimacy when the partner was experienced as understanding, validating, and caring (Laurenceau et al., 1998). The lesson is not to reveal as much as possible. It is to disclose at an appropriate pace, notice whether the other person reciprocates voluntarily, and respond to what was entrusted rather than treating vulnerability as a technique.

Find depth in an ordinary opening

An ordinary question can open a meaningful conversation when the follow-up follows the answer. “What do you do?” may produce a job title; “What part of the work matters most to you?” invites a value or experience. Table 34.3 offers openings across familiar topics. Choose one that fits the setting, then let the answer determine the next turn.

For example, a fictional interview might move from “Why did you become a teacher?” to “Who encouraged you?” The answer may reveal a relationship that mattered more than the career itself. If the speaker mentions a painful separation, continuing with increasingly personal questions may feel intrusive. Ask whether they want to say more, or return to how that person influenced their teaching. Curiosity needs room for the speaker to choose the boundary.

Test the social forecast

The obstacle to connection can arise before a conversation begins. Epley and Schroeder (2014) randomly assigned commuters to connect with a stranger, remain disconnected, or commute as usual. People expected more enjoyment from solitude, but participants assigned to connect reported a more positive experience, without the predicted loss of productivity. Boothby and colleagues (2018) documented a related liking gap: after conversations, people tended to underestimate how much their partners liked them. Anxiety is therefore a forecast, not a verdict. When the situation is appropriate, test a small prediction—say hello, ask one genuine follow-up question, or invite later contact—and compare the anticipated with the experienced result.

Avoidance can preserve the original prediction: a conversation that never happens supplies no evidence about how it would have gone. When contact is safe and welcome, do not let an untested pessimistic forecast make the decision by itself. Leave the other person an easy way to decline.

Follow a point the other person has made rather than searching for the next question on a list. If the colleague says, “I felt you had already decided I was unreliable,” ask what in the message conveyed that verdict.

Put the partner at the center of attention

Partner-centered attention means deliberately tracking the other person’s goals, interpretations, constraints, and dignity while still pursuing your own purpose. Egocentrism makes our own goal and discomfort more accessible, so a behavior intended as efficient may land as dismissive or unsafe. When context is uncertain, state the observation, offer a plausible benefit of the doubt, and ask before attributing motive: “The draft arrived after review time. I may be missing a constraint—what happened from your side?” The question allows the colleague to correct the account before a judgment about character hardens (Epley et al., 2004; Laurenceau et al., 1998).

Benefit of the doubt is a starting hypothesis, not a requirement to ignore repeated harm. Where conduct is unsafe, the appropriate move may be a boundary, documentation, representation, or a formal channel.

Replace mind-reading with verified understanding

Use the ask–listen–reflect–verify routine to give the other person an account they can recognize or correct. Its relational value comes from showing what you have understood: “You thought the original date still applied because the client’s message never reached you.” That response gives the colleague something to check and shows that the explanation affected your judgment. Nods, eye contact, generic agreement, and silence are too ambiguous to do this on their own (Laurenceau et al., 1998).

Verification may take several turns. Suppose the manager paraphrases, “You were upset because the date changed.” The colleague corrects this: “Changes happen. I was upset that you questioned my reliability before checking whether I knew.” A useful next response incorporates the correction: “The accusation, rather than the new date, is what needs repairing.” Continue until the speaker recognizes the account; agreement about the explanation or solution can be considered afterward.

Make listening visible

Attention fluctuates, and private concentration is hard for a partner to observe. Eye contact, nodding, generic advice, or “exactly” can accompany either careful listening or a rehearsed response. Silence can give space or leave someone wondering whether they were heard. The short backchannels that keep an exchange moving are useful, but the moves in Table 34.5 show more specifically what the listener has processed.

The last move matters because pretending to follow conceals a repairable gap. Put down the competing task, name where you lost the thread, and return attention to the answer. Likewise, enthusiasm about good news should engage the speaker’s experience rather than immediately become a story about your own success.

Listen for the layer beneath the words

The manager sees a late section and wants to prevent another missed deadline. The colleague hears a judgment about reliability and wants the accusation corrected. Treating either account as the whole problem would leave the other concern unanswered. Start by separating the event, your own felt impact, and your interpretation. Table 34.6 is a self-check before listening: it helps identify which parts of your account the other person needs an opportunity to correct.

In the project case, the event is a late section, the manager’s feeling is exposure to the client, and the first interpretation is unreliability. The colleague’s concern is being judged for information they never received. Separating these elements makes two corrections possible: revise the attribution and repair the handoff process.

Choose the prompts that expose an uncertainty in your account, rather than asking the colleague to work through every category.

Building connection through validation and shared experiences

Validation shows that a partner’s meaning was noticed, understood, and incorporated. It may be as small as a precise “That makes sense because…” or “I see why that mattered.” It does not require endorsing every conclusion or action. Repeated, diagnostic signals—paraphrase, follow-up, acknowledgment, and specific appreciation—help build shared reality (Laurenceau et al., 1998; Rossignac-Milon et al., 2021).

Shared reality concerns a felt commonality in how people understand an event or what it means to them. Matching biographies is insufficient: two people can hold the same job and experience it very differently. Conversely, people with different backgrounds can discover that a piece of music, a joke, a shared ritual, or a difficult project matters to them in a similar way. Such recognition can create a sense of belonging and confidence in one’s interpretation (Rossignac-Milon et al., 2021). Shared confidence still needs checking against evidence, as Chapter 33 explains.

Validation establishes that the partner’s experience has a place in the conversation. Self-expansion adds a different possibility: a relationship can broaden the perspectives, skills, roles, and activities available to each person. Learning a new skill together or tackling an unfamiliar problem gives partners something new to discover about themselves and one another. Aron and colleagues (2000) found that couples assigned a brief novel and arousing joint activity reported larger immediate improvements in relationship quality than those assigned a mundane activity. This supports a benefit from shared novelty in that setting; it does not establish lasting change from one activity.

Once the colleague’s experience has been acknowledged, redesigning the handoff can become a shared learning task. The manager discovers how the colleague organizes work, the colleague gains influence over the process, and both acquire a way to handle future changes. Shared activity builds on repair when each person’s contribution matters.

Express appreciation and receive help

People often underestimate the positive impact of gratitude, compliments, and small acts of kindness, and overestimate how awkward appreciation will feel (Kumar & Epley, 2018, 2023; Zhao & Epley, 2021). Searching for perfect wording can postpone a message whose warmth and specificity already make it worth receiving. “Thank you for staying after the meeting to explain the data; it helped me catch a mistake before sending the report” identifies both the action and its effect. “You’re amazing” offers less information about what was valued.

Asking for help also gives the other person an opportunity to contribute, yet people can underestimate willingness to help and the positive experience of helping (Bohns, 2016; Zhao & Epley, 2022). A useful request names the task, explains why this person’s contribution fits, and makes refusal safe: “You understand this client’s reporting needs. Could you spend ten minutes checking the handoff plan? If this week is full, I can ask someone else.”

Receive the help without dismissing its value or turning it immediately into a debt: “That clarified the missing step—thank you.” Then close the loop: “Your suggestion caught the scheduling conflict before it reached the client.” The full sequence is specific request, reason for asking this person, room to decline, acknowledgment, and a later account of what changed.

When asking for help can build liking

The Ben Franklin effect names the possibility that doing someone a favor increases liking for that person. It is often explained through dissonance reduction or self-perception, but the request can also communicate a wish for connection.

Niiya (2016) tested a direct request for help with a puzzle in Japanese and American student samples. Liking increased relative to baseline when the recipient personally asked for help, but not when participants helped at the experimenter’s instruction. Participants already had the ability and time to help, so this was a favorable setting for a modest request. The study suggests that the interpersonal meaning of asking matters.

For the colleagues repairing a difficult handoff, a sincere request for advice may acknowledge competence and invite cooperation. It should follow acknowledgment of the problem, preserve a comfortable refusal, and serve a real need. Chapter 30 separates this relational possibility from attempts to secure escalating compliance.

2. Speak with warm honesty

Truth, vulnerability, and feedback

After an unfair accusation, it can be tempting to protect the relationship by avoiding another uncomfortable subject. Yet the delivery problem remains, and silence gives neither person the information needed to prevent a repeat. The challenge is to make truth usable without turning it into another attack.

Speaking honestly also requires deciding what private information the conversation needs. Relevant disclosure can help another person understand a concern, but disclosure for its own sake can burden the listener. Research on secrecy shows why keeping information private can be costly too: secrets can create psychological burden and isolation. Slepian and colleagues have shown that the burden of secrecy often comes not only from hiding in social interaction but from mind-wandering back to the secret (Slepian et al., 2017). Sharing a secret with a trusted person can sometimes reduce isolation and increase connection, though disclosure must be safe and appropriate.

Honesty is complicated because people often use kindness as a reason to avoid truth. White lies can protect feelings, but they can also be paternalistic or prevent learning. People often underestimate others’ desire for honest feedback and overpredict how badly honest conversations will go (Levine & Cohen, 2018; Levine et al., 2020). In professional contexts, avoiding necessary feedback may feel kind in the moment but unfair in the long run.

The alternatives in Table 34.7 show how attempts to protect a relationship can withhold useful information or deliver it in a damaging form.

Useful feedback identifies behavior the recipient can examine and change. Instead of “You are unreliable,” the manager can begin, “The section arrived after the client deadline; I had not checked whether you received the revised date.” This makes the event discussable without turning it into a character judgment.

A specific observation can still contain an inference. “You interrupted Anna twice” is easier to verify than “You made her feel unimportant.” If the impact on Anna matters, ask her rather than presenting your interpretation as her experience.

Admitting uncertainty or a mistake can make this exchange more credible when it is relevant and followed by action. Disclosure should serve the conversation, not make the other person responsible for reassuring the speaker.

Conflict is a cycle across three realities

Bradford and Robin (2021) distinguish three realities in an interaction: the actor’s intent, observable behavior, and the recipient’s experienced impact. Table 34.8 shows who has direct access to each. The distinction explains how both people can describe the same exchange sincerely while talking about different parts of it.

The recipient’s response then becomes the next action in the cycle shown in Figure 34.2. Frustration may produce a sharp reply; the first actor hears disrespect and responds defensively. Each person knows their own reason for reacting, while seeing only the other’s behavior. Repair starts by making these missing parts discussable (Bradford & Robin, 2021; Stone et al., 2010).

Stay on your side of the net during conflict

Imagine a tennis net separating your knowledge of an interaction from the other person’s private experience. Stay on your side of the net by describing what you observed and how it affected you. Ask about the other person’s motives instead of announcing them. “You changed the agenda because you do not respect my time” crosses the net: it treats a possible explanation as known intent. “I feel that you do not respect me” makes the same attribution despite beginning with “I feel.”

A useful opening follows five moves: care, observation, impact, inquiry, and request. For example: “I want us to prepare well together. The agenda changed after I had prepared the original items. I felt frustrated because I lost preparation time. What was happening on your side? Could we agree on changes by noon, or flag an urgent exception?” The statements and questions in Table 34.9 keep the two kinds of knowledge distinct.

This boundary works in both directions. The person who changed the agenda can explain their intention but must ask how the change affected the colleague. Good intentions do not cancel an experienced cost, and a painful impact does not by itself establish a hostile intention. Staying on your side still permits a firm limit: “I can prepare the new item for tomorrow; I cannot present it accurately in ten minutes.”

In the deadline case, the manager could have opened: “The section arrived after the client deadline, and I felt exposed because I had promised delivery. I may be missing information—when did you hear about the revised date?” That question would have exposed the missing message before the judgment about reliability. A request for earlier warning cannot repair a date the colleague was never given.

Address the pinch before it becomes a crunch

The practice metaphor pinch → story → avoidance → crunch describes one possible escalation pattern. A small disappointment becomes a private explanation of the other person’s character. Avoiding a conversation prevents correction, while later incidents are read through the same story.

Suppose an agenda changes without notice. The initial pinch is wasted preparation. The story is “My contribution does not matter.” The colleague says nothing, withdraws from planning, and eventually erupts when asked why they seem disengaged. Raising the first observable event gives both people a chance to discover a constraint or renegotiate an expectation: “When the agenda changed, I felt unprepared. How should we handle a late client request next time?”

Early discussion is useful when the other person can respond constructively. Where there is intimidation or a strong power imbalance, the next step may instead be documentation, a representative, or a formal channel. The aim is to make the problem addressable without requiring the person affected to absorb further harm.

3. Repair meaning and relationship

Figure 34.3 brings the conversation back to three tasks: describe the event, check what it meant, and agree on the next action. Each needs an answer from both people.

Support without trapping attention

Repeated discussion can provide closeness while keeping attention fixed on a problem. Rose (2002) called extensive revisiting of problems and negative feelings co-rumination; in a questionnaire study of children and adolescents, it was associated with both closer friendships and symptoms of anxiety or depression. For an everyday conversation, the practical question is whether talking is producing understanding, perspective, or a useful next step.

Ask what kind of support is wanted: listening, problem-solving, distraction, or company. First make the feeling intelligible; then, with permission, ask what else might be true, what remains controllable, or how the event may look from a more distant standpoint (Kross & Ayduk, 2011). Support that rushes to optimism feels dismissive. Support that never releases attention from the injury can maintain distress.

Constructive disagreement

The manager and colleague may agree that the accusation was unfair while still disagreeing about how the next handoff should work. Suppressing that disagreement could preserve a calm meeting and an unreliable process. Pursuing it as another contest over competence could undo the repair. They need a way to test competing accounts while keeping each other able to contribute.

Disagreement becomes destructive when people feel unheard, disrespected, or existentially isolated: “How can you not see what is obvious?” When people disagree about seemingly self-evident truths, they may feel that the other person inhabits a different reality. That feeling can push people toward groups that validate them and away from dialogue.

Civility matters because contempt closes minds and damages the conditions for thought. Civility makes room for firm disagreement and careful comparison of evidence.

Expectations can discourage even a manageable disagreement. Across three experiments with U.S. adults, Wald and colleagues (2024) found that people underestimated how positively conversations about potentially divisive topics would unfold, especially with someone who disagreed. Participants underestimated the common ground that could emerge. The finding gives a reason to test a pessimistic forecast in a suitable setting, while the practices here help keep that test constructive.

Moral reframing is also useful. A review by Feinberg and Willer (2019) describes evidence that political arguments can be more persuasive when framed in terms of the audience’s moral values rather than only the speaker’s. If someone values loyalty, authority, liberty, care, fairness, or purity differently than you do, repeating only your own moral language may fail. Translation is not manipulation when it remains truthful; it is respect for the listener’s moral world.

Personal experience can also preserve respect across moral disagreement. Kubin and colleagues (2021) found that experiences of harm were judged as more rational and increased respect more than factual claims in several political disagreements. Such stories help make the personal stakes understandable.

Before answering an argument, reflect it in terms the other person recognizes. In the project case: “You want the correction recorded because the original accusation could affect how others judge your work.” This identifies a concern that another explanation of the deadline would miss.

Then examine the evidence together and state what remains in disagreement. Respect makes the discussion possible; it does not require equal weight for claims with unequal support.

Apology and repair

Explaining “I only wanted to protect the deadline” leaves the colleague’s damaged standing untouched. The manager’s intention and the harm require different responses: the colleague needs the false accusation corrected and a reason to expect a different process next time. An apology should identify the harm, accept appropriate responsibility, and make a credible offer of repair. Studies of apology components find that responsibility and repair can be particularly influential in how apologies are evaluated (Fehr & Gelfand, 2010; Lewicki et al., 2016).

“I’m sorry if you were offended” leaves the disputed action and responsibility unclear. The manager’s apology can instead identify the actual mistake: “I called your reliability into question before checking whether I had sent you the revised date. I had not, and the accusation was unfair. I am sorry. I will correct that account with the client team, and from now on we will record deadline changes in the shared plan and confirm who has received them.”

The colleague may still want time, an explanation, or a stronger safeguard. Forgiveness and reconciliation are separate decisions; an apology does not entitle the speaker to either. What can be checked is the promised correction and whether the next deadline change reaches the people responsible for it.

NoteOptional research note: forgiveness without surrendering judgment

Suppose the manager corrects the accusation, but the colleague still replays it after work. Whether to forgive now concerns how the colleague wants to relate to that hurt. In Worthington’s (2006) account, emotional forgiveness involves a movement away from resentment and ill will toward more benevolent feelings. It can develop gradually, even after someone has decided not to retaliate.

Forgiveness does not establish that the accusation was acceptable or that the manager has become reliable. Reconciliation concerns rebuilding the relationship; trust concerns what the colleague is prepared to rely on in future. The colleague might choose to forgive while insisting on written deadline changes and waiting for a record of fair conduct before trusting again. The manager can offer repair, but cannot demand an internal change from the person harmed.

Worthington’s REACH approach offers a structure for someone who wants to work toward forgiveness: recall the hurt; try to empathize with the other person’s perspective without excusing the act; consider forgiveness as an altruistic gift, freely chosen; commit to the forgiveness one has reached; and hold on to that commitment when painful memories return. These prompts provide a way to revisit the experience, without requiring emotions to change on command.

What does the evidence show? Toussaint et al. (2015) collect research and theory on forgiveness and health. More directly, a randomized trial involving 4,598 adults found that a self-directed REACH workbook reduced self-reported unforgiveness and, to a smaller extent, depression and anxiety symptoms at two weeks relative to a waitlist (Ho et al., 2024). The workbook produced short-term benefits.1 The colleague’s decision about reconciliation depends on what the manager does next.

Connection as decision infrastructure

At the next project meeting, the important difference is observable: the deadline is written in a shared place, the colleague can raise a concern before committing, and the manager corrects a mistaken account instead of defending it. Trust, if it improves, will have something to rest on.

Connection can help information reach a decision, but friendliness alone is insufficient. People also need accurate records, clear responsibilities, and ways to challenge a claim without depending on the goodwill of one person.

When apparent agreement could reflect authority, suppressed dissent, unshared information, pluralistic ignorance, or diffused responsibility, use the mechanism-specific diagnosis in Authority, Groupthink, and Shared Responsibility.

These practices become especially consequential in negotiation. Parties need enough confidence in the conversation to disclose a priority or correct an assumption, while retaining the freedom to reject an unsuitable deal. The next part asks how that information can produce agreements worth making.

Practice Lab

Lab 1: Predict and experience a conversation — 10 minutes

Work with a partner. During the first minute, privately predict enjoyment, awkwardness, and connection on scales from 0 to 10. Choose a topic you are comfortable discussing: a choice you are proud of, a challenge you faced, a moment of support or feeling understood, an experience that changed how you see yourself, or a time you felt deeply moved or close to tears. Either person can pass or choose a less personal topic.

Give each person four minutes as the main speaker. The listener follows the answer with genuine questions; then exchange roles. Use the final minute to rate actual enjoyment, awkwardness, and connection on the same scales. Compare forecasts with experience, keeping personal disclosures private.

Debrief: Which prediction was furthest off? What did the partner do that affected the experience? If the conversation went badly, what boundary, topic, or behavior mattered? Record what this encounter suggests changing in your next forecast. A class can plot anonymous average predicted and experienced ratings, using its own observations rather than assumed results.

Lab 2: Practice depth and responsiveness — 14 minutes

Take two seven-minute turns, exchanging speaker and listener roles. Choose a new partner if possible. In the first three minutes of each turn, converse naturally about a meaningful but safe topic. In the next four minutes, ask permission to go one level deeper and ask two or three follow-up questions about meaning or emotion. Paraphrase the account, invite a correction, and revise until the speaker recognizes what mattered. The speaker identifies one response that made them feel understood and one that could improve.

Before and after the exercise, rate enjoyment, awkwardness, and connection from 0 to 10. Debrief which question changed the depth, which response showed that the answer was heard, and when curiosity felt welcome or intrusive. Compare experiences with Lab 1 without treating the difference as an experimental effect: partners, topics, practice, and order may all differ.

Lab 3: Warm honesty and repair — 15 minutes

Form groups of three: speaker, partner, and observer. Use the fictional late-project case or a low-stakes disagreement. Rotate roles every five minutes so everyone practices each role. In each round, allow three minutes for the exchange and two for feedback.

The speaker opens with care, an observable event, its impact, a question, and a concrete request. The partner paraphrases what they heard, corrects what needs correcting, and offers their account, including a detail the speaker did not know. They may accept, revise, or decline the request. The speaker reflects that account and incorporates any correction. If responsibility becomes clear, finish with an apology and a credible repair. Produce a short corrected account, a revised opening, and a repair statement specifying what changes next.

The observer uses Table 34.10 to record actual words, then offers one specific improvement.

Debrief what reduced defensiveness, where a judgment disguised itself as an observation, and whether the proposed change addressed both the practical and relational problem. Identify how the opening would need to change if one person controlled the other’s grade or employment. Participants can stop or switch to the fictional case at any time; no one needs to disclose a real conflict or accept a repair they do not find adequate.

Take it forward

The next conversation need not resolve the whole relationship. Start with one specific moment, explain its effect without claiming access to the other person’s motives, and make room for their account. What happens afterward will matter more than a perfectly worded apology.
