39  Behavior Design

Make the Better Action Easier

“For a target behavior to happen, a person must have sufficient motivation, sufficient ability, and an effective trigger.”

— B. J. Fogg, “A Behavior Model for Persuasive Design”, 2009, p. 2

A student intends to study before opening email, but the laptop opens onto the inbox each morning. Replies offer small tasks with clear endings; the problem set offers a difficult task whose starting point is still vague. By the time the student opens it, much of the morning has gone.

One possible change is to leave the problem set open the evening before, with the first question marked. The next morning begins with a visible action instead of a decision about where to start. That could help if the vague starting point was the main obstacle. If the student lacks course materials needed to solve it, the same change will accomplish little.

The useful change depends on why the intention fails. Behavior design starts with that explanation, then changes a specific condition and tests whether the intended action becomes easier to carry out.

NoteCore Idea

Begin with an action someone wants to take and a specific occasion when it fails to happen. Find what makes starting or continuing difficult, change that condition, and observe the result. A useful design also explains how to resume after a lapse and when to reconsider the goal or seek more support.

Learning goals

  • Apply one five-step workflow: define the behavior, diagnose the bottleneck, redesign the decisive point, measure, and review.
  • Match a practical change to the obstacle it addresses.
  • Distinguish follow-through from the outcome it is meant to improve, and plan how to resume after a lapse.

The habit chapter explained how familiar cues can retrieve an action, and why wanting and enjoyment can diverge (Wood & Rünger, 2016). This chapter turns that understanding into a plan for change, whether the aim is to alter an established routine or begin something new.

From intention to a testable change

“Study more” gives the student a direction, but it does not specify what should happen differently tomorrow. “Attempt the marked problem before opening email” identifies an action, a competing response, and an occasion to observe. Now the student can ask why email comes first and choose a change that addresses that obstacle.

Table 39.1 turns the figure into a short record. Write down the prediction before trying the change, so that the result can challenge the explanation that seemed plausible at the start.

For the student, the prediction might be: leaving a marked problem open will make studying more likely to begin before email. Starting is the first thing to observe; whether the student learns more is a separate question to return to when evaluating the change.

Diagnose the moment with B=MAP

The student wants to study, yet willingness alone has not settled what happens when the laptop opens. The Fogg Behavior Model, B=MAP, describes behavior as occurring when motivation, ability, and a prompt converge (Fogg, 2009, 2020). Here ability means how easy the action is to perform at that moment; a prompt calls for it to happen.

This distinction gives the student three questions to answer. Is studying worth doing now? Is the first task feasible with the time and materials available? What will bring it to attention before the inbox does? A reminder addresses the third question, but cannot supply materials missing from the second. Calling both problems “low motivation” would obscure the difference.

When motivation fluctuates, preparing an easier first action and a timely prompt may offer a more workable plan than expecting the same enthusiasm every morning.

Look for mornings when studying already begins before email. Such bright spots can suggest conditions worth reproducing and testing, such as a clearer first task or a quieter setting (Heath & Heath, 2010).

Make the first action small

The inbox offers an easy response with a quick sense of completion. To give studying a feasible starting point, the student could attempt the first part of the marked problem. The small action provides an entry into the work; its value depends on what follows, including whether the student continues and learns.

Fogg’s practical recipe connects that small start to an existing routine: After I [reliable anchor], I will [tiny behavior]. Then I will [authentically acknowledge completion]. The anchor supplies a prompt, the small action reduces the effort needed to begin, and acknowledgment marks progress. For the student, the anchor might be sitting down with breakfast finished, and the action might be writing the first line of a solution.

An inspiring class or approaching deadline may make an ambitious plan feel easy. Use that motivation to prepare for a tired Wednesday: obtain the materials, mark the next question, and arrange a workable time. The arrangement still has to be tried when the initial enthusiasm fades.

Implementation intentions

A feasible action can still be forgotten at the moment it matters. Implementation intentions connect an anticipated situation to a specified response: If this happens, then I will do that. The plan helps bring a goal to mind and guide action when the situation occurs (Gollwitzer, 1999).

The student can turn “I will study more” into “If I finish breakfast on a weekday, then I will sit at my desk and attempt the marked problem before opening email.” Twenty-five minutes of study might remain the session goal, while attempting the marked problem specifies how to begin. The same structure works beyond study: a manager might plan to ask one clarifying question when receiving criticism, or a saver to transfer 10 percent of each salary payment immediately. Each plan identifies an occasion and an action concrete enough to carry out.

In a workplace vaccination study, employees received reminders about an available flu clinic. A reminder asking them to write the date and time of their planned visit increased vaccination compared with one without that planning field (Milkman et al., 2011). The extra step connected the intention to a concrete opportunity to act.

Sometimes the difficulty is choosing a realistic response to an obstacle. WOOP (Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan) adds that preparation: identify a meaningful, feasible wish, imagine the valued outcome, consider what could obstruct it, and form an if-then plan. For the student, “I want to understand the material” becomes more actionable after identifying the vague starting point that sends him toward email. Imagining success alone would leave that obstacle unchanged. Evidence on mental contrasting with implementation intentions supports modest average gains in goal attainment compared with control conditions without the combined exercise, with variation across goals and settings (Oettingen, 2012; Wang et al., 2021).

Friction, rewards, and commitment

A plan specifies what to do, but the effort and immediate appeal of competing actions still matter. Friction includes the steps, time, and effort needed to act. Leaving the problem set open reduces friction for studying; putting the phone in another room adds friction to checking it. The same principle can guide other changes, such as removing saved payment details before an impulsive purchase or simplifying a form that people repeatedly abandon.

Changing the default also changes what happens without an active choice. Chapter 40 develops this approach across a whole choice process, including its boundary conditions and ethics. Here the student can begin with the narrower question of which action the current arrangement makes easiest.

When effort is immediate and the benefit delayed, temptation bundling pairs a beneficial activity with something enjoyable now. In a field experiment, making tempting audiobooks available only at the gym initially increased visits compared with a control group given a bookstore gift certificate and encouragement to exercise (Milkman et al., 2014). A person might likewise reserve a favorite podcast for walking or special coffee for difficult reading. Choose a reward that supports the activity, and consider what happens if the routine is disrupted or the reward becomes available elsewhere.

If the problem is a predictable future temptation, precommitment can constrain the later choice (Thaler & Shefrin, 1981). App blockers, cancellation penalties, and restricted-access accounts do this in different ways, with different costs and possibilities for reversal. Assess whether the constraint supports the person’s goal and remains acceptable when circumstances change. The intertemporal-choice chapter reviews commitment evidence, including the retracted deadline study; a plausible device still needs evidence that it helps.

Design an attention boundary

For the student, beginning the marked problem will accomplish little if every new message interrupts it. In Indistractable, Eyal (2019) distinguishes traction, which serves an intended activity, from distraction, which takes us away from it. Email can support a planned correspondence period and interrupt a study period; chosen rest also deserves protected time.

Checking can begin with an external trigger, such as an alert, or an internal trigger, such as boredom or uncertainty. An attention boundary therefore needs more than silenced notifications: notice what prompts the detour, reserve realistic time for the intended activity, and adjust interrupting cues. The precommitment tools above can help protect that plan when temptation is predictable.

These changes should address an observed problem. Ward and colleagues (2017) reported lower cognitive performance when participants’ own phones were nearby, but a later meta-analysis found a narrower pattern: pooled impairment appeared for working memory, while other cognitive outcomes showed no significant effect (Parry, 2024). Phone presence and an actual notification are different conditions; Limited Attention discusses interruption. Switching away from an unfinished task also creates a distinct difficulty: part of the previous task can remain mentally active—attention residue—and reduce performance on the next task (Leroy, 2009).

Digital services can also make returning attractive. Eyal’s (2014) Hook Model describes four stages: Trigger → Action → Variable reward → Investment. A reply alert prompts the student to open a study forum, where the usefulness of new answers varies. Posting a follow-up question invests effort and may generate the next reply; saving an explanation makes a later visit more useful. Table 39.2 examines when these features support the user’s goal and when they make disengagement difficult.

Evaluate the loop against the outcome the user values. As the near-miss example in Chapter 21 shows, renewed desire to continue need not indicate pleasure or progress. More clicks, check-ins, or time spent can coexist with less learning or lower well-being; measure goal attainment and opportunity cost as well as engagement.

Make the change last

Beginning once and maintaining a routine pose different problems. The student needs a plan that can survive a busy week, a missed morning, or a change in schedule.

Use a fresh start

A new semester can make a fresh study plan feel credible. Temporal landmarks can create this psychological “fresh start” (Dai et al., 2014), but the same inbox and vague first task may still win on the first morning. Use the occasion to prepare what will be different: the study location, the first task, or the time available.

A change in circumstances can also interrupt familiar cues. As Chapter 21 explained, moving or changing a schedule can make an established response less likely to be retrieved, while leaving it available if the old cues return. Prepare a routine for the changed setting, including replacement cues for useful habits that the disruption might otherwise unsettle (Wood, 2019).

Identity can affect how people interpret that practice. Difficulty might mean “this matters to people like me” or “this proves I do not belong” (Oyserman, 2009). “I am becoming someone who begins with one problem” gives the student an action to practice without requiring every morning to go well. Treat the identity as flexible enough to accommodate rest, revision, and setbacks.

Plan for lapses

A missed morning should lead to a specific next step. The student might plan: “If I miss the study period, then I will mark the next problem that evening and begin with it after breakfast.” This connects recovery to another workable occasion, instead of leaving it dependent on a renewed promise.

Repeated lapses call for another look at the diagnosis. The task may remain too difficult, the time may be unavailable, or the competing action may serve a need the plan ignored. Review the support and goal as well as the prompt. Recovery from addiction may require clinical assessment and evidence-based treatment alongside changes to everyday routines (Volkow et al., 2016).1

CautionWillpower is not one depletable fuel

The classic ego-depletion account of willpower as a depletable resource has faced serious replication and measurement challenges (Hagger et al., 2016). Fatigue, motivation, beliefs, opportunity costs, and context offer alternative explanations of changes in self-control. Appendix E tracks the disputed evidence and its boundaries.

Changing a routine together

The student can rearrange his own desk, but an employee who wants to speak openly depends on how colleagues respond. An organizational routine involves several people, roles, tools, and rules. Deliberate incentives and formal requirements can sustain it alongside automatic responses, so changing one person’s habit may leave the larger pattern intact.

The same design workflow now has to follow an interaction: who does what, how others respond, and which part of that sequence could change.

Mini-case: the defensive meeting loop

A product team wants concerns to surface early, yet its Monday meetings repeatedly discourage them. When a senior manager questions a progress update, the project lead hears criticism and explains why the team’s choices were reasonable. Other members fall silent. The manager interprets the explanation as a refusal to take responsibility and presses harder, prompting more defense. The meeting ends with action items but little new information about the project’s risks.

The manager meant to improve the project, the lead meant to protect the team, and colleagues meant to avoid conflict. Their reactions nevertheless sustained a pattern none of them wanted. One possible explanation is that defending past choices protects the lead’s standing in the moment, while silence protects colleagues from joining the dispute. Ask participants what they understood and examine repeated meetings before settling on that diagnosis.

Agree with the team on a short, reversible trial; giving people a say and reducing uncertainty can make a change easier to try (Berger, 2020). Begin with one change: before questioning the update, the manager asks, “What would be most useful for us to challenge?” Keep the rest of the meeting reasonably stable and record whether a concrete uncertainty is raised before a defensive exchange begins. If that change helps, a later test might add the lead’s implementation intention: “If I hear criticism, I will ask which risk the question is meant to clarify.” Introducing both changes together would make their separate contributions harder to learn.

The manager’s subsequent conduct remains part of the design. As Chapter 28 emphasized, inviting a concern and punishing the answer would teach silence again. Track both whether concerns appear earlier and whether they receive useful responses, including when they challenge the manager’s own view.

Whose goals does the design serve?

The meeting example makes a further question unavoidable: who gets to decide what counts as better behavior? The student can choose a study arrangement for himself, while a manager or platform designer changes conditions for other people. More reporting or longer engagement may serve the organization without serving those asked to participate.

Ask whether affected people can understand the arrangement, question its goals, choose otherwise, and discover its costs. A streak may encourage practice while making rest feel like failure; a reporting dashboard may improve coordination while exposing staff to surveillance. These effects belong in the evaluation alongside the behavior the designer hopes to increase.

Measure what the change achieves

The student may begin before email more often without understanding the material better. A team may report more concerns without addressing any of them. Separate the action a design encourages from the outcome that makes it worth encouraging.

Before making the change, record what happens at relevant opportunities. For the student, that means noting whether study begins before email and what work gets done. During the test, continue that record and check learning as well—for example, whether the student can explain a solution without consulting it. For the team, note whether concerns receive an answer or lead to a useful change in the project.

Choose a few measures that answer distinct questions:

  • Follow-through: Did the planned action happen when the opportunity arose?
  • Outcome: Did it improve the learning, judgment, cooperation, or well-being it was meant to support?
  • Maintenance: Does the change persist after novelty or intensive support declines?
  • Costs and access: Who can use the arrangement, and what effort, trust, rest, or flexibility does it cost?

Set a review date and decide what would justify keeping, revising, or stopping the change. A personal before-and-after comparison can inform the next attempt, but other changes may explain the result. Stronger causal claims require a comparison that can separate those explanations.

Practice Lab: behavior-design studio

Choose one manageable everyday behavior you want to change and complete the record in Table 39.1:

  1. Define: write the observable target, competing action, and recurring situation.
  2. Diagnose: name the cue, the current action’s immediate function, and the main obstacle. Use B=MAP to distinguish willingness, feasibility, and the prompt.
  3. Redesign: change one decisive point. Choose no more than one primary tool from Table 39.3 and state exactly what will be different in the environment or response.
  4. Measure: record what happens before the change, predict what will improve, and choose one measure of follow-through and one of the valued outcome for a short reversible test.
  5. Review: set a date and a rule for keeping, revising, or stopping the change. Add a way to resume after a lapse and one cost or unintended effect that would make you reconsider.

Conclude with one sentence: “If this diagnosis is useful, we should observe…” Name the action or event that would support the explanation, along with one result that would send you back to the diagnosis.

Take it forward

The student’s intention becomes a plan when tomorrow morning has a clear starting point and a way to check whether the work helps him learn. Choose one recurring occasion in your own day and make the same move: identify the obstacle, prepare a response, and decide what result would lead you to revise it.

The next chapter, Choice Architecture, widens the view from a particular action to the sequence of choices through which people must pass.

NoteResearch note: the ideas behind the workflow

The workflow combines diagnosis, planning, and review. The Fogg Behavior Model describes motivation, ability, and a cue for action converging at a behavioral moment; its earlier term “trigger” became “prompt” in later presentations (Fogg, 2009, 2020). Research on implementation intentions, habits, and behavior-change maintenance supports planning, feedback, and preparation for obstacles (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006; Kwasnicka et al., 2016; Wood & Rünger, 2016).

NoteResearch Lens: COM-B widens the diagnosis

COM-B asks whether capability, opportunity, and motivation converge (Michie et al., 2011). Capability includes skill, knowledge, energy, and physical ability. Opportunity includes time, tools, permission, safety, and social context. Motivation includes reflective and automatic processes. A motivational speech cannot fix a broken opportunity structure. COM-B is useful when B=MAP’s moment-level diagnosis needs a broader account of resources and institutions.

Use the separate framework questions in Table 39.4 to identify when the barrier calls for resources, access, treatment, or organizational change. Their components overlap, but a prompt at the right moment cannot substitute for missing capability or opportunity.
