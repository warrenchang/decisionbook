39  Behavior Design

Make the Better Action Easier

“For the great doesn’t happen through impulse alone, and is a succession of little things that are brought together.”

— Vincent van Gogh, letter to Theo van Gogh, 22 October 1882, letter 274

A student intends to work on a term paper before opening email, but the laptop opens onto the inbox each morning. Replies offer small tasks with clear endings; the paper offers a vague instruction: “Work on the literature review.” By the time the student opens the draft, much of the available writing time has gone.

One possible change is to leave the draft open the evening before, beside notes on two studies and a precise next step: “Write one sentence explaining how their findings agree or differ.” The next morning begins with a visible action instead of a decision about where to start. That could help if the vague starting point was the main obstacle. If the student cannot yet interpret the studies, needs guidance on academic writing, or has no time available that morning, a clearer prompt will accomplish little.

The useful change depends on why the intention fails. Behavior design starts with that explanation, then changes a specific condition and tests whether the intended action becomes easier to carry out.

NoteCore Idea

Begin with an action someone wants to take and a specific occasion when it fails to happen. Find what makes starting or continuing difficult, change that condition, and observe the result. A useful design also explains how to resume after a lapse and when to reconsider the goal or seek more support.

Learning goals

  • Apply one five-step workflow: define the behavior, diagnose the bottleneck, redesign the decisive point, measure, and review.
  • Match a practical change to the obstacle it addresses.
  • Increase the feasibility of a desired action by reducing friction, and make an unwanted competing action harder to perform.
  • Anchor a new action to a reliable prompt and distinguish prompting, planning, and habit formation.
  • Compare immediate enjoyment, celebration, temptation bundling, commitment, skill building, and social support as ways to sustain action.
  • Distinguish follow-through from the outcome it is meant to improve, and plan how to resume after a lapse.

The habit chapter used bedtime scrolling to distinguish familiar cues, wanting, and enjoyment (Wood & Rünger, 2016). Term-paper writing is a useful transfer case because several other obstacles can produce a similar detour. Opening email may be habitual, but it can also avoid an unclear or uncomfortable task, meet a real coordination need, or fill time too short for the planned work. This chapter turns that diagnosis into a plan for change, whether the aim is to alter an established routine or begin something new.

From intention to a testable change

“Work on my paper” gives the student a direction, but it does not specify what should happen differently tomorrow. “Draft one comparison of the two selected studies before opening email” identifies an action, a competing response, and an occasion to observe. Now the student can ask why email comes first and choose a change that addresses that obstacle.

Goal setting connects a valued outcome to a standard for progress. An outcome goal might be a well-supported literature review; a behavior goal specifies writing and checking one comparison during the morning session. The familiar SMART checklist—specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound—can make the target clearer. “By the end of tomorrow’s session, draft one comparison and check it against both papers” tells the student what completion would look like.

The goal also needs to fit what the person knows how to do. Goal-setting research emphasizes commitment, feedback, and task knowledge alongside specificity and challenge (Locke & Latham, 2002). If the student cannot yet compare research designs, a useful learning goal is to identify one methodological difference and explain why it matters. A higher word target would leave that missing skill untouched. Explaining why the comparison matters can strengthen its purpose; instruction and practice address the ability to produce it.

Relying on willpower alone would mean resisting the inbox afresh whenever writing feels difficult. That can work on a particular morning, but it leaves success dependent on repeatedly winning the same conflict. A more reliable plan prepares the conditions under which the desired action starts and continues (Duckworth et al., 2016).

CautionSelf-control effort and the debate over ego depletion

The ego-depletion account pictures willpower as a fuel supply that each act of self-control uses up. Large preregistered tests have challenged that account (Hagger et al., 2016; Vohs et al., 2021). In a 36-laboratory study with 3,531 participants, the planned analysis found a near-zero effect of prior self-control effort on subsequent performance. Another multilab study found a small effect (Dang et al., 2021), but a performance cost does not establish that a fuel has been consumed. Willpower should not be treated as a fixed, depletable supply; effort and fatigue can matter without that explanation. Appendix E examines the disputed evidence.

Two tasks clarify what self-control evidence can establish. The Stroop demonstration in Chapter 8 shows interference from a practiced response. The marshmallow studies in Chapter 20 separate the effect of a reliable promise from the association between childhood waiting and later achievement. Neither task is a direct gauge of a person’s fixed supply of willpower.

Use the CIAO habit loop to organize that preparation: choose a reliable cue, anticipate the impulse that competes with the goal, make the intended action easy enough to begin, and arrange an outcome that makes progress visible. For the student, this means returning to the prepared draft after breakfast, marking an uncertainty instead of escaping into email, and completing one useful comparison. Repeating that start in a familiar setting can build a cue–action association; deliberate effort helps establish and adjust the arrangement.

For the student, the prediction might be: leaving the draft open with a specific comparison to make will make writing more likely to begin before email. Starting is the first thing to observe; whether the paragraph offers an accurate, useful comparison is a separate question to return to when evaluating the change.

Diagnose the moment with B=MAP

The student wants to advance the paper, yet willingness alone has not settled what happens when the laptop opens. The Fogg Behavior Model, B=MAP, describes behavior as occurring when motivation, ability, and a prompt converge (Fogg, 2009, 2020). Here ability means how easy the action is to perform at that moment; a prompt calls for it to happen. Read B=MAP as a framework for diagnosing an occasion for action, rather than an equation with measured quantities to multiply.

This distinction gives the student three questions to answer. Is this writing step worth doing now, or does fear of producing a poor paragraph make email more attractive? Can the student carry it out with the writing skills, understanding, materials, and time available? What will bring it to attention before the inbox does? A reminder addresses the last question, but cannot supply missing understanding or protected time.

Opportunity can also influence motivation. The smoking studies in Chapter 21 show that expecting access can strengthen craving itself. Changing access may therefore affect the impulse before action, as well as make the action easier or harder to carry out.

The framework applies to one-time actions as well as repeated behavior. A person might want to support an emergency appeal, have an easy payment method available, and encounter a request to donate now. No established donation habit is needed for that action to occur. Daily writing presents an additional problem: the conditions for starting must work across many mornings. Fogg’s Tiny Habits method addresses that recurring task by combining a small action, a reliable anchor, and a celebration of completion. B=MAP diagnoses the moment; the method helps arrange repeated opportunities to act and learn.

This distinction also matters in business. An online shop may want a customer to complete one purchase; a language-learning service needs the learner to return. Both can examine motivation, ease, and prompts. Sustained participation also depends on what the experience provides and whether it continues to be worth the person’s time. The app-design research lens considers Instagram, Clubhouse, and Duolingo, distinguishing documented connections to Fogg’s teaching from applications of his model to product features.

When motivation fluctuates, preparing an easier first action and a timely prompt may offer a more workable plan than expecting the same enthusiasm every morning. Look for mornings when writing already begins before email. Such bright spots can suggest conditions worth reproducing and testing, such as a clearer first task or a quieter setting (Heath & Heath, 2010).

The techniques below change different parts of this situation: what the person is trying to achieve, how demanding the action is, what prompts it, what makes it worth repeating, and what support and feedback are available. Start with the obstacle the observation record identifies. Several tools can eventually work together, but adding them all at once would make it difficult to learn which change helped.

Change friction in both directions

Friction is the time, effort, steps, cost, or uncertainty involved in carrying out an action. In B=MAP, reducing these demands increases ability at that moment: the action becomes more feasible with the resources the person has. This can happen without any change in intelligence, discipline, or skill. A prepared draft is easier to begin than a folder that must first be searched and organized.

Lower friction for the desired action. Put the necessary materials where they will be used, remove avoidable steps, simplify instructions, and make the first decision in advance. The student can download the two papers, mark the relevant passages, and leave a comparison question at the top of the draft. For another person, the decisive change might be an accessible tool, help with a form, transport, or a protected period of time. Inspect the whole route to action, including access to the materials it requires.

Raise friction for the competing action. Logging out of a distracting account, removing its shortcut, putting the phone in another room, or turning off autoplay adds a step before continuing. A person trying to curb impulse purchases might remove saved payment details or put an item on a list to reconsider the next day. The interruption gives the current goal a chance to influence the next move. Place the barrier at the point where the unwanted action usually begins, while preserving access needed for legitimate tasks.

These two changes can be paired: the draft is ready and the inbox is closed. Environmental restructuring also changes what is visible and nearby; defaults and automation determine what happens with little further action. The student might make the writing workspace the laptop’s startup view, while someone saving regularly might arrange a chosen transfer after payday. Automation can maintain a behavior without turning it into a personal habit. Chapter 40 examines defaults and the design of the wider choice process.

Added friction has a purpose only in relation to someone’s goal. A pause chosen to reduce impulsive spending serves a different purpose from making an unwanted subscription hard to cancel. Chapter 40’s discussion of sludge asks whose interests a barrier protects.

Make the first action small

When beginning a change, less can be more: start tiny and make success feel good. A demanding plan can turn every attempt into a test of whether enough has been done. A small, useful action provides a clear opportunity to follow through and experience progress. The student need not solve the whole literature review this morning; drafting one comparison sentence is a start worth recognizing. A brief acknowledgment of that success can make the first step encouraging, as the celebration practice below explains.

Laozi captures the value of small beginnings in the Daodejing, chapter 63: “天下难事，必作于易，天下大事，必作于细”—translated here as “Difficult undertakings begin with what is easy; great undertakings begin with what is small.” Applied to behavior change, the two halves suggest complementary choices: find a manageable part of a difficult task, and define a small, concrete action within it. “Write a literature review” becomes “write one sentence comparing these two findings.” The larger goal gives direction; the small action gives a place to begin.

Graded tasks build from a manageable action toward a more demanding one. Once the starting routine and the underlying skill are workable, the student can develop a full paragraph. Keep the first step small enough to begin on an ordinary day, and increase the challenge as it becomes manageable. As the work grows, each comparison still needs to be accurate and help answer the research question.

When the obstacle is missing know-how, use instruction, demonstration, and rehearsal. A tutor can model how to compare two studies, have the student explain a difference, and give feedback before the student tries a new pair. A checklist can support execution after the procedure is understood. These techniques build capability, while a prepared workspace reduces the demands on that capability. Both routes can make action easier (Fogg, 2009, 2020; Michie et al., 2011).

An inspiring class or approaching deadline may make an ambitious plan feel easy. Use that motivation to prepare for a tired Wednesday: obtain the studies, identify the next comparison, and arrange a workable time. The arrangement still has to be tried when the initial enthusiasm fades.

Anchor the action to a reliable prompt

An easy action can remain undone if nothing brings it to mind. An anchor is an existing event selected to prompt the new behavior. Finishing breakfast, opening the laptop for a writing session, or ending a meeting can each supply a recognizable moment. Linking a new action to an established routine is often called habit stacking.

Fogg’s practical recipe connects a small start to that event: After I [reliable anchor], I will [tiny behavior]. Then I will [celebrate]. The anchor supplies the prompt, the small action reduces the effort needed to begin, and celebration brings attention to the success. For the student, “After I open the laptop for my writing session, I will draft the selected comparison sentence” brings the response close to the moment when email usually wins. A quiet acknowledgment immediately afterward completes the recipe; the discussion of celebration below explains its purpose and timing.

A useful anchor happens reliably, is easy to recognize, and occurs where the action is feasible. Finishing breakfast is a poor anchor for desk work if the person immediately leaves to catch a bus. Choose an event that already belongs to the relevant situation, and specify what happens immediately after it. Repeating the response at that cue can gradually make the cue itself retrieve the action, as Chapter 21 explains.

Reminders and prompts can also be deliberately added: a calendar alert, a note on the keyboard, or materials placed at the point of use. Make the message actionable—“Open the prepared draft and write the first comparison”—and time it for an available opportunity. If a reminder is repeatedly dismissed, check timing, feasibility, and willingness before adding more alerts. For an unwanted behavior, remove avoidable prompts: silence nonessential notifications or move tempting material out of the usual line of sight. Internal cues such as worry may still require a prepared response.

Implementation intentions

A feasible action can still be forgotten at the moment it matters. Implementation intentions connect an anticipated situation to a specified response: If this happens, then I will do that. The plan helps bring a goal to mind and guide action when the situation occurs (Gollwitzer, 1999).

The student can turn “I will work on my paper” into “If I finish breakfast on a weekday, then I will sit at my desk and draft the selected comparison before opening email.” Twenty-five minutes of writing might remain the session goal, while drafting the comparison specifies how to begin. The same structure works beyond writing: a manager might plan to ask one clarifying question when receiving criticism, or a saver to transfer 10 percent of each salary payment immediately. Each plan identifies an occasion and an action concrete enough to carry out.

When the aim is to replace an existing habit, connect the plan to the cue that actually precedes the old response. If opening the laptop reliably leads this student into the inbox, a closer plan is “When I open the laptop for my writing session, I will type the first comparison sentence in the prepared draft.” Leaving that draft open makes the response available at the critical moment. Chapter 21 explains how to select this replacement and practice it until it starts more readily.

Milkman and colleagues (2011) randomly assigned employees to three flu-clinic reminders: basic information, information plus a field for a planned date, or fields for both date and time. Vaccination rates were 33.1% in the basic-reminder group and 37.1% in the date-and-time group—a 4.0-percentage-point difference. The covariate-adjusted estimate was 4.2 points (95% confidence interval: 0.5–7.8); the date-only prompt did not reliably outperform the basic reminder. A concrete plan helped translate an available opportunity into action.

Sometimes the difficulty is choosing a realistic response to an obstacle. WOOP (Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan) adds that preparation: identify a meaningful, feasible wish, imagine the valued outcome, consider what could obstruct it, and form an if-then plan. For the student, “I want to explain what these studies tell us” becomes more actionable after identifying the vague starting point that sends them toward email. Imagining success alone would leave that obstacle unchanged. Evidence on mental contrasting with implementation intentions supports modest average gains in goal attainment compared with control conditions without the combined exercise, with variation across goals and settings (Oettingen, 2012; Wang et al., 2021). The WOOP research lens explains what that evidence establishes.

Coping plans specify a response to a foreseeable difficulty: “If I cannot resolve a methodological question, I will mark it and draft what I can establish from the papers.” If the obstacle is an urge to escape discomfort, the BRAIN practice offers a pause before acting. The replacement-response method then gives that pause a next step. Preparation matters because the difficult moment is often a poor time to invent an alternative.

Make the activity rewarding now

The student may value the finished paper while finding today’s writing unpleasant. Immediate enjoyment gives the activity something to offer during the work: an interesting question, a comfortable setting, or a way of working the person prefers. In an experiment by Woolley and Fishbach (2016), gym-goers asked to choose an exercise they enjoyed completed more sets than those asked to choose one for its usefulness to their health goals. Delayed benefits can explain why a goal is chosen; the experience of pursuing it can influence whether someone continues.

Positive emotion can come from the activity itself or from recognizing what has just been accomplished. The student who finishes one useful comparison can immediately think, “I made that idea clearer.” This makes progress available to experience now, while the completed paper remains weeks away. A routine built around such moments has something encouraging to return to; one experienced only as a test of inadequacy gives the person another reason to avoid it.

Fogg’s celebration technique deliberately creates a small feeling of success at this point (Fogg, 2020, n.d.). The gesture can be modest: a small victory sign, a smile, or a quiet “Well done.” Choose a response that actually feels encouraging. A loud cheer that feels embarrassing is poorly matched to the person or setting; quietly noticing that the intended step is complete may fit better. The point is to experience the progress, not to perform enthusiasm for an imaginary audience.

Celebrate while carrying out the tiny action or immediately upon completing it. Fogg emphasizes this close timing so that the feeling refers to the behavior just performed. After writing the comparison sentence, the student pauses to appreciate having begun; after one planned push-up, a person acknowledges having followed through. Waiting until the whole paper or fitness goal is finished leaves the early steps without that immediate recognition. A later dinner to mark submitting the paper serves a different purpose from making today’s first sentence feel worthwhile.

Reward learning helps explain the logic of this timing. As Chapter 21 explains, dopamine contributes to learning which actions lead to rewards and to motivating their pursuit. If acknowledging a small success feels rewarding, doing so immediately may help connect that experience with the action just taken, making the action more attractive to repeat. This gives celebration a plausible role in reinforcement, although its specific effect on dopamine has not been established.1 The practical aim is to give the useful action an encouraging consequence now, while its larger benefits remain distant.

Repeated rewarding experiences can also change what the next opportunity promises. Opening the prepared draft may begin to suggest a manageable step and the satisfaction of making progress. This is how the earlier discussion of predictive cues connects to behavior design: an expected benefit can help draw the person into action before that benefit arrives. Repetition at the same prompt also helps the situation retrieve the response more readily. Celebration can support the willingness to practice; the stable prompt and repeated action help build the habit.

Be precise about the success being recognized. “I began as planned” is a fair description of drafting one sentence; “My argument is now excellent” may not be. The student can appreciate starting and still revise a weak comparison. This distinction lets encouragement accompany honest feedback. It also connects celebration with the response to lapses in Chapter 21: acknowledge useful actions when they occur, and use setbacks to adjust the next attempt without condemning yourself.

A person need not feel cheerful before beginning. A tiny action creates an opportunity for encouragement even on a difficult day. If the celebration feels forced, adjust it or notice the practical benefit of completing the step. The student might simply recognize that tomorrow’s work now has a clearer starting point. Keep the action feasible enough to return to when enthusiasm is low.

Temptation bundling pairs a desirable but effortful activity with an immediately appealing one, often by reserving the pleasure for that occasion. “I listen to this favorite audiobook only while walking” makes the walk a route to something enjoyable now. The activities occur together. Promising entertainment after completing the walk instead creates a reward following the behavior.

Milkman and colleagues (2014) randomly assigned participants to gym-only access to tempting audiobooks, encouragement to restrict audiobook listening to the gym themselves, or a comparison condition. Initially, gym visits were 51% higher with restricted access and 29% higher with encouragement than in the comparison group. The effects weakened over time, especially after the Thanksgiving break.

Choose a pairing that preserves the purpose of the desired activity. An audiobook may fit a familiar walk; a gripping television program is likely to compete with understanding a difficult paper. The student might test a pleasant drink or comfortable study setting instead. Check the quality of the work as well as time spent on it, and prepare how the pairing will resume after a break.

Incentives promise a consequence contingent on an action or result: a small reward after a planned session, recognition for a useful contribution, or payment for a completed task. Deliver the consequence close enough to the behavior for the connection to be clear, and tie it to something worth encouraging. A word-count reward may produce more words while leaving the argument unchanged; rewarding completion of a checked comparison better matches the student’s aim.

An outcome functions as a reinforcer when it strengthens subsequent behavior. Chapter 21 distinguishes reinforcement from punishment and explains how relief can also sustain repetition. Removing an agreed privilege or imposing a penalty is intended to discourage an action, but the meaning of the consequence also matters. The day-care fine example shows how a penalty can instead be interpreted as a price.

Gamification adds devices such as points, badges, levels, or streaks. These can make progress visible and introduce a challenge, but the score must represent the activity that matters. A streak that encourages superficial check-ins needs redesign. For the student, a record of completed, checked comparisons provides more useful feedback than a count of how often the document was opened. Acknowledge progress in a way that leaves room for planned rest and a return after missed occasions.

Protect the plan from temptation and distraction

If the problem is a predictable future temptation, precommitment can constrain the later choice (Thaler & Shefrin, 1981). App blockers, cancellation penalties, and restricted-access accounts do this in different ways, with different costs and possibilities for reversal. Assess whether the constraint supports the person’s goal and remains acceptable when circumstances change. The intertemporal-choice chapter reviews commitment evidence, including the retracted deadline study; a plausible device still needs evidence that it helps.

The constraint may be soft, such as agreeing to show a colleague a draft at a chosen time, or hard, such as a lock that cannot be immediately disabled or a deposit forfeited if an agreed action is missed. Friction raises the effort needed to depart from a plan; commitment deliberately changes the future options or consequences. A device may do both. Choose the smallest constraint that addresses the recurring problem, with terms that remain acceptable if illness, urgent work, or other legitimate needs intervene.

For the student, beginning the comparison will accomplish little if every new message interrupts it. In Indistractable, Eyal (2019) distinguishes traction, which serves an intended activity, from distraction, which takes us away from it. Email can support a planned correspondence period and interrupt a writing period; chosen rest also deserves protected time.

Checking can begin with an external trigger, such as an alert, or an internal trigger, such as boredom or uncertainty. An attention boundary therefore needs more than silenced notifications: notice what prompts the detour, reserve realistic time for the intended activity, and adjust interrupting cues. Time blocking reserves a period for a chosen activity; batching groups similar tasks into planned periods, such as checking email after the writing session. Leave space for breaks and genuine urgent demands. If uncertainty about a study repeatedly precedes checking, mark the unresolved issue or seek the needed explanation. If a rough sentence feels unacceptable, try drafting it before editing. The precommitment tools above can help protect a feasible plan when temptation is predictable; they cannot teach writing skills or create unavailable time.

These changes should address an observed problem. Ward and colleagues (2017) reported lower cognitive performance when participants’ own phones were nearby, but a later meta-analysis found a narrower pattern: pooled impairment appeared for working memory, while other cognitive outcomes showed no significant effect (Parry, 2024). Phone presence and an actual notification are different conditions; Limited Attention discusses interruption. Switching away from an unfinished task also creates a distinct difficulty: part of the previous task can remain mentally active—attention residue—and reduce performance on the next task (Leroy, 2009).

Digital services can also encourage the repetitions through which habits develop. Eyal’s (2014) Hook Model describes a product-design sequence: Trigger → Action → Variable reward → Investment. The last two elements help explain why someone might return after the first visit.

A variable reward concerns the outcome: opening a course forum may reveal useful feedback, a routine reply, or nothing new. In four experiments with real consequences, Shen and colleagues (2019) found that uncertain incentives could encourage repetition even when financially inferior to certain incentives. The advantage appeared after people experienced the activity and when uncertainty was resolved immediately; delaying resolution removed it. This supports an explanation in which finding out the outcome can itself reward participation.

Compare that with Shen and colleagues’ (2015) pursuit experiments in Chapter 21: uncertainty can make working toward a reward more engaging, whereas the 2019 studies examine repeating an activity after experiencing its outcome. Neither finding establishes that repetition has become automatic or that uncertainty always helps.

An investment is effort that makes a later visit more valuable or sets up another cue. Saving an explanation creates useful material to return to; posting a follow-up question may generate the next reply alert (Eyal, 2014). In the student’s writing routine, leaving notes and a selected comparison prepares the next start. Variable rewards and investment can thus support repetition, but neither is required for every habit: reliable, satisfying progress can also encourage return. Table 39.2 examines when these features support the user’s goal and when they make disengagement difficult.

Evaluate the loop against the outcome the user values. As the near-miss example in Chapter 21 shows, renewed desire to continue need not indicate pleasure or progress. More clicks, check-ins, or time spent can coexist with less learning or lower well-being; measure goal attainment and opportunity cost as well as engagement.

Support the change over time

Some barriers are easier to change with another person. Practical support supplies something needed for action: shared notes, help understanding a method, protected time, or someone to handle another responsibility. Emotional support makes it possible to acknowledge difficulty and ask for help. Accountability creates an agreed occasion to explain what happened and decide what to do next. These forms of support address different problems.

The student might arrange a quiet writing session with a peer, state the comparison each intends to draft, and finish by reading each other’s paragraph. The arrangement supplies a prompt, protected time, and feedback. A useful check-in asks, “What did you try, where did you get stuck, and what support would help?” It can reveal why a plan failed without turning a missed session into a public verdict on character. Agree on what will be shared and how the other person will respond.

Modeling and social comparison offer information about how others act. Watching a peer work through an imperfect draft can reveal a strategy worth trying; comparing only final polished work may hide that process. Norm messages describe what relevant others do or approve of. They need to be truthful and relevant to the setting: advertising that almost everyone procrastinates may make procrastination seem ordinary. Chapter 26 explains social influence, while Chapter 40 examines its use in design.

Beginning once and maintaining a routine pose different problems. The student needs a plan that can survive a busy week, a missed morning, or a change in schedule.

A new semester can make a fresh writing plan feel credible. Aspirational behavior often increases around such temporal landmarks (Dai et al., 2014), but the same inbox and vague first task may still win on the first morning. Use the occasion to prepare what will be different: the writing location, the first task, or the time available. The fresh-start research lens distinguishes a calendar landmark from an actual change in the setting.

The cue-based account in Chapter 21 suggests why a move or changed schedule can disrupt a familiar response, while leaving it available if the old cues return. Prepare a routine for the changed setting, including replacement cues for useful habits that the disruption might otherwise unsettle (Wood, 2019).

Identity can affect how people interpret that practice. Difficulty might mean “this matters to people like me” or “this proves I do not belong” (Oyserman, 2009). “I am becoming someone who begins with a rough draft and improves it” gives the student an action to practice without requiring every morning to go well. Treat the identity as flexible enough to accommodate rest, revision, and setbacks.

A missed morning should lead to a specific next step. The student might plan: “If I miss the writing period, then I will mark the next comparison that evening and begin with it after breakfast.” This connects recovery to another workable occasion, instead of leaving it dependent on a renewed promise.

Repeated lapses call for another look at the diagnosis. The task may remain too difficult, the time may be unavailable, or the competing action may serve a need the plan ignored. Review the support and goal as well as the prompt. Recovery from addiction may require clinical assessment and evidence-based treatment alongside changes to everyday routines (Volkow et al., 2016).2

Changing a routine together

The student can rearrange his own desk, but an employee who wants to speak openly depends on how colleagues respond. An organizational routine involves several people, roles, tools, and rules. Deliberate incentives and formal requirements can sustain it alongside automatic responses, so changing one person’s habit may leave the larger pattern intact.

The same design workflow now has to follow an interaction: who does what, how others respond, and which part of that sequence could change.

A product team wants concerns to surface early, yet its Monday meetings repeatedly discourage them. When a senior manager questions a progress update, the project lead hears criticism and explains why the team’s choices were reasonable. Other members fall silent. The manager interprets the explanation as a refusal to take responsibility and presses harder, prompting more defense. The meeting ends with action items but little new information about the project’s risks.

The manager meant to improve the project, the lead meant to protect the team, and colleagues meant to avoid conflict. Their reactions nevertheless sustained a pattern none of them wanted. One possible explanation is that defending past choices protects the lead’s standing in the moment, while silence protects colleagues from joining the dispute. Ask participants what they understood and examine repeated meetings before settling on that diagnosis.

Agree with the team on a short, reversible trial; giving people a say and reducing uncertainty can make a change easier to try (Berger, 2020). Begin with one change: before questioning the update, the manager asks, “What would be most useful for us to challenge?” Keep the rest of the meeting reasonably stable and record whether a concrete uncertainty is raised before a defensive exchange begins. If that change helps, a later test might add the lead’s implementation intention: “If I hear criticism, I will ask which risk the question is meant to clarify.” Introducing both changes together would make their separate contributions harder to learn.

The manager’s subsequent conduct remains part of the design. As Chapter 28 emphasized, inviting a concern and punishing the answer would teach silence again. Track both whether concerns appear earlier and whether they receive useful responses, including when they challenge the manager’s own view.

Evaluate the change and whose goals it serves

The meeting example makes a further question unavoidable: who gets to decide what counts as better behavior? The student can choose a writing arrangement, while a manager or platform designer changes conditions for other people. More reporting or longer engagement may serve the organization without serving those asked to participate.

Ask whether affected people can understand the arrangement, question its goals, choose otherwise, and discover its costs. A streak may encourage practice while making rest feel like failure; a reporting dashboard may improve coordination while exposing staff to surveillance. These effects belong in the evaluation alongside the behavior the designer hopes to increase.

The student may begin before email more often without improving the paper. A team may report more concerns without addressing any of them. Separate the action a design encourages from the outcome that makes it worth encouraging.

Before making the change, record what happens at relevant opportunities. For the student, that means noting whether writing begins before email and what work gets done. During the test, continue that record and check the resulting argument: does the paragraph compare the studies accurately, support its claims with appropriate evidence, and help answer the research question? For the team, note whether concerns receive an answer or lead to a useful change in the project.

Self-monitoring makes behavior and progress available for review. A brief record could note the cue, whether the planned start happened, the work completed, and the obstacle encountered. In a meta-analysis of 138 randomized studies, Harkin and colleagues (2016) found that interventions encouraging progress monitoring improved goal attainment on average. Recording can therefore be part of the intervention itself. Keep the monitoring arrangement consistent when comparing an earlier period with a changed routine.

Feedback relates that record to a goal and helps select an adjustment. “You worked for twenty minutes” reports duration; “This paragraph now distinguishes the samples, but the causal claim still exceeds the design” identifies what to improve. Review at a useful interval, choose the next change, and keep the record short enough to maintain. A visible checklist can acknowledge completion, while the substance of the work determines whether the goal is being served. Chapter 40’s feedback examples apply this reasoning to choices made across a wider system.

Choose a few measures that answer distinct questions:

  • Follow-through: Did the planned action happen when the opportunity arose?
  • Outcome: Did it improve the learning, judgment, cooperation, or well-being it was meant to support?
  • Maintenance: Does the change persist after novelty or intensive support declines?
  • Costs and access: Who can use the arrangement, and what effort, trust, rest, or flexibility does it cost?

Set a review date and decide what would justify keeping, revising, or stopping the change. A personal before-and-after comparison can inform the next attempt, but other changes may explain the result. Stronger causal claims require a comparison that can separate those explanations.

Practice Lab: behavior-design studio

Choose one manageable everyday behavior you want to change and complete the record in Table 39.1:

  1. Define: write the observable target, competing action, and recurring situation.
  2. Diagnose: name the cue, the current action’s immediate function, and the main obstacle. Use B=MAP to distinguish willingness, feasibility, and the prompt.
  3. Redesign: change one decisive point. Choose one primary technique from Table 39.3. Write the desired action, its cue or prompt, and the preparation that makes it feasible. If a competing action repeatedly wins, identify where added friction could interrupt it. If testing celebration, specify how and when you will acknowledge the completed step. State which change you will test first.
  4. Measure: record what happens before the change, predict what will improve, and choose one measure of follow-through and one of the valued outcome for a short reversible test.

Conclude with one sentence: “If this diagnosis is useful, we should observe…” Name the action or event that would support the explanation, along with one result that would send you back to the diagnosis.

Take it forward

Tomorrow morning, the student opens a laptop onto a prepared comparison, with the inbox closed and the necessary papers ready. Opening the laptop supplies the prompt; preparation makes the response feasible. After drafting the sentence, the student can pause to notice: “I have started, and I know what to work on next.” A useful result, an enjoyable working experience, or supportive feedback may make returning worthwhile. A detour toward email still calls for diagnosis: the next change may concern a cue, a writing skill, discomfort, or unavailable time. Choose one recurring occasion in your own day, prepare the response it should prompt, and decide what result would lead you to revise the arrangement.

NoteResearch Lens: behavior design in apps

Instagram’s history provides a documented connection to Fogg’s teaching. In a Stanford talk, Tristan Harris (2017) described Instagram co-founder Mike Krieger as his project partner in Fogg’s persuasive-technology class and said that techniques they learned informed Instagram. This is firsthand testimony about educational influence. It does not isolate B=MAP as the cause of the company’s growth, which also depended on the product, its users, and the market it entered.

Clubhouse offers a concrete feature to analyze. Its 2021 Wave announcement described sending a friend an invitation to talk: the friend received a notification and could join a private audio room (Clubhouse, 2021). Through B=MAP, the opportunity to talk with a friend supplies a possible motivation, the notification prompts a response, and the short path into the room reduces effort. This is our application of the model to the feature, rather than evidence that Clubhouse’s designers used Fogg’s model. Even a well-timed invitation needs a conversation the recipient wants and time to participate.

Duolingo reported an A/B test of making a streak easier to maintain. Previously, users had to meet their full daily learning goal; the revised design let one completed lesson extend the streak while displaying the larger goal separately (Duolingo, n.d.). The company reported a 3.3% relative increase in retention at day 14, alongside fewer users meeting their full daily goals. Lowering the demand supported return, while changing how much completion was needed for the progress signal. Retention and meeting the learning target could move in different directions. The report supports this particular design change; it does not establish that the team adopted B=MAP or that greater retention by itself meant greater language proficiency.

These examples separate acquiring a user, helping the user take a first action, and making later visits worthwhile. A subscription service depends on continued value; an advertising service may benefit from more attention even when the user would prefer to stop. B=MAP helps identify a behavioral moment to redesign. The evaluation must still ask whether the resulting action serves the person’s goal, a question taken up in evaluating whose goals a design serves.

NoteResearch note: the ideas behind the workflow

The workflow combines diagnosis, planning, and review. The Fogg Behavior Model describes motivation, ability, and a cue for action converging at a behavioral moment; its earlier term “trigger” became “prompt” in later presentations (Fogg, 2009, 2020). Research on implementation intentions, habits, and behavior-change maintenance supports planning, feedback, and preparation for obstacles (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006; Kwasnicka et al., 2016; Wood & Rünger, 2016).

Michie and colleagues’ (2013) Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy distinguishes 93 techniques in 16 groups, providing a common vocabulary for describing interventions. The practical families in Table 39.3 bring together techniques commonly used in everyday behavior change. A taxonomy identifies what an intervention contains; evidence about a particular technique’s effects must come from studies of its use. Some familiar packages combine several techniques, as a writing group can combine scheduling, prompts, support, and feedback.

NoteResearch Lens: COM-B widens the diagnosis

COM-B asks whether capability, opportunity, and motivation converge (Michie et al., 2011). Capability includes skill, knowledge, energy, and physical ability. Opportunity includes time, tools, permission, safety, and social context. Motivation includes reflective and automatic processes. A motivational speech cannot fix a broken opportunity structure. COM-B is useful when B=MAP’s moment-level diagnosis needs a broader account of resources and institutions.

Use the separate framework questions in Table 39.4 to identify when the barrier calls for resources, access, treatment, or organizational change. Their components overlap, but a prompt at the right moment cannot substitute for missing capability or opportunity.

NoteResearch Lens: what WOOP evidence establishes

WOOP combines mental contrasting—considering a desired future alongside a present obstacle—with an implementation intention specifying a response to that obstacle. Wang, Wang, and Gai’s (2021) meta-analysis of this combined procedure included 21 articles, 24 independent effect sizes, and 15,907 participants. The pooled effect on goal attainment was small to moderate (Hedges’ g = 0.34; 95% confidence interval: 0.23–0.44).

Effects varied across studies, and indications of publication bias suggest the average may be overstated. Comparisons also used different control conditions. The result supports trying a structured obstacle-and-plan exercise. For the student, the test remains whether the chosen plan addresses the actual obstacle to writing.

NoteResearch Lens: a fresh start and a changed setting

Calendar landmarks can encourage initiation. Dai and colleagues (2014) examined diet-related internet searches, gym attendance, and commitments to pursue goals in three archival studies. Aspirational activity rose around temporal landmarks such as a new week, month, or year. These patterns are consistent with treating a new period as a break from past shortcomings.

Relocation can make intervention more timely. Verplanken and Roy (2016) compared a sustainability intervention with a control condition among 800 participants, including recent movers and matched nonmovers. Across 25 self-reported behaviors measured at baseline and eight weeks later, the intervention was more effective among recent movers. Moving may interrupt familiar cues and create an opening for a new routine. Relocation itself was not randomly assigned.

A new semester supplies a date. A new desk, timetable, or commute changes the conditions of action. Either may provide an opening, but a useful plan specifies what will happen differently when the next opportunity arrives.
