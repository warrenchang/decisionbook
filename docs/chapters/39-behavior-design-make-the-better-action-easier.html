39  Behavior Design

Make the Better Action Easier

“For the great doesn’t happen through impulse alone, and is a succession of little things that are brought together.”

— Vincent van Gogh, letter to Theo van Gogh, 22 October 1882, letter 274

A student intends to work on a term paper before opening email, but the laptop opens onto the inbox each morning. Replies offer small tasks with clear endings; the paper offers a vague instruction: “Work on the literature review.” By the time the student opens the draft, much of the available writing time has gone.

One possible change is to leave the draft open the evening before, beside notes on two studies and a precise next step: “Write one sentence explaining how their findings agree or differ.” The next morning begins with a visible action instead of a decision about where to start. That could help if the vague starting point was the main obstacle. If the student cannot yet interpret the studies, needs guidance on academic writing, or has no time available that morning, a clearer prompt will accomplish little.

The useful change depends on why the intention fails. Behavior design starts with that explanation, then changes a specific condition and tests whether the intended action becomes easier to carry out.

NoteCore Idea

Begin with an action someone wants to take and a specific occasion when it fails to happen. Find what makes starting or continuing difficult, change that condition, and observe the result. A useful design also explains how to resume after a lapse and when to reconsider the goal or seek more support.

Learning goals

  • Apply one five-step workflow: define the behavior, diagnose the bottleneck, redesign the decisive point, measure, and review.
  • Match a practical change to the obstacle it addresses.
  • Distinguish follow-through from the outcome it is meant to improve, and plan how to resume after a lapse.

The habit chapter used bedtime scrolling to distinguish familiar cues, wanting, and enjoyment (Wood & Rünger, 2016). Term-paper writing is a useful transfer case because several other obstacles can produce a similar detour. Opening email may be habitual, but it can also avoid an unclear or uncomfortable task, meet a real coordination need, or fill time too short for the planned work. This chapter turns that diagnosis into a plan for change, whether the aim is to alter an established routine or begin something new.

From intention to a testable change

“Work on my paper” gives the student a direction, but it does not specify what should happen differently tomorrow. “Draft one comparison of the two selected studies before opening email” identifies an action, a competing response, and an occasion to observe. Now the student can ask why email comes first and choose a change that addresses that obstacle.

Relying on willpower alone would mean resisting the inbox afresh whenever writing feels difficult. That can work on a particular morning, but it leaves success dependent on repeatedly winning the same conflict. A more reliable plan prepares the conditions under which the desired action starts and continues (Duckworth et al., 2016).

CautionSelf-control effort and the debate over ego depletion

The ego-depletion account pictures willpower as a fuel supply that each act of self-control uses up. Large preregistered tests have challenged that account (Hagger et al., 2016; Vohs et al., 2021). In a 36-laboratory study with 3,531 participants, the planned analysis found a near-zero effect of prior self-control effort on subsequent performance. Another multilab study found a small effect (Dang et al., 2021), but a performance cost does not establish that a fuel has been consumed. Willpower should not be treated as a fixed, depletable supply; effort and fatigue can matter without that explanation. Appendix E examines the disputed evidence.

Two tasks clarify what self-control evidence can establish. The Stroop demonstration in Chapter 8 shows interference from a practiced response. The marshmallow studies in Chapter 20 separate the effect of a reliable promise from the association between childhood waiting and later achievement. Neither task is a direct gauge of a person’s fixed supply of willpower.

Use the CIAO habit loop to organize that preparation: choose a reliable cue, anticipate the impulse that competes with the goal, make the intended action easy enough to begin, and arrange an outcome that makes progress visible. For the student, this means returning to the prepared draft after breakfast, marking an uncertainty instead of escaping into email, and completing one useful comparison. Repeating that start in a familiar setting can build a cue–action association; deliberate effort helps establish and adjust the arrangement.

The return arrow in Figure 39.1 matters: an unsuccessful attempt can reveal that the obstacle was misidentified. Making the next writing step visible tests a different explanation from obtaining missing studies, learning how to evaluate their methods, or finding a feasible period to work.

Table 39.1 turns the figure into a short record. Write down the prediction before trying the change, so that the result can challenge the explanation that seemed plausible at the start.

For the student, the prediction might be: leaving the draft open with a specific comparison to make will make writing more likely to begin before email. Starting is the first thing to observe; whether the paragraph offers an accurate, useful comparison is a separate question to return to when evaluating the change.

Diagnose the moment with B=MAP

The student wants to advance the paper, yet willingness alone has not settled what happens when the laptop opens. The Fogg Behavior Model, B=MAP, describes behavior as occurring when motivation, ability, and a prompt converge (Fogg, 2009, 2020). Here ability means how easy the action is to perform at that moment; a prompt calls for it to happen.

This distinction gives the student three questions to answer. Is this writing step worth doing now, or does fear of producing a poor paragraph make email more attractive? Can the student carry it out with the writing skills, understanding, materials, and time available? What will bring it to attention before the inbox does? A reminder addresses the last question, but cannot supply missing understanding or protected time. Calling every detour a habit or “low motivation” would obscure the diagnosis.

Opportunity can also influence motivation. The smoking studies in Chapter 21 show that expecting access can strengthen craving itself. Changing access may therefore affect the impulse before action, as well as make the action easier or harder to carry out.

When motivation fluctuates, preparing an easier first action and a timely prompt may offer a more workable plan than expecting the same enthusiasm every morning.

Look for mornings when writing already begins before email. Such bright spots can suggest conditions worth reproducing and testing, such as a clearer first task or a quieter setting (Heath & Heath, 2010).

Make the first action small

The inbox offers an easy response with a quick sense of completion. To give the paper a feasible starting point, the student could draft one rough comparison using the two studies already selected. The small action provides an entry into the work; its value depends on what follows, including whether the comparison is accurate and helps answer the research question.

Fogg’s practical recipe connects that small start to an existing routine: After I [reliable anchor], I will [tiny behavior]. Then I will [authentically acknowledge completion]. The anchor supplies a prompt, the small action reduces the effort needed to begin, and acknowledgment marks progress. For the student, the anchor might be sitting down with breakfast finished, and the action might be drafting the first comparison sentence.

An inspiring class or approaching deadline may make an ambitious plan feel easy. Use that motivation to prepare for a tired Wednesday: obtain the studies, identify the next comparison, and arrange a workable time. The arrangement still has to be tried when the initial enthusiasm fades.

Implementation intentions

A feasible action can still be forgotten at the moment it matters. Implementation intentions connect an anticipated situation to a specified response: If this happens, then I will do that. The plan helps bring a goal to mind and guide action when the situation occurs (Gollwitzer, 1999).

The student can turn “I will work on my paper” into “If I finish breakfast on a weekday, then I will sit at my desk and draft the selected comparison before opening email.” Twenty-five minutes of writing might remain the session goal, while drafting the comparison specifies how to begin. The same structure works beyond writing: a manager might plan to ask one clarifying question when receiving criticism, or a saver to transfer 10 percent of each salary payment immediately. Each plan identifies an occasion and an action concrete enough to carry out.

Milkman and colleagues (2011) randomly assigned employees to three flu-clinic reminders: basic information, information plus a field for a planned date, or fields for both date and time. Vaccination rates were 33.1% in the basic-reminder group and 37.1% in the date-and-time group—a 4.0-percentage-point difference. The covariate-adjusted estimate was 4.2 points (95% confidence interval: 0.5–7.8); the date-only prompt did not reliably outperform the basic reminder. A concrete plan helped translate an available opportunity into action.

Sometimes the difficulty is choosing a realistic response to an obstacle. WOOP (Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan) adds that preparation: identify a meaningful, feasible wish, imagine the valued outcome, consider what could obstruct it, and form an if-then plan. For the student, “I want to explain what these studies tell us” becomes more actionable after identifying the vague starting point that sends them toward email. Imagining success alone would leave that obstacle unchanged. Evidence on mental contrasting with implementation intentions supports modest average gains in goal attainment compared with control conditions without the combined exercise, with variation across goals and settings (Oettingen, 2012; Wang et al., 2021). The WOOP research lens explains what that evidence establishes.

Friction, rewards, and commitment

A plan specifies what to do, but the effort and immediate appeal of competing actions still matter. Friction includes the steps, time, and effort needed to act. Leaving the draft and source notes open reduces friction for writing; putting the phone in another room adds friction to checking it. The same principle can guide other changes, such as removing saved payment details before an impulsive purchase or simplifying a form that people repeatedly abandon.

Changing the default also changes what happens without an active choice. Chapter 40 develops this approach across a whole choice process, including its boundary conditions and ethics. Here the student can begin with the narrower question of which action the current arrangement makes easiest. Defaults change the starting point; the home-energy-report experiments in Chapter 40 instead test repeated feedback and whether its effects persist after messages stop. These are different interventions, even when both support follow-through.

When effort is immediate and the benefit delayed, temptation bundling pairs a beneficial activity with something enjoyable now. Milkman and colleagues (2014) randomly assigned participants to gym-only access to tempting audiobooks, encouragement to restrict audiobook listening to the gym themselves, or a comparison condition. Initially, gym visits were 51% higher with restricted access and 29% higher with encouragement than in the comparison group. These are relative differences in visits, not percentage-point changes in attendance rates. The effects weakened over time, especially after the Thanksgiving break.

A person might similarly reserve a favorite podcast for walking. The experiment suggests that linking an immediate attraction to an effortful activity can help people begin, while the fading effect makes recovery after disruption part of the design.

If the problem is a predictable future temptation, precommitment can constrain the later choice (Thaler & Shefrin, 1981). App blockers, cancellation penalties, and restricted-access accounts do this in different ways, with different costs and possibilities for reversal. Assess whether the constraint supports the person’s goal and remains acceptable when circumstances change. The intertemporal-choice chapter reviews commitment evidence, including the retracted deadline study; a plausible device still needs evidence that it helps.

Design an attention boundary

For the student, beginning the comparison will accomplish little if every new message interrupts it. In Indistractable, Eyal (2019) distinguishes traction, which serves an intended activity, from distraction, which takes us away from it. Email can support a planned correspondence period and interrupt a writing period; chosen rest also deserves protected time.

Checking can begin with an external trigger, such as an alert, or an internal trigger, such as boredom or uncertainty. An attention boundary therefore needs more than silenced notifications: notice what prompts the detour, reserve realistic time for the intended activity, and adjust interrupting cues. If uncertainty about a study repeatedly precedes checking, mark the unresolved issue or seek the needed explanation. If a rough sentence feels unacceptable, try drafting it before editing. The precommitment tools above can help protect a feasible plan when temptation is predictable; they cannot teach writing skills or create unavailable time.

These changes should address an observed problem. Ward and colleagues (2017) reported lower cognitive performance when participants’ own phones were nearby, but a later meta-analysis found a narrower pattern: pooled impairment appeared for working memory, while other cognitive outcomes showed no significant effect (Parry, 2024). Phone presence and an actual notification are different conditions; Limited Attention discusses interruption. Switching away from an unfinished task also creates a distinct difficulty: part of the previous task can remain mentally active—attention residue—and reduce performance on the next task (Leroy, 2009).

Digital services can also encourage the repetitions through which habits develop. Eyal’s (2014) Hook Model describes a product-design sequence: Trigger → Action → Variable reward → Investment. The last two elements help explain why someone might return after the first visit.

A variable reward concerns the outcome: opening a course forum may reveal useful feedback, a routine reply, or nothing new. In four experiments with real consequences, Shen and colleagues (2019) found that uncertain incentives could encourage repetition even when financially inferior to certain incentives. The advantage appeared after people experienced the activity and when uncertainty was resolved immediately; delaying resolution removed it. This supports an explanation in which finding out the outcome can itself reward participation.

Compare that with Shen and colleagues’ (2015) pursuit experiments in Chapter 21: uncertainty can make working toward a reward more engaging, whereas the 2019 studies examine repeating an activity after experiencing its outcome. Neither finding establishes that repetition has become automatic or that uncertainty always helps.

An investment is effort that makes a later visit more valuable or sets up another cue. Saving an explanation creates useful material to return to; posting a follow-up question may generate the next reply alert (Eyal, 2014). In the student’s writing routine, leaving notes and a selected comparison prepares the next start. Variable rewards and investment can thus support repetition, but neither is required for every habit: reliable, satisfying progress can also encourage return. Table 39.2 examines when these features support the user’s goal and when they make disengagement difficult.

Evaluate the loop against the outcome the user values. As the near-miss example in Chapter 21 shows, renewed desire to continue need not indicate pleasure or progress. More clicks, check-ins, or time spent can coexist with less learning or lower well-being; measure goal attainment and opportunity cost as well as engagement.

Make the change last

Beginning once and maintaining a routine pose different problems. The student needs a plan that can survive a busy week, a missed morning, or a change in schedule.

Use a fresh start

A new semester can make a fresh writing plan feel credible. Aspirational behavior often increases around such temporal landmarks (Dai et al., 2014), but the same inbox and vague first task may still win on the first morning. Use the occasion to prepare what will be different: the writing location, the first task, or the time available. The fresh-start research lens distinguishes a calendar landmark from an actual change in the setting.

The cue-based account in Chapter 21 suggests why a move or changed schedule can disrupt a familiar response, while leaving it available if the old cues return. Prepare a routine for the changed setting, including replacement cues for useful habits that the disruption might otherwise unsettle (Wood, 2019).

Identity can affect how people interpret that practice. Difficulty might mean “this matters to people like me” or “this proves I do not belong” (Oyserman, 2009). “I am becoming someone who begins with a rough draft and improves it” gives the student an action to practice without requiring every morning to go well. Treat the identity as flexible enough to accommodate rest, revision, and setbacks.

Plan for lapses

A missed morning should lead to a specific next step. The student might plan: “If I miss the writing period, then I will mark the next comparison that evening and begin with it after breakfast.” This connects recovery to another workable occasion, instead of leaving it dependent on a renewed promise.

Repeated lapses call for another look at the diagnosis. The task may remain too difficult, the time may be unavailable, or the competing action may serve a need the plan ignored. Review the support and goal as well as the prompt. Recovery from addiction may require clinical assessment and evidence-based treatment alongside changes to everyday routines (Volkow et al., 2016).1

Changing a routine together

The student can rearrange his own desk, but an employee who wants to speak openly depends on how colleagues respond. An organizational routine involves several people, roles, tools, and rules. Deliberate incentives and formal requirements can sustain it alongside automatic responses, so changing one person’s habit may leave the larger pattern intact.

The same design workflow now has to follow an interaction: who does what, how others respond, and which part of that sequence could change.

Mini-case: the defensive meeting loop

A product team wants concerns to surface early, yet its Monday meetings repeatedly discourage them. When a senior manager questions a progress update, the project lead hears criticism and explains why the team’s choices were reasonable. Other members fall silent. The manager interprets the explanation as a refusal to take responsibility and presses harder, prompting more defense. The meeting ends with action items but little new information about the project’s risks.

The manager meant to improve the project, the lead meant to protect the team, and colleagues meant to avoid conflict. Their reactions nevertheless sustained a pattern none of them wanted. One possible explanation is that defending past choices protects the lead’s standing in the moment, while silence protects colleagues from joining the dispute. Ask participants what they understood and examine repeated meetings before settling on that diagnosis.

Agree with the team on a short, reversible trial; giving people a say and reducing uncertainty can make a change easier to try (Berger, 2020). Begin with one change: before questioning the update, the manager asks, “What would be most useful for us to challenge?” Keep the rest of the meeting reasonably stable and record whether a concrete uncertainty is raised before a defensive exchange begins. If that change helps, a later test might add the lead’s implementation intention: “If I hear criticism, I will ask which risk the question is meant to clarify.” Introducing both changes together would make their separate contributions harder to learn.

The manager’s subsequent conduct remains part of the design. As Chapter 28 emphasized, inviting a concern and punishing the answer would teach silence again. Track both whether concerns appear earlier and whether they receive useful responses, including when they challenge the manager’s own view.

Whose goals does the design serve?

The meeting example makes a further question unavoidable: who gets to decide what counts as better behavior? The student can choose a writing arrangement, while a manager or platform designer changes conditions for other people. More reporting or longer engagement may serve the organization without serving those asked to participate.

Ask whether affected people can understand the arrangement, question its goals, choose otherwise, and discover its costs. A streak may encourage practice while making rest feel like failure; a reporting dashboard may improve coordination while exposing staff to surveillance. These effects belong in the evaluation alongside the behavior the designer hopes to increase.

Measure what the change achieves

The student may begin before email more often without improving the paper. A team may report more concerns without addressing any of them. Separate the action a design encourages from the outcome that makes it worth encouraging.

Before making the change, record what happens at relevant opportunities. For the student, that means noting whether writing begins before email and what work gets done. During the test, continue that record and check the resulting argument: does the paragraph compare the studies accurately, support its claims with appropriate evidence, and help answer the research question? For the team, note whether concerns receive an answer or lead to a useful change in the project.

Choose a few measures that answer distinct questions:

  • Follow-through: Did the planned action happen when the opportunity arose?
  • Outcome: Did it improve the learning, judgment, cooperation, or well-being it was meant to support?
  • Maintenance: Does the change persist after novelty or intensive support declines?
  • Costs and access: Who can use the arrangement, and what effort, trust, rest, or flexibility does it cost?

Set a review date and decide what would justify keeping, revising, or stopping the change. A personal before-and-after comparison can inform the next attempt, but other changes may explain the result. Stronger causal claims require a comparison that can separate those explanations.

Practice Lab: behavior-design studio

Choose one manageable everyday behavior you want to change and complete the record in Table 39.1:

  1. Define: write the observable target, competing action, and recurring situation.
  2. Diagnose: name the cue, the current action’s immediate function, and the main obstacle. Use B=MAP to distinguish willingness, feasibility, and the prompt.
  3. Redesign: change one decisive point. Choose no more than one primary tool from Table 39.3 and state exactly what will be different in the environment or response.
  4. Measure: record what happens before the change, predict what will improve, and choose one measure of follow-through and one of the valued outcome for a short reversible test.
  5. Review: set a date and a rule for keeping, revising, or stopping the change. Add a way to resume after a lapse and one cost or unintended effect that would make you reconsider.

Conclude with one sentence: “If this diagnosis is useful, we should observe…” Name the action or event that would support the explanation, along with one result that would send you back to the diagnosis.

Take it forward

The student’s intention becomes a plan when tomorrow morning has a clear starting point and a way to check whether the work improves the paper. A detour toward email still calls for diagnosis: the next change may concern a cue, a writing skill, task uncertainty, discomfort, or time. Choose one recurring occasion in your own day and make the same move: identify the obstacle, prepare a response, and decide what result would lead you to revise it.

The next chapter, Choice Architecture, widens the view from a particular action to the sequence of choices through which people must pass.

NoteResearch note: the ideas behind the workflow

The workflow combines diagnosis, planning, and review. The Fogg Behavior Model describes motivation, ability, and a cue for action converging at a behavioral moment; its earlier term “trigger” became “prompt” in later presentations (Fogg, 2009, 2020). Research on implementation intentions, habits, and behavior-change maintenance supports planning, feedback, and preparation for obstacles (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006; Kwasnicka et al., 2016; Wood & Rünger, 2016).

NoteResearch Lens: COM-B widens the diagnosis

COM-B asks whether capability, opportunity, and motivation converge (Michie et al., 2011). Capability includes skill, knowledge, energy, and physical ability. Opportunity includes time, tools, permission, safety, and social context. Motivation includes reflective and automatic processes. A motivational speech cannot fix a broken opportunity structure. COM-B is useful when B=MAP’s moment-level diagnosis needs a broader account of resources and institutions.

Use the separate framework questions in Table 39.4 to identify when the barrier calls for resources, access, treatment, or organizational change. Their components overlap, but a prompt at the right moment cannot substitute for missing capability or opportunity.

NoteResearch Lens: what WOOP evidence establishes

WOOP combines mental contrasting—considering a desired future alongside a present obstacle—with an implementation intention specifying a response to that obstacle. Wang, Wang, and Gai’s (2021) meta-analysis of this combined procedure included 21 articles, 24 independent effect sizes, and 15,907 participants. The pooled effect on goal attainment was small to moderate (Hedges’ g = 0.34; 95% confidence interval: 0.23–0.44).

Effects varied across studies, and indications of publication bias suggest the average may be overstated. Comparisons also used different control conditions. The result supports trying a structured obstacle-and-plan exercise. For the student, the test remains whether the chosen plan addresses the actual obstacle to writing.

NoteResearch Lens: a fresh start and a changed setting

Calendar landmarks can encourage initiation. Dai and colleagues (2014) examined diet-related internet searches, gym attendance, and commitments to pursue goals in three archival studies. Aspirational activity rose around temporal landmarks such as a new week, month, or year. These patterns are consistent with treating a new period as a break from past shortcomings.

Relocation can make intervention more timely. Verplanken and Roy (2016) compared a sustainability intervention with a control condition among 800 participants, including recent movers and matched nonmovers. Across 25 self-reported behaviors measured at baseline and eight weeks later, the intervention was more effective among recent movers. Moving may interrupt familiar cues and create an opening for a new routine. Relocation itself was not randomly assigned.

A new semester supplies a date. A new desk, timetable, or commute changes the conditions of action. Either may provide an opening, but a useful plan specifies what will happen differently when the next opportunity arrives.
