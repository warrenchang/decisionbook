1  Decision-Making Is a Process, Not a Moment

A decision is already in the making before choice becomes visible

NoteCore Idea

A visible choice is the endpoint of hidden work: attention selected evidence, interpretation supplied meaning, options became imaginable, prediction projected consequences, and valuation made those consequences matter. A rational-choice model helps us state what coherent comparison would require, but it does not by itself describe how that hidden work is actually done. Therefore, this chapter introduces one functional decision loop, one rational benchmark, and three questions—normative, descriptive, and prescriptive—that the rest of the book will keep separate and reconnect.

Learning goals

  • Map a decision as a recursive process rather than a single moment.
  • State the elements of a rational-choice benchmark without treating it as a psychological portrait or a moral theory.
  • Distinguish normative, descriptive, and prescriptive questions about the same decision.
NoteWhere this chapter enters the loop

This chapter introduces the complete navigation system: Context and information → Notice and interpret → Construct options → Predict → Value → Choose, commit and act → Observe and learn. Later chapters slow down particular connections; this chapter keeps the whole process visible.

Return to the hiring committee in the preface. At 3:17 p.m., the chair asks for a vote. The visible question asks for one name, but the decision underneath it is larger. Which qualities were built into the role? Which applicants survived the first screen? What did each interviewer notice? What does “fit” mean? Which observations predict future performance, which express what the organization values, and which merely feel persuasive? What alternatives exist besides an immediate yes or no?

A rational benchmark can make the committee state its candidates, beliefs, values, constraints, and comparison rule. But that benchmark cannot tell us, by itself, why one vivid story dominated discussion, why an early favorite became the reference point, or why junior members withheld dissent after a senior colleague spoke. A scientific account therefore needs all three of the book’s perspectives: a standard for coherent comparison, evidence about how judgments and choices actually form, and methods for improving the process when those two diverge.

The hidden process

When people explain a decision, they usually begin near the end: “I chose the consulting job because it had better prospects”; “We hired her because she was the strongest candidate”; “I bought the product because it offered the best value.” These explanations may contain genuine reasons. They are rarely the whole causal story.

Before the choice became visible, some information entered attention and other information did not. Some alternatives appeared while others remained unconstructed. Some outcomes were vivid while others stayed abstract. Some sources were trusted and others discounted. Identity, emotion, habit, social approval, and the design of the environment changed what felt relevant or feasible.

The visible choice is therefore the tip of an iceberg. Decision science begins by making the process beneath it inspectable.

A functional decision loop

The original loop in Figure 1.1 makes three distinctions visible. Prediction concerns what is true or likely to happen. Valuation concerns how desirable, costly, fair, meaningful, or unacceptable an outcome would be. Choice turns those judgments into commitment, after which action produces consequences and feedback.

The diagram is a functional map, not a claim that people consciously execute fixed stages in this order. Options, predictions, and values can revise one another. Feedback can arrive before an outcome is complete. A practiced response may compress several functions into a moment. Other people and designed environments can affect every function. The map tells us what work a decision somehow accomplishes; it does not yet tell us how the mind accomplishes it or whether the result is coherent.

A rational benchmark

A deliberately thin rational-choice model asks the decision-maker to make five elements explicit:

  1. Feasible alternatives: What can actually be done?
  2. Beliefs or predictions: What consequences may follow from each alternative, and with what uncertainty?
  3. Values or preferences: How desirable or undesirable are those consequences?
  4. Constraints: What limits money, time, authority, capacity, rights, or acceptable risk?
  5. A coherent comparison rule: Given the preceding elements, which feasible option best advances the relevant objectives?

This benchmark is useful because it exposes disagreement. Two colleagues may agree about the forecast but value the outcome differently. They may share values but disagree about the probability of success. They may agree about both and still have different feasible alternatives. Words such as best, fit, and risky often conceal these distinct questions.

The benchmark does not require selfishness or emotional coldness. A person can coherently value family, loyalty, dignity, fairness, environmental welfare, or a moral commitment. Emotion can reveal what matters. Nor does rational choice provide a complete moral theory: a decision can be coherent for an agent while violating another person’s rights. Legitimate objectives, affected parties, and procedural justice cannot be smuggled into a utility score and treated as settled.

Under risk, expected utility gives this benchmark a more formal representation. That model becomes useful in Risky Decision-Making, where probabilities, final consequences, certainty equivalents, and risk premia are developed. Here the simpler point is enough: a benchmark makes assumptions inspectable; it is not a literal account of mental activity.

Three questions of decision science

Normative, descriptive, and prescriptive are not three kinds of people or three kinds of decisions. They are three questions we can ask about the same decision.

Consider hiring. A normative analysis asks which outcomes matter, which criteria are job-relevant, what evidence should influence the choice, and which legal and ethical constraints apply. A descriptive analysis asks whether confidence, similarity, prestige, order, or an early favorite changes how the committee interprets later evidence. A prescriptive analysis asks whether criteria should be defined in advance, work samples compared, judgments recorded independently, and later performance used to test the forecast.

The three questions support one another. A standard without a behavioral account may demand capacities people do not possess. A behavioral regularity without a standard cannot tell us whether anything should change. An intervention without either can move behavior while making the decision worse.

ImportantDo not confuse is, ought, and improvement

What people commonly do is not automatically what they ought to do. What a coherent model recommends need not describe how people actually think. And an intervention is not justified merely because it changes behavior: its objective, evidence, transparency, fairness, autonomy, and unintended consequences still require evaluation.

To decide is to cut off

The English word decide derives from the Latin dēcīdere, meaning “to cut off.” The etymology captures the structure of commitment. To choose one path is also to give up others.

Economics represents that sacrifice as opportunity cost: the value of the best feasible alternative forgone. If a student attends class, the cost is the best alternative use of that time, perhaps work, rest, or a film. If a person accepts one job, the cost is not every imaginable career but the best feasible path that commitment displaces.

This is why option construction precedes good comparison. The visible menu may omit negotiation, delay, a pilot, further search, a hybrid arrangement, or a redefinition of the problem. Yet adding options without structure can also create confusion. A good menu is broad enough to escape false either-or choices and disciplined enough to compare. Chapter 2 turns this principle into a practical tool.

Outcome is not process

A good decision can produce a bad outcome, and a poor decision can produce a favorable one. Uncertainty separates process quality from result quality.

An investor may buy after one enthusiastic post and make money. Another may diversify on defensible evidence and lose during an unexpected market shock. A surgeon can recommend the treatment best supported at the time and still encounter a rare complication. Outcome bias judges the decision mainly by its result; hindsight bias makes the result seem more predictable after it occurs (Baron & Hershey, 1988; Fischhoff, 1975).

Feedback therefore does not automatically create learning. A useful review reconstructs what was known, which options were considered, what was predicted, what mattered, and what could have changed the decision. The dated record protects the earlier judgment from a narrator who already knows the ending. The Narrator After Choice develops that problem; Decision Hygiene turns it into organizational practice.

One architecture across the book

The loop expands when other minds enter.

In a decision, a person manages a model of the situation: what is happening, what is possible, what is likely, and what matters. In persuasion, one person tries to change what another notices, believes, values, or feels able to do. In communication, people make their models mutually testable. In negotiation, interdependent parties construct a joint path while retaining alternatives and the ability to refuse. In behavior and choice design, environments make some paths visible, easy, normal, or difficult.

The practical and ethical question is therefore the same throughout: which part of whose decision process is being changed, on what evidence, toward whose objective, and with what opportunity to question, refuse, or revise it?

The puzzle that follows

The rational benchmark gives the opening question sharper teeth. If the consequences, beliefs, values, and action costs attached to two options were genuinely held fixed, merely changing wording or adding a dominated alternative should not reverse preference. Yet some choices do change. In other cases, what looks irrelevant changes attention, meaning, effort, or the consequence of doing nothing—variables that the thin benchmark omitted.

Chapter 2 examines seven such cases. The aim is not to declare people irrational seven times. It is to ask, case by case, which assumption failed, what entered the process, how strong the evidence is, and what a better decision design would do next.

The hiring decision, mapped once

A marketing director must choose a product manager. The visible decision is a name on an offer letter; the hidden process begins much earlier.

Practice Lab

Choose one consequential decision. First write five sentences without using the chapter’s vocabulary: what happened, why you chose as you did, what you expected, what mattered, and how it turned out. Keep that account unchanged. Then draw the loop from Notice through Learn. Mark the strongest forecast, the most important value, one missing option, the best feasible path commitment would cut off, and one record that would let you judge the process after the outcome. Label one normative, one descriptive, and one prescriptive question.

Take it forward

  • Use this when: a visible choice or outcome makes the process that produced it disappear.
  • Do not infer: that a functional loop is a rigid stage theory, that rationality requires selfish values, or that a favorable outcome proves a good process.
  • Try this next: before commitment, state the feasible menu, the forecast, the values, the sacrifice, and what evidence would change the choice.
