1  Understanding Decision Making

Normative, Descriptive, and Prescriptive Perspectives

“For to be possessed of a vigorous mind is not enough; the prime requisite is rightly to apply it.”

— René Descartes, Discourse on the Method, 1637, Part I, translated by John Veitch

Return to the hiring committee in the preface, just before the chair asks for a vote. Two candidates remain, and the committee wants the person who will contribute most to the organization. Its choice will affect both the work the team can do and the opportunities each candidate receives. Before counting the hands, ask:

How should the committee decide?

A defensible process would identify the feasible candidates and any other feasible course of action, such as extending the search, collecting a work sample, redesigning the role, or making a conditional offer. It would predict the consequences of each alternative using job-relevant evidence. It would clarify what the organization values: immediate performance, learning, leadership, reliability, diversity of expertise, retention, or some combination. It would then choose the feasible alternative expected to produce the most valued consequences.

Now ask a different question:

How will the committee actually decide?

One candidate may have become the early favorite. A vivid story may receive more attention than a reliable but unremarkable indicator. The word fit may mean one thing to the chair and another to the future supervisor. A prestigious employer may be treated as evidence without anyone stating what it predicts. A confident senior colleague may speak first, causing junior members to revise or conceal their judgments. The option of extending the search may never enter the discussion. By the time the vote is called, much of the decision may already have been made.

The committee can agree that it should hire the strongest candidate while allowing prestige, speaking order, or an unexamined idea of fit to determine who looks strongest. A shared intention to decide well has not supplied a reliable process. The first question asks for a standard; the second asks for a causal explanation. The practical challenge is to bring the actual process closer to the standard.

NoteCore Idea

A sound decision needs a standard for judging the options and an account of how people actually judge them. Rational choice helps clarify what is feasible, what may happen, and what matters. Behavioral evidence shows how attention, context, and experience shape those ingredients before anyone announces a choice.

Learning goals

After reading this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish normative, descriptive, and prescriptive questions about the same decision.
  • Explain how feasible alternatives, beliefs about consequences, and values support a rational-choice benchmark.
  • Explain why context-sensitive choice challenges a thin rational model as a stand-alone description without treating every contextual effect as irrational.
  • Map a visible choice onto the process through which it was already being made.

Three questions of decision science

The committee needs to improve its hiring process, but it cannot diagnose a failure merely by restating what a good decision would look like. It must identify how the actual judgment departed from that standard and what could change it. The normative, descriptive, and prescriptive perspectives in Table 1.1 separate these tasks and set different standards of success.

Here, descriptive has a broader meaning than descriptive statistics: it includes explaining and predicting actual decisions (Bell et al., 1988). Counting how many employees retain a pension default documents a pattern; investigating whether effort or perceived endorsement causes that pattern seeks an explanation. Both belong to the empirical side of decision science. In a classification of research methods, the second question would more specifically be called explanatory or causal.

That explanation can inform a prescription. Recommending an enrollment procedure also requires deciding what counts as improvement and assessing the procedure’s benefits and costs. A causal finding that a higher default increases contributions does not by itself establish which default best serves employees with different needs.

For the hiring committee, these questions lead to different work. It must agree on relevant evidence and legitimate objectives, investigate how prestige or hierarchy influences the discussion, and design a procedure that makes those influences open to examination. Recording judgments before discussion, for example, could reveal disagreements that an immediate public vote would conceal.

More agreement or faster voting would not by themselves establish a better hiring decision.

How a decision should be made

If everyone recommends the “best” candidate, apparent agreement can hide disagreement about performance, priorities, or even which options remain open. A rational-choice model makes those ingredients explicit and offers a deliberately thin answer to the normative question. In its most basic form, the decision-maker faces a set of feasible alternatives, ranks them, and chooses a highest-ranked alternative. For decisions whose consequences are uncertain, it is useful to unpack that ranking into three substantive inputs (von Neumann & Morgenstern, 1944; Edwards, 1954).

  1. Feasible alternatives: What courses of action can actually be taken?

  2. Beliefs or predictions about consequences: What may follow from each alternative, with what likelihood, timing, and uncertainty?

  3. Values or preferences over consequences: How desirable, undesirable, important, fair, meaningful, or unacceptable would those consequences be?

A forecast concerns what may follow from each alternative. Valuation concerns how much those consequences matter. Together they support a ranking among feasible alternatives. The committee can then record why it chose as it did and compare its forecasts with later experience.

Judgment includes assessing what may happen (prediction) and how desirable it would be (valuation). Choice selects an alternative, while implementation turns that commitment into action. Implementation connects choice to outcome; review and interpretation turn feedback into learning.

Information also differs from feasibility. The committee may never hear about a candidate it could hire, or may consider someone who cannot accept. Constraints such as budget, law, and time determine what can actually be done; they are already reflected in the feasible set. Chapter 2 shows how to distinguish these limits from assumptions that can be challenged.

The formal conditions for coherent rankings, including transitivity, belong in Appendix A. Risky Decision-Making develops expected utility, one representation of consequence-based choice under risk.

The benchmark is powerful because it separates disagreements that ordinary language often hides. Two colleagues may agree about what matters but disagree about the probability of success. They may agree about the forecast but value the consequences differently. They may agree about both while recognizing different alternatives as feasible. Words such as best, fit, safe, and risky can conceal all three disagreements.

Values may include family welfare, dignity, fairness, environmental consequences, loyalty, moral duties, future generations, or the interests of other affected people. Emotion can provide information about what matters. A choice may consistently advance one person’s objectives while violating another person’s rights. Legitimate objectives, affected parties, and procedural justice therefore cannot simply be hidden inside an unexplained score.

The model is a benchmark, not yet a psychological portrait. It tells us how the ingredients of a coherent consequence-based choice should relate once the alternatives, beliefs, and values have been specified. It does not tell us how alternatives become visible, which evidence receives attention, how that evidence is interpreted, or why beliefs and values change with context. The six examples that follow test those descriptive assumptions.

From a normative benchmark to descriptive predictions

A normative model generates descriptive predictions only after we add assumptions about stability. Suppose that two presentations leave the feasible alternatives, predicted consequences, values, and action costs unchanged. A thin rational-choice account then predicts that the choice should also remain unchanged.

For example, expressing the same outcome as survival or mortality should not reverse preference when the descriptions are understood as equivalent. Adding an inferior option should not improve the attributes of an existing one. Yet a default may change the effort needed to act, and an internal state may change what someone currently values. Those cases require different explanations.

The treatment comparison below tests this prediction by changing the description of a fixed outcome.

When the same consequences yield a different choice

A treatment recommendation should respond to its consequences. Merely switching from the language of survival to the language of mortality leaves those consequences unchanged—yet it may change which treatment people prefer.

McNeil, Pauker, Sox, and Tversky (1982) presented a hypothetical choice between surgery and radiation therapy for lung cancer to ambulatory patients with chronic illnesses, graduate students, and physicians. Different groups received outcome data framed in terms of survival or mortality. The cumulative probabilities and time horizons were complementary, yet surgery was relatively more attractive in the survival frame.

A thin rational model treats the descriptions as different ways of presenting the same consequences. If the event, denominator, horizon, and implications are understood as equivalent, preference should not change merely because one description foregrounds living and another foregrounds dying. The observed shift is therefore a comparatively direct challenge to description invariance.

Complementary descriptions can produce different representations of the same outcome data, and those representations can alter treatment preference. Framing develops the mechanism and the ethical response.

There are other ways a choice can change. In Chapter 11, the subscription itself stays the same while another offer changes what people compare it with. In Chapter 40, a default changes what happens when someone takes no action. A behavioral account earns its place when it predicts which change will matter and under what conditions (Simon, 1955).

How decisions are actually made

When people explain a decision, they usually begin near the end: “I chose the consulting job because it had better prospects”; “We hired her because she was the strongest candidate”; “I bought the product because it offered the best value.” These explanations may contain genuine reasons. They are rarely the whole causal story.

Long before the committee votes, members have selected evidence, interpreted it, considered some candidates more seriously than others, and formed expectations about working together. Discussion can change both those expectations and the importance attached to them. Even after a choice, an unavailable candidate or an unclear offer can prevent the intended action.

Return to the hiring vote. A member may rank reliability first throughout, yet revise her forecast of who would deliver reliably after hearing an interview answer. Another member may hold the same forecast and change what weight he gives to developing new work. The two changes can produce the same vote and call for different responses.

Evidence selection, interpretation, prediction, and valuation can overlap or recur as people talk. The account of how the actual vote formed must allow for the effects of attention, order, and context.

The committee also constructs options and turns choice into action. Reopening the search may change the comparison. Before an offer can become a hire, somebody needs authority to make it and the candidate needs to accept it.

Structuring a Decision develops these ingredients through a choice between job offers: which alternatives are feasible, what each commitment gives up, and which information could change the choice. Making those elements explicit provides a foundation for improving decisions (Milkman et al., 2009).

A decision has a history

The committee’s immediate discussion is only one timescale of explanation. A member’s response to a confident candidate may depend on today’s workload, years of experience with colleagues, and an organization that has repeatedly rewarded a particular style. These are hypotheses to investigate, not causes established merely by telling a plausible story. They also suggest different interventions: reducing distraction, improving feedback, and changing promotion criteria address different parts of the process.

In Behave, Sapolsky (2017) follows behavior backward from the moments before an action through bodily states, development, and evolutionary history. The useful question is how these influences meet in this particular situation. Naming a brain region does not explain why it responded as it did; naming a childhood experience does not establish that it caused today’s choice. Appendix B develops this demand for causal depth. It complements the functional map: the map identifies what the decision process does, while a historical explanation asks how that process acquired its present tendencies.

Evaluate decision quality separately from outcomes

Once an outcome arrives, judging the decision seems easier: success appears to vindicate the choice and failure to condemn it. But a good decision can produce a bad outcome, and a poor decision can produce a favorable one. Rewarding luck or punishing a defensible risk can teach the wrong lesson. A useful review must separate process quality from result quality.

Baron and Hershey (1988) tested whether people could keep that distinction in view. Participants evaluated hypothetical medical decisions and gambles whose risks were described before the outcome. A favorable result made the decision-maker’s thinking seem better than an unfavorable result did, even when the information available at the time of choice was the same. Participants were allowing information the decision-maker could not have known to change their assessment of the earlier reasoning.

This is outcome bias: judging a decision by how it turned out rather than by the information and reasoning available when it was made. Hindsight bias adds a related distortion, making the result seem more predictable after it occurs (Fischhoff, 1975). An unexpected market shock can turn a defensible investment into a loss; a lucky gain cannot establish that an impulsive purchase was well judged.

Feedback therefore does not automatically create learning. A useful review reconstructs what was known, which alternatives were considered, what was predicted, what mattered, and what could have changed the decision. A dated record protects the earlier judgment from a narrator who already knows the ending. Rationalization develops that problem; Decision Hygiene turns it into organizational practice.

The preface’s map sends feedback to five decision functions because a review can change more than a forecast. It may reveal a missed signal, suggest another option, alter the importance attached to a consequence, or change how a choice is carried out. Reconsidering what matters also requires judgment about whose interests count, rather than simply copying whichever action was rewarded.

Learning changes future information as well. An option avoided after a disappointing experience is less likely to be tried again, leaving fewer opportunities to discover that the first result was misleading. Denrell and March (2001) show how adaptive sampling can sustain this kind of error. Current context and information therefore describe the starting point for a particular decision, not conditions that remain fixed across decisions.

Use the decision map across settings

The same questions extend beyond individual choice. Persuasion changes what another person notices or believes; communication makes interpretations open to checking; negotiation lets people construct a path they could not take alone. Throughout, we will ask whose decision is being changed, toward whose objective, and with what opportunity to question or revise it.

The final chapter, Deciding With Data and AI, returns to forecast and valuation. A tool can help anticipate consequences. The people responsible for the decision still need to decide what matters, which actions are feasible, and how they will review the result.

The distinction between a standard and an explanation now gives the committee a practical task. Use Table 1.2 to identify where its process is vulnerable, then select a response aimed at that difficulty. Adding another interview will not settle a disagreement about priorities, just as agreeing on priorities will not repair unreliable evidence.

NoteOther changes in context

Compare changes in representation, options, and the path to action in Table 1.3. The subscription comparison comes from Ariely (2008) and related attraction-effect experiments (Huber et al., 1982). Stated agreement in Johnson and Goldstein (2003) and deceased donation in Dallacker et al. (2024) are different outcomes. The reported increase in clicks followed a bundled redesign (Land & Yuan, 2012).1

News reports in 1997 linked rising Mars-bar sales to publicity around NASA’s Pathfinder mission. Repeated references to Mars offer an intuitive hypothesis: the product name may have become easier to retrieve without any change in the chocolate. The available sales account cannot establish that causal connection (Grant, 1997).2

Berger and Fitzsimons (2008) later used the association to motivate experiments on environmental cues and choice. Priming, Fluency, and Familiarity develops the experimentally grounded account.

Griskevicius and colleagues (2009) independently manipulated an induced state—fear, romantic desire, or a neutral condition—and the appeal used in advertisements for a museum or Las Vegas. Appeals based on behavioral social proof—many people visit—or distinctiveness—stand out from the crowd—changed in effectiveness across the induced states. Fear made behavioral social proof relatively more persuasive and distinctiveness less persuasive; romantic desire produced the opposite pattern in the reported evaluations.

The venue did not change, but students’ persuasion and product-desirability ratings depended on the interaction between the induced motivational state and the appeal.

The experiment illustrates how an option’s appeal can depend on the concerns brought to it. The same venue can promise safety, belonging, novelty, or distinction. A thin model can represent this by changing values, but it does not predict when and how those values will change. Valuation develops state-dependent value.

Practice Lab

Open exercise

Choose one consequential decision that has not yet been finalized.

  1. Write two short accounts. The first answers How should this decision be made? The second answers How is it actually being made?
  2. Identify one normative, one descriptive, and one prescriptive question.
  3. State the feasible alternatives, beliefs about their consequences, and values over those consequences. Show how constraints determine feasibility rather than listing them as another preference input.
  4. Use the six examples as diagnostic prompts. Could framing, comparison, a default, an action path, accessibility, or message–motive fit be changing the choice?
  5. Draw the decision loop. Mark one function where the actual process is most vulnerable and one piece of evidence that would change your current recommendation.

Take it forward

Before calling the vote, the chair could ask: “What are we predicting about each candidate, and where do our judgments differ?” The answer would reveal more than a show of hands. It would expose the assumptions that need attention while there is still time to change the decision.

Chapter practice

Open practice questions

Choose one answer for each of the 5 questions. Complete the set, then select Check answers to see your score and explanations. Each question is worth one point.

1. A team first measures the proportion of employees who retain the preselected pension contribution. It then develops a practical enrollment procedure to help employees choose contributions suited to their goals. Which perspectives correspond to these two tasks?

2. A buyer uses reliable information and her stated priorities to select a supplier. An unforeseeable flood then closes that supplier. What follows about the original decision?

3. A team compares two offices carefully but never considers remote work, although it is feasible. At which stage does the clearest omission occur?

4. Two people can choose either of two jobs. They agree about each job's pay, hours, and risks, but prefer different jobs. Which difference can explain their choices within the rational-choice benchmark?

5. An annual subscription can be described as “€10 per month, billed annually” or “€120 per year.” Which finding challenges description invariance?

0 of 5 answered

Answers and explanations

  1. Question 1: B

    A. Normative, then prescriptive (Incorrect)
    The second classification fits. The first task measures what employees do rather than determining which contribution they ought to choose.
    B. Descriptive, then prescriptive (Correct)
    Measuring actual choices is descriptive; developing practical support for better choices is prescriptive. Descriptive decision research also includes explaining why people choose as they do. Such explanations can inform a prescription without themselves recommending a change.
    C. Descriptive, then normative (Incorrect)
    The first classification fits. The second task concerns a usable intervention; a normative task would specify the standard by which choices should be judged.
    D. Normative, then descriptive (Incorrect)
    The first task measures behavior rather than defining a standard, and the second designs practical support rather than describing existing choices.
    E. Prescriptive, then normative (Incorrect)
    Measuring behavior does not prescribe a change. The second task develops a practical procedure rather than specifying an ideal choice under stated assumptions.

    Review: Chapter 1. Back to question 1

  2. Question 2: E

    A. The buyer should have given every supplier an equal rating. (Incorrect)
    Equal ratings ignore relevant differences.
    B. The buyer's priorities must have changed after the purchase. (Incorrect)
    The outcome supplies no evidence of a preference change.
    C. The flood shows that the buyer assigned incoherent probabilities. (Incorrect)
    An unlikely event can occur under coherent beliefs.
    D. Its quality depends only on the eventual delivery failure. (Incorrect)
    This confuses a bad outcome with a bad process.
    E. Its quality depends on the information available when made. (Correct)
    Evaluate the process using what could reasonably be known then.

    Review: Chapter 1. Back to question 2

  3. Question 3: E

    A. Predicting commuting time (Incorrect)
    The omission concerns an option, not a forecast about it.
    B. Valuing staff satisfaction (Incorrect)
    No evidence says satisfaction was misvalued.
    C. Implementing the selected lease (Incorrect)
    The omission occurred before implementation.
    D. Learning from the office move (Incorrect)
    Feedback comes after this missed alternative.
    E. Constructing the options under consideration (Correct)
    A feasible alternative never entered the comparison.

    Review: Chapter 1. Back to question 3

  4. Question 4: E

    A. The salary each expects the higher-paying job to provide (Incorrect)
    The two people agree about the pay offered.
    B. The working hours each predicts for the two jobs (Incorrect)
    The two people agree about working hours.
    C. The set of jobs each is able to accept (Incorrect)
    Both can accept either job.
    D. The probability each assigns to the stated employment risks (Incorrect)
    Their assessments of the risks are held equal.
    E. The importance each assigns to income relative to free time (Correct)
    Shared forecasts can be evaluated using different priorities.

    Review: Chapter 1. Back to question 4

  5. Question 5: A

    A. Random assignment produces a reliable purchase-rate difference between the two descriptions. (Correct)
    Equivalent descriptions should produce the same choices under description invariance.
    B. Customers purchase more often after the annual fee falls from €120 to €100. (Incorrect)
    The price reduction changes the monetary consequence of subscribing.
    C. Customers purchase less often after learning that fewer services are included. (Incorrect)
    New information about the service can change its valuation.
    D. Customers with different usage needs purchase at different rates under one description. (Incorrect)
    Different preferences can explain this difference without violating description invariance.
    E. Customers purchase at different rates when billing changes from annual to monthly. (Incorrect)
    Payment timing changes the consequences, not merely their description.
