25  Cooperation and Social Preferences

Self-Interest Is Not the Only Payoff

“We are all caught in an inescapable network of mutuality.”

— Martin Luther King Jr., remarks at the Africa Freedom Dinner, 13 May 1959

Two colleagues need each other’s working notes to finish a joint report. Preparing clear notes takes time away from work assessed individually. Each would welcome the other’s help while skipping their own contribution. If both withhold their notes, both lose useful input.

They agree on what working together would require. Their difficulty is that each can gain by letting the other do the work. In the stag hunt in Coordination and Focal Points, confidence that the other person would join made joining worthwhile. Here, confidence that the colleague will help still leaves a reason to withhold one’s own contribution.

Yet people often share the notes, return a favor, or refuse an unfair advantage. Some expect a later benefit; others care about the colleague or believe that helping is the right thing to do. Those differences matter when we try to make cooperation last.

NoteCore Idea

Cooperation can become worthwhile through future interaction, reputation, and rules. It can also reflect concern for others, fairness, and shared obligations. Group identity helps determine whom that concern includes. A durable arrangement needs to take incentives, motives, and the boundaries of “us” seriously.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish a coordination problem from a conflict between individual incentives and collective gains.
  • Explain how continued interaction, reputation, and institutions can support cooperation, including the need for repair after mistakes.
  • Use differences between games to separate strategic incentives, social preferences, and expectations about appropriate behavior.
  • Explain how ingroup favoritism can restrict cooperation and how shared goals and a more inclusive identity can help rebuild it.

When cooperation invites exploitation

The colleagues choose simultaneously whether to share their notes. Table 25.1 represents sharing as Cooperate and withholding as Defect. Its illustrative payoffs combine the benefit of receiving useful input and the cost of providing it.

Defecting raises Player 1’s payoff from 4 to 6 if Player 2 cooperates, and from 0 to 2 if Player 2 defects. It does the same for Player 2. Defection is therefore a strictly dominant strategy for each: it pays more regardless of the other’s choice. The unique Nash equilibrium is Defect–Defect, giving each 2 instead of the 4 each could receive through mutual cooperation.

This is the Prisoner’s Dilemma: individually attractive departures undermine a mutually beneficial outcome. Unlike the stag hunt, no greater confidence in the partner’s cooperation makes cooperating a best response in this one-shot calculation. Something beyond reassurance must change.

Watch the ferry dilemma from The Dark Knight and reconstruct each group’s options and payoff ranking. Does defection dominate, who can communicate, and can the threat be trusted? The fictional scene tests the model’s assumptions.

The opening matrix also leaves out future encounters and concerns beyond the stated rewards. Adding either can change the choice. Repeated interaction and reputation attach later consequences to today’s act, rules alter the conditions of the interaction, and social preferences change what a player values. The optional evolutionary lens examines how the structure of a population can support cooperation over time.

How relationships and rules support cooperation

The future can outweigh today’s temptation

Suppose the colleagues will work together again. Withholding notes may save effort today but cost access to useful notes later. Cooperation can now serve each person’s own interests, provided the future relationship is valuable enough and the response to defection is credible.

Consider an indefinitely repeated version of the opening game. Under a grim-trigger strategy, each player cooperates until someone defects, then defects forever. Defecting brings a larger reward today but replaces future mutual cooperation with mutual defection.

Whether that trade is worthwhile depends on how strongly the future relationship counts, allowing for both the chance of another encounter and the discounting of later payoffs. With the opening payoffs, cooperation can remain a best response when each next round carries at least half the weight of the current round. The optional analysis derives that threshold.

This identifies when cooperation can be sustained by these strategies, not that players will necessarily reach it. The comparison assumes reliable observation and the specified response to defection. Misobserved actions can trigger punishment after an innocent error. A known final round can also unravel cooperation by backward induction when the stage-game incentives and rationality assumptions apply throughout. Experimental evidence reviewed by Dal Bó and Fréchette (2018) makes continuation and incentives central to understanding when repeated cooperation emerges. Reputational links or further relationships can preserve future consequences after one particular project ends.

NoteMathematical analysis: when future cooperation outweighs defection

Consider an indefinitely repeated version of the opening game. Under a grim-trigger strategy, each player cooperates until someone defects, then defects forever. Let \(T\) be the temptation payoff from defecting against a cooperator, \(R\) the reward from mutual cooperation, and \(P\) the payoff from mutual defection. The continuation weight \(\delta\), between 0 and 1, captures how strongly the next round counts, allowing for both the chance of another encounter and discounting of future payoffs.

Continued cooperation yields

\[ \frac{R}{1-\delta}, \]

whereas a one-time defection followed by permanent punishment yields

\[ T+\frac{\delta P}{1-\delta}. \]

For cooperation to remain a best response to the other player’s grim-trigger strategy,

\[ \frac{R}{1-\delta}\geq T+\frac{\delta P}{1-\delta}, \qquad\text{or}\qquad \delta\geq\frac{T-R}{T-P}. \]

With \(T=6\), \(R=4\), and \(P=2\), the threshold is \(\delta\geq0.5\). At \(\delta=0.5\), continued cooperation is worth \(4/(1-0.5)=8\); defecting is worth \(6+0.5\times2/(1-0.5)=8\) as well. At \(\delta=0.75\), cooperation is worth 16 and defection followed by punishment only 12. The current gain of 2 is outweighed by the loss of future cooperation.

Reciprocity needs a way to repair mistakes

Permanent punishment illustrates an incentive; it is a severe policy for working with fallible people. A colleague may fail to send notes because a file was lost, not because they intended to exploit anyone. A useful response needs to deter deliberate withholding while allowing cooperation to recover from error.

Tit-for-tat begins by cooperating and then copies the partner’s previous move. Axelrod’s tournaments made four properties memorable: be nice, be provocable, be forgiving, and be clear (Axelrod, 1984). Here, forgiving means returning to cooperation after the partner does. These properties help explain its success in those tournaments.

An accidental defection can nevertheless send two tit-for-tat players into alternating retaliation. Generous tit-for-tat sometimes cooperates after a defection, while win-stay, lose-shift repeats an action after satisfactory outcomes and switches after unsatisfactory ones. Imhof, Fudenberg, and Nowak (2007), for example, find win-stay, lose-shift rather than tit-for-tat selected above a cooperation-benefit threshold in their particular finite-population evolutionary process. Which strategy succeeds depends on mistakes, the alternatives present, and how success changes future play or population composition.

The “live-and-let-live” system that emerged in parts of the Western Front during the First World War illustrates reciprocal restraint outside a laboratory: units facing one another repeatedly could respond to local restraint and punish violations (Axelrod, 1984). The possibility of a continuing response helped sustain restraint even between enemies.

Reputation and institutions support cooperation

Future consequences need not come from the person we help today. A colleague who shares useful work may later be invited onto another team because others have learned that they can rely on them. Indirect reciprocity operates through that wider reputation, while direct reciprocity operates through another encounter with the same partner (Nowak, 2006).

For a reputation to affect incentives, an action must leave a record that reaches someone who can respond. A rating system, a reference, or informal information sharing can provide that link. The link can also fail: records can be mistaken, selective, or manipulated, and a permanent bad label can block recovery. The design of the record and the possibility of correcting it are part of the cooperation problem.

A social cue can also suggest an audience without creating one. Burnham and Hare (2007) displayed images of Kismet, a robot with humanlike eyes, during a public-goods game. Participants seeing Kismet contributed an average of 5.39 of 10 tokens across six rounds, compared with 4.17 without it—about 29% more.

The comparison did not isolate the eyes from the rest of the robot image. Later meta-analyses, which included this study, found no reliable overall increase in generosity from artificial surveillance cues (Northover et al., 2017). A reputation system still needs consequences that other people can actually impose.

An organization need not wait for colleagues to punish one another. It can recognize useful contributions, make responsibility visible, or require a shared handover before a project is accepted. Contracts and enforcement can similarly change the expected return from keeping an agreement. A penalty for withholding notes, however, needs a clear definition of the required contribution, a proportionate response, and a way to correct a mistaken record. Otherwise the rule can punish a technical failure or a justified refusal to disclose sensitive information.

Rules also communicate what the relationship means. Paying people for helping, fining them for withholding, and recognizing a shared obligation can produce different responses even when the monetary changes seem small. We will return to this issue after considering what people value beyond their own rewards.

A televised choice brings reputation, prior interaction, and immediate incentives together. The game Golden Balls ended with contestants choosing Split or Steal for large and widely varying prizes. Stealing could secure the whole prize against a partner who split; splitting exposed a contestant to leaving with nothing. Before reading the evidence, watch one Split-or-Steal round and an unusual communication strategy. Record the promises, threats, and choices that remain credible.

Van den Assem, van Dolder, and Thaler (2012) report stakes averaging more than $20,000. Many contestants chose Split even though Steal weakly dominated it in immediate monetary payoffs: Steal did better against Split and equally well against Steal. The authors find evidence consistent with reciprocal preferences from earlier interaction, little support in their sample for conditional cooperation based on an opponent’s expected action, and an age-dependent gender pattern.

The choices show cooperation at high stakes in a setting where contestants interacted before choosing and faced a television audience.1

What people value beyond their own payoff

Future consequences explain why helping can serve a player’s own material interests. They do not exhaust the possibilities: a person may share even when doing so brings no later return. A social preference means that utility depends on more than one’s own material payoff. People may care about another person’s welfare, inequality, total surplus, the worst-off person’s outcome, intentions, or obligations to a group.

Two important forms can predict different choices. Altruism, in an outcome-based preference model, gives positive weight to another person’s welfare. Inequity aversion places a cost on unequal outcomes, often distinguishing being behind from being ahead. Helping someone already better off can improve their welfare while widening the gap. Allocation experiments vary such trade-offs to distinguish motives; the optional research track develops the models (Andreoni & Miller, 2002; Fehr & Schmidt, 1999).

Fairness, bargaining, and giving

In the ultimatum game, a proposer divides a sum. A responder accepts, implementing the division, or rejects, giving both zero. A responder concerned only with their own money prefers accepting any positive offer to rejecting it. With a smallest positive monetary unit, and if the responder rejects zero, backward reasoning predicts that smallest positive offer. If zero is accepted, a zero offer can be optimal instead; continuously divisible money has no smallest positive amount.

Classic experiments instead commonly find modal offers around 40–50% and mean offers around 30–40%; offers below 20% are often rejected roughly half the time in multi-study summaries (Camerer, 2003; Oosterbeek, Sloof, & van de Kuilen, 2004).

Rejecting a positive offer costs the responder money. That sacrifice changes the proposer’s calculation: an offer that leaves little for the responder may also leave nothing for the proposer. Fairness can therefore affect the bargain even when the proposer cares only about their own return.

An offer can still mix motives. A proposer may value fairness, fear rejection, follow a norm, or misunderstand responders. A responder may dislike inequality, punish an insulting intention, preserve self-respect, or enforce a norm. Rejection reveals a willingness to sacrifice money, without uniquely identifying the reason.

The linked ultimatum scene provides a communication exercise. Identify the demand, the threatened consequence, and the recipient’s outside options. Then consider whether posing the ultimatum changes only the material menu or also respect, identity, and the perceived intention behind the offer.

A generous ultimatum offer can express care or simply buy acceptance. To test how much acceptance matters, remove the recipient’s power to reject. In the dictator game, the allocator can keep the money without risking that punishment. Removing rejection usually reduces transfers. In classic comparable treatments, average giving is around 20% in Forsythe and colleagues (1994) and closer to 10% under the double-blind procedures of Hoffman and colleagues (1994).

Removing rejection does not remove the rest of the setting. Anonymity, earned entitlement, experimenter observation, and whether the menu allows taking as well as giving can change the meaning of the action. Removing rejection changes the recipient’s power; allowing taking changes the allocator’s options and the meaning of giving.

Reciprocity changes what feels rewarding

Sharing notes may matter because the other person responds in kind. In two fMRI experiments involving 36 women, Rilling and colleagues (2002) found that mutual cooperation with a presumed human partner was associated with stronger responses in parts of the ventral striatum and orbitofrontal cortex than the average of the other three Prisoner’s Dilemma outcomes. Mutual cooperation paid less than exploiting a cooperating partner.

Reciprocated help may acquire value through social connection and expectations of continued cooperation. The repeated-game design leaves both in play. For the colleagues, a reliable exchange can make contributing valuable because of the relationship it sustains as well as the notes it delivers.

The measurement was blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) activity, an indirect indicator of neural activity. It did not measure dopamine release. The findings support a role for reward processing in mutual cooperation. This is the same distinction between brain activity and neurotransmitter release discussed in Chapter 21.

Social preferences can also make another person’s loss attractive. Someone who has been deliberately betrayed may be willing to pay to punish the offender, even when doing so cannot recover the lost money. The prospect of seeing a wrong answered may itself enter the payoff.

De Quervain and colleagues (2004) studied this possibility using positron emission tomography (PET) during a trust game. Their analysis included 14 men who had trusted their partners. After a partner kept money instead of returning a share, participants could assign punishment points. Effective punishment reduced the partner’s earnings; symbolic punishment did not. While participants considered their response, effective punishment produced greater activity in the dorsal striatum, a region involved in reward processing and goal-directed action. Stronger activation was also associated with willingness to spend more of one’s own money to punish.

The authors interpreted this pattern as evidence of anticipated satisfaction from punishment. A monetary sacrifice can therefore be consistent with pursuing something the person values. The term altruistic punishment does not establish a selfless psychological motive: enforcing a cooperative norm and expecting personal satisfaction can coexist.

Anticipation can also differ from the emotional aftermath. In separate behavioral experiments, Carlsmith, Wilson, and Gilbert (2008) found that participants expected punishment to improve their mood. Yet participants given the opportunity to punish, most of whom used it, reported worse mood than those without it. Continued rumination about the offender helped explain the pattern.

For the colleagues sharing notes, this creates a practical tension. Wanting an exploiter to face consequences can motivate enforcement, but the appeal of retaliation does not establish that it will repair the relationship. A useful sanction needs to be judged by what happens to cooperation as well as by how satisfying it seems to impose.

When cooperation is a shared obligation

The colleagues may share notes because they value helping, because they expect a return, or because contributing is understood to be part of the job. These reasons can reinforce one another. They can also come apart: someone may believe colleagues approve of sharing while privately wishing to keep their work to themselves.

Bicchieri (2006) distinguishes empirical expectations, about what others do, from normative expectations, about what others think one should do. Social norms can make behavior depend on both. A shared obligation therefore requires more than a manager announcing a preferred action: the relevant expectations must exist within the group whose conduct or approval matters.

Table 25.2 separates these expectations from private preferences and enforcement. Its purpose is to keep an observed act of helping from being treated as a complete explanation of itself.

Changing a rule may also change its social meaning. Gneezy and Rustichini’s (2000) daycare study found that introducing a fine for late pickup increased lateness in the treated centers; the effect did not simply disappear when the fine was removed. One interpretation is that parents began to treat lateness as a service they could buy instead of an imposition they should avoid. The result makes the relationship between incentives and obligation part of the design problem. The applied notes examine this study alongside fairness in markets and punishment.

Social Norms and Conformity develops how expectations about others form and change. Here, their significance is that people can experience cooperation as something owed to one another, alongside any future reward.

When “us and them” blocks cooperation

An obligation to help still leaves a question: help whom? Suppose the colleagues preparing the joint report belong to departments with a history of rivalry. Each readily shares notes with colleagues in their own department but withholds them from the other. Cooperation is already strong within each team. The difficulty lies at the boundary between them.

Ingroup favoritism means treating members of one’s own group more favorably than members of another. It can shape whose request receives attention, whose mistake gets the benefit of the doubt, and whose success feels worth supporting. Minimal-group experiments show that even arbitrary categorization can produce such favoritism under some conditions (Tajfel, 1970). Chen and Li (2009) find that experimentally induced group identity changes social preferences and strategic responses. A long history of conflict is therefore not necessary for group membership to affect a choice.

Favoritism does not necessarily mean hostility. In a meta-analysis of 212 studies, Balliet and colleagues (2014) found greater cooperation with ingroup than outgroup members. Comparisons with unclassified strangers supported a pattern driven mainly by extra cooperation toward insiders, rather than reduced cooperation toward outsiders. The practical distinction matters: someone can sincerely deny disliking another department while giving their own colleagues preferential help. The size of that difference depends on the task, information, and relationship.

An evolutionary model asks how such selective cooperation could become common. Hammond and Axelrod (2006) model agents that recognize a group marker and can help their own group, other groups, both, or neither. Local interaction and reproduction allow cooperation within a group to spread alongside noncooperation across its boundary. Their ethnocentric strategy means helping agents with the same marker while withholding help from others; it requires neither hatred nor a belief in the group’s superiority. The evolutionary notes below explain how this pattern can emerge.

These are complementary levels of explanation: experiments examine responses to group labels, whereas the model tracks the spread of conditional strategies. The model keeps the rule for identifying insiders fixed. Shared goals matter because people can change which boundary defines the group whose success they value.

Telling rival teams to be friendlier leaves their working relationship largely unchanged. A superordinate goal is valuable to both groups and requires their combined efforts or resources. Both wanting the same prize creates competition; needing each other to achieve a valued outcome creates a reason to cooperate.

Sherif’s (1958) account of the Robbers Cave camp study gives a concrete illustration. Researchers first established two groups of boys, then introduced competitive activities and prizes that generated hostility. Pleasant contact alone did not dissolve the rivalry. Later, the researchers arranged problems requiring joint effort, including restoring a water supply and getting transport working to collect food. Across a series of shared tasks, hostility declined and friendships increasingly crossed the group boundary.

In this staged study with a deliberately homogeneous sample, shared tasks changed both interdependence and social experience: the boys came to need one another and repeatedly experienced being useful to the other group.

Shared work can change who “we” are

A shared task can help through two distinct routes. It can make another group’s contribution valuable to my own success, changing incentives. It can also change how I categorize the contributors: from outsiders whose help I need to members of a team to which I belong. The common ingroup identity model proposes that this broader category can extend favorable treatment to people previously regarded as outsiders (Dovidio, Gaertner, & Saguy, 2009).

Gaertner and colleagues (1990) tested this account with pairs of three-person groups, varying cooperation and whether participants were encouraged to see one group or two. Among participants initially encouraged to see two groups, cooperation strengthened the perception of a single group and reduced evaluative bias. The findings support recategorization as a pathway.

Levine and colleagues (2005) illustrate how the boundary can also shape helping. In one study, football supporters whose club identity had been emphasized were more likely to help an apparently injured stranger wearing their own team’s shirt than a rival team’s or an unbranded shirt. In a second study, emphasizing the broader identity of football supporter extended helping to rival fans, but not equally to people without a football shirt. The two-study pattern suggests that a more inclusive identity can widen concern while leaving a boundary in place.

For the departments, “We produce a report that clients can rely on” becomes credible when each team can see how the other’s contribution improves that report. A shared label may help people recognize the relationship. Successful joint work gives them evidence for it.

This differs from the common knowledge discussed in Chapter 24. Common knowledge concerns what people know about one another’s knowledge; common identity concerns whom they understand themselves to belong with. Everyone may know the plan while still viewing the other department as an adversary.

Building that wider identity need not require either department to abandon its expertise or history. A dual identity preserves subgroup membership within a wider “we”: two distinct departments contributing to one service. Dovidio and colleagues (2009) also warn that an emphasis on sameness can obscure unequal treatment. A common identity is more credible when people can name differences and challenge unfair arrangements without being accused of undermining unity.

For the joint report, a practical rebuilding sequence is:

  • Choose an outcome both groups value. Agree what a reliable report must allow its users to do, and what would demonstrate improvement.
  • Make contributions complementary and visible. Give each department a useful role, recognize both contributions, and align assessment with the joint result.
  • Begin with a manageable shared task. Exchange a small set of notes, complete one report section together, and review whether each side delivered. Repeated reliable contributions can give trust something to rest on.
  • Keep voice and accountability. Agree how to share costs, correct mistakes, and address renewed withholding. A shared identity should support those conversations.

Check whether help actually crosses the old boundary and persists after the initial task. Better relations can make cooperation easier, but they cannot make an exploitative agreement fair or remove a remaining incentive to free-ride.

Building an arrangement people can sustain

Return to the joint report. Individual assessment may reward withholding useful notes; colleagues may doubt that anyone will reciprocate; they may disagree about what can legitimately be shared; or they may reserve help for their own department. Recognizing shared work addresses the first problem, a small reciprocal trial with visible contributions can address the second, and an agreed disclosure rule can address the third. A joint task with complementary roles can help bridge a group boundary.

NoteCompare the conditions for cooperation

The same arrangements can sustain harmful cooperation: firms can maintain a cartel at buyers’ expense, and a project team can reward silence about mistakes. When defining the contributions to support, include their effects on clients and others outside the team. Members need a way to raise concerns, refuse a harmful request, or leave the arrangement without being treated as defectors.

Practice Lab

Use the opening payoff matrix for one anonymous round. Then play four announced rounds with the same partner. Record each action and the action you expected from the partner; afterward, note whether reciprocity, fairness, obligation, or the approaching final round influenced your choice.

Compare the patterns without assuming that repetition caused any difference: familiarity, order, and changing beliefs also changed. Design one additional comparison that separates two explanations—for example, randomizing whether partners remain together or whether contributions are publicly observable. State what each explanation predicts and which result would count against it.

For an application to group boundaries, choose two teams that cooperate internally but struggle to help one another. Propose a superordinate goal and specify each team’s contribution. Separate the incentive change from the identity change you expect: what would people gain, and whom might they begin to count as “us”? Identify observable help across teams and a follow-up check after the task, rather than treating agreement with a team slogan as evidence of restored cooperation.

Take it forward

The colleagues’ difficulty is easier to address once we distinguish the reward for withholding notes, the future value of the relationship, the obligation each feels, and whom that obligation includes. Make useful contributions worthwhile, give people experience of succeeding together, and leave room to repair mistakes. When cooperation stops at a group boundary, ask what credible shared task could make the other side part of “us.”

The next chapter follows another influence of other people: their behavior supplies evidence about what works, what is normal, and what earns approval. Those signals can help cooperation grow, but they can also spread a practice whose value nobody has checked.

Optional research and application notes

NoteAdvanced research track: how cooperative strategies spread

Explaining why a colleague cooperates today differs from explaining why a cooperative practice spreads through a workplace. The first question concerns a choice; the second concerns change over time. The population question complements the reasoning explored in Strategic Interdependence and the equilibrium analysis developed in Coordination and Focal Points.

The comparison in Table 25.4 makes the object of explanation explicit. A population can change because existing members revise what they do, because some members are replaced, or because the rules of the interaction change. None of these possibilities can be inferred from a final pattern alone.

Evolution depends on the transmission process

Evolutionary game theory studies how success in an interaction affects the persistence or spread of strategies. A strategy here need not be a consciously chosen plan: it can be an inherited behavioral rule. Evolutionary stability asks whether a resident strategy can resist a sufficiently rare alternative under the model’s conditions; it is distinct from an individual’s calculation about an opponent (Maynard Smith & Price, 1973).

One recurring condition supporting cooperation is positive assortment: cooperators encounter cooperators more often than random mixing would predict. The model must explain how this pattern arises. Table 25.5 separates how relatedness, repeated encounters, reputation, and population structure can support cooperation (Nowak, 2006).

Kin, repeated contact, reputation, and networks can matter in human settings, but laws, organizations, language, and deliberate design also determine who interacts and which outcomes reproduce.

Spatial models: who encounters whom?

A spatial model adds rules for interaction or movement. Cooperators may persist in clusters where much of the benefit they provide reaches other cooperators. Whether that happens depends on the network, payoffs, and update rule.

Schelling’s segregation model shows another reason to distinguish an individual’s motive from a population pattern: local decisions about neighbors can generate substantial aggregate segregation without a corresponding individual preference for that degree of segregation (Schelling, 1969; Wilensky, 1997). Evolutionary Prisoner’s Dilemma models instead examine payoff-dependent strategy transmission (Wilensky, 2002). These are executable hypotheses under specified rules, not direct evidence about human motives.

Tournament success, repeated-game equilibrium, and evolutionary stability are different criteria. Tit-for-tat’s tournament performance does not make it successful under every population process (Axelrod, 1984; Imhof et al., 2007). A dynamic model needs declared payoffs, initial conditions, transmission or update rules, interaction structure, and time horizon; a stochastic simulation also needs recorded random seeds and independent runs. Appendix B develops these requirements and separates evolutionary function, current mechanism, and normative interpretation.

The ingroup favoritism discussed above has a precise counterpart in the ethnocentrism model. In the NetLogo teaching version, agents inherit a color marker and two rules: whether to help agents of the same color and whether to help agents of a different color (Wilensky, 2003). Helping reduces the donor’s chance of reproducing and raises the recipient’s. Offspring occupy vacant neighboring sites and inherit their parent’s traits, with occasional mutation; immigration and death also change the population.

Local reproduction creates clusters whose members tend to share markers and strategies. Mutual help can sustain these clusters, while selective helpers avoid the cost of helping differently marked neighbors. The marker does not guarantee cooperation: same-marker free riders can exploit a cooperative cluster. In Hammond and Axelrod’s (2006) reported dynamics, selectively cooperative clusters of another marker can spread at those free riders’ expense. Ingroup favoritism can therefore coexist with considerable cooperation while leaving a boundary across which help is withheld.

The connection to common goals has a limit. The model defines an insider by marker equality; it does not model rival departments learning to regard themselves as one team. The shared-goal discussion concerns changing that social boundary, not simply making a fixed ethnocentric rule more successful.

A spreading routine can have several explanations

Suppose sharing notes becomes common across teams. Employees may repeat it after a useful return, copy respected colleagues, or comply with a new rule. Alternatively, teams that share may attract resources and expand while other teams disappear. Table 25.6 separates changes within continuing units from changes in which units remain.

The processes can coexist. Greater prevalence alone establishes neither a particular mechanism nor a desirable result: a routine can spread because it attracts attention, receives a subsidy, or excludes competitors, as well as because it improves joint work.

NoteAdvanced research track: identify social preferences by contrast

Distributional models: inequality aversion

For two people, Fehr and Schmidt’s (1999) model can be written as

\[ U_i=x_i-\alpha_i\max(x_j-x_i,0)-\beta_i\max(x_i-x_j,0), \]

with the standard restrictions \(\alpha_i\geq\beta_i\) and \(0\leq\beta_i<1\). The first penalty represents disadvantageous inequality; the second represents advantageous inequality. Because disadvantageous inequality can hurt more, a responder may reject a low ultimatum offer, while a proposer may share even when rejection is impossible.

Bolton and Ockenfels’s (2000) ERC model emphasizes one’s relative share, while Charness and Rabin (2002) allow concerns for total surplus, the worst-off person, and reciprocity. Efficiency and equality can conflict: giving an extra euro to the better-off person may create several euros of total benefit, while equalization may reduce the sum.

Revealed preference over allocations

A generous choice at one price leaves open how much someone will sacrifice when giving becomes more costly. To predict the next allocation, we need to know whether these trade-offs form a consistent pattern. Andreoni and Miller (2002) gave 176 participants 8–11 allocation choices at varying prices of giving, producing more than 1,600 decisions. Eighteen of the 176 participants violated at least one of the revealed-preference axioms tested. Only three—fewer than 2%—had violations classified as severe using a critical cost efficiency index below 0.95. The small severe-violation rate supports approximate consistency for most participants.

A familiar summary assigns 47.2% to a selfish type, 30.4% to an egalitarian or maximin type, and 22.4% to a utilitarian type. But only 43% fit one of the pure categories exactly; the remaining 57% were assigned to the nearest category. The percentages demonstrate structured heterogeneity.

Outcomes and intentions are different

Outcome-based models ask who gets what. Reciprocity also asks why the other person acted. The same unequal allocation can be judged differently if it was freely chosen, forced by the available menu, produced by chance, or intended to help.

To separate these accounts, suppose a responder faces the same proposed split—€8 for the proposer and €2 for the responder—in two conditions. In one, the proposer freely chose that split while an equal split was available. In the other, a computer assigned the split and the proposer had no control. Keep the responder’s consequences and rejection option unchanged. Record the response, perceived intentionality, expected social approval, and belief about the proposer’s alternatives.

If rejection changes across conditions, a model based only on final payoffs is incomplete. Perceived intent, responsibility, norm violation, or demand may have changed. The additional records reveal which explanation is plausible and which further treatment is needed.

Cross-cultural, comparative, and neural evidence generate hypotheses

Henrich and colleagues (2001) conducted ultimatum, public-goods, and dictator-type experiments across 15 small-scale societies. Behavior varied substantially. In Lamalera, Indonesia, offers were often hyperfair and have been discussed in relation to the cooperative organization of whale hunting. Among the Au and Gnau in Papua New Guinea, some high offers were rejected and have been interpreted in relation to obligations created by accepting gifts.

These comparisons do not isolate culture from recruitment, comprehension, stakes, markets, kinship, or production systems. Meta-analysis likewise finds variation across settings (Oosterbeek et al., 2004). Use it to generate a specific hypothesis about entitlements or expectations, then test a contrast that separates that hypothesis from the other differences.

In a well-known capuchin comparison, a monkey that receives cucumber while a neighbor receives a preferred grape sometimes refuses the cucumber. Inequality aversion, frustration, changed reward expectation, and protest against the experimenter remain competing accounts (Brosnan & de Waal, 2003). Neural studies of inequality and equity–efficiency trade-offs likewise identify correlates consistent with social valuation (Hsu et al., 2008; Tricomi et al., 2010). Comparative and neural evidence should determine the next discriminating contrast, not close the diagnosis.

NoteApplied Lens: measuring and enforcing social meaning

Eliciting norms as a coordination problem

Krupka and Weber (2013) ask participants to rate how socially appropriate an action is, with payment for matching the modal rating of others. The task converts a private opinion into a coordination problem: “Which rating will people in this setting recognize as shared?”

A pedagogical representation is

\[ u(a_k)=\beta\pi(a_k)+\gamma N(a_k), \]

where \(\pi(a_k)\) is the material payoff and \(N(a_k)\) is the perceived appropriateness of action \(a_k\). The weight \(\gamma\), the reference group, and the norm score must be measured.

Dictator variants illustrate why this matters. Giving from an unearned windfall, taking from another person’s endowment, choosing whether to enter the allocation task, or selecting from a menu with a harmful alternative can yield the same final allocation with different social meaning. A model based only on final payoffs misses what the action communicates relative to the alternatives.

Punishment can enforce cooperation—and create another dilemma

People sometimes pay to punish defectors or unfair partners. Punishment can stabilize cooperation when future interactions, reputation, or internalized norms are present. It can also be antisocial, mistaken, retaliatory, or captured by powerful actors.

The anticipated reward from punishment adds a design concern: people may find enforcing a sanction attractive even when its contribution to future cooperation is uncertain.

Before adding sanctions, establish who judges violations, how errors can be appealed, and whether powerful members face the same accountability. Measure repair of cooperation as well as the punishment imposed.

Enforcement can also change what compliance means. The daycare example discussed above (Gneezy & Rustichini, 2000) illustrates how an incentive may change a social obligation into a priced service. Its practical implication depends on why parents responded as they did. A fine that is large, legitimate, and well enforced could deter lateness; a small charge perceived as permission may encourage it. Assess both the price of the behavior and the meaning the rule gives it.

Fairness in markets: entitlement constrains price

A price increase can cover a seller’s higher costs while disrupting a customer’s sense of what is owed. The amount alone may therefore miss what makes a change objectionable. The following comparisons ask how the reason for an increase and the transaction it replaces shape judgments of fairness.

Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler (1986) asked separate samples of respondents to judge market practices. Figure 25.1 shows four selected historical vignettes. The car questions hold the size of the price increase at $200 but vary its relation to list price: one adds a surcharge above list, while the other removes a discount below list.2

Two further scenarios show how judgments depend on the transaction: 74% rated auctioning one scarce doll to the highest bidder unfair (\(N=101\)), and 91% rated a rent increase exploiting a tenant’s location-specific dependence unfair (\(N=157\)). “Unfair” combines the two unfavorable response categories.

The contrast suggests that people protect reference transactions and accepted entitlements while allowing some cost-based adjustments. For managers, explain the changed cost or scarcity, preserve procedural consistency, avoid exploiting a trapped party, and consider rationing rules that remain defensible if customers understand them.
