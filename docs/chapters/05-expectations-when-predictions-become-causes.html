5  Expectations: When Predictions Become Causes

Forecast consequences first; then ask whether the forecast can alter them

“The self-fulfilling prophecy is, in the beginning, a false definition of the situation evoking a new behavior.”

— Robert K. Merton, “The Self-Fulfilling Prophecy”, 1948, p. 195

A manager expects a new employee to need close supervision. She keeps the difficult assignments, asks to approve every small decision, and gives little room for independent work. Three months later, the employee still waits for instructions. The manager feels that her judgment has been confirmed—but how much initiative did the job allow?

Expectations can describe what will happen, and they can also influence what happens. Telling these roles apart changes how we should evaluate a forecast.

NoteCore Idea

A useful forecast names an outcome, a time horizon, and the conditions under which it might occur. In human systems, acting on the forecast may change those conditions. To learn from the result, examine both the prediction and the treatment it prompted.

Learning goals

  • Construct a consequence forecast with a defined option, outcome, time horizon, range, and reference class.
  • Distinguish an external prediction from a forecast that can alter physiology, behavior, treatment by others, or opportunity.
  • Design a supported expectation and an independent test of its effects.

Build the forecast before telling its story

Suppose a leadership team is deciding whether to launch a product in October or delay until February. “October will probably work” is not yet a useful prediction. What counts as working—shipping on time, reaching a revenue target, avoiding severe defects, or retaining customers after three months? Compared with what launch? Over what period? Under which staffing, price, and marketing assumptions?

A consequence forecast should make six elements visible:

The reference class supplies an outside starting point; the causal model explains why the present case might differ. Neither should automatically defeat the other. If comparable launches retain users only 35% of the time, the team needs diagnostic evidence before moving far from that base rate. If this product has a validated feature or a new constraint absent from the reference cases, the inside account may justify an update. Chapters 14 and 15 provide the probability and calibration tools; the present task is to make the forecast answerable to them.

Use ranges or scenarios when one precise number would hide model uncertainty. A base scenario states the best-supported path, an upside scenario identifies the conditions required for a better outcome, and a downside scenario identifies a plausible failure path. Scenarios are not three stories chosen for emotional balance. Each should specify assumptions, an observable sign, and what decision follows if the sign appears.

Forecasting also requires separating prediction from valuation. The estimate that an October launch has a 45% chance of meeting the retention target does not determine whether launching is worthwhile. The answer depends on the value of speed, learning, revenue, reputation, workload, and the harm of failure. Nor does a predictive association establish which intervention will improve the outcome. A team may predict churn accurately without knowing whether price, reliability, support, or customer fit causes it.

One final question changes the rest of the chapter: Can acting on or communicating this forecast change the outcome being predicted? A weather forecast normally changes preparation rather than weather. A forecast about a bank, employee, patient, student, or market may change behavior, resources, and feedback. In those settings, prediction is no longer only a representation of the future. It is a possible input into that future.

When a forecast enters the causal system

The sociologist Robert K. Merton introduced the term self-fulfilling prophecy to describe a false definition of a situation that evokes behavior making the originally false belief come true (Merton, 1948). His classic example was a bank run. If enough people believe a bank is in trouble, they withdraw their money. Their withdrawals then create the very crisis they feared. The belief becomes real through the behavior it causes.

The manager in the opening faces a similar possibility. Her expectation may reduce opportunities for initiative; the employee’s response then seems to confirm what she believed. This does not prove that her original assessment was false. It means the later behavior is not independent evidence of what the employee would have done with more discretion.

Expectations can also prevent their predicted outcomes. A warning about failure may prompt preparation that makes the project succeed. Such a self-defeating prediction can be useful even though the forecasted event never occurs. The key question is whether people reacted in a way that changed the system.

Diagnose the direction of the loop

An anxious presenter expects to look incompetent. That expectation directs attention toward bored-looking faces and makes neutral silence feel like failure. The presenter speeds up, stops asking questions, and avoids eye contact. The audience then disengages, producing the evidence the presenter feared. Merely disputing the initial thought misses the causal chain; changing the sampling or action can test it. The presenter might ask an early question, deliberately scan engaged as well as disengaged faces, or compare audience ratings with the private prediction recorded beforehand.

This yields a better review question than “Was the forecast right?” Ask: Through what actions did the forecast change exposure, effort, resources, or other people’s responses? Then ask what evidence remains independent of that intervention.

Forecasts can alter budgets, assignments, and opportunities as well as personal effort. These pathways help explain why a label such as “promising” or “unreliable” deserves examination: it may influence the conditions under which the person will next be judged.

Expectation in the body

Figure 5.1 is a teaching synthesis, not a claim that every expectation passes through a fixed sequence. One pathway may operate alone, or several may combine. Attention and interpretation can mediate each pathway—for example, symptom monitoring can alter physiology, and a manager’s interpretation can alter resource allocation—but they are not additional outcomes by themselves. The key identification question is which pathway changed what followed (Merton, 1948; Harris & Rosenthal, 1985; Kirsch, 1985).

Placebo effects are changes attributable to treatment context, including expectations and learning, beyond the specific action of the treatment. Nocebo effects are adverse changes attributable to such contextual processes. They can accompany active treatment as well as an inert intervention (Benedetti, 2008; Colloca & Barsky, 2020; Colloca & Miller, 2011). Improvement in a placebo group alone does not establish a placebo effect: natural recovery, regression to the mean, and other influences may also contribute.

Pain research provides one example of plausible pathways. Expectations can affect anxiety, attention to symptoms, and endogenous pain regulation, although the mechanisms and size of effects vary by outcome and procedure (Finniss et al., 2010; Kirsch, 1985). These are specific psychological and physiological processes, not a general capacity to overcome illness through belief.

Risk communication should therefore preserve accurate probabilities and explain what support is available. A clinician can describe both the proportion likely to experience a side effect and the proportion unlikely to experience it, using the same outcome and time horizon. Reassurance should not replace information or imply that every adverse effect is manageable.

NoteResearch Lens: expectations can alter reported experience

Price and product information have changed reported pleasantness, preference, or performance in controlled tasting and consumer tasks (Lee et al., 2006; Plassmann et al., 2008; Shiv et al., 2005). These results make an important possibility visible: information supplied before an experience can help construct the experience rather than merely justify it afterward. They do not show that every price or brand effect is an expectancy effect; demand characteristics, attention, learning, and other mechanisms require discriminating tests. Broader claims that mindsets change health or physiology also need particular care because interventions, outcomes, and replications vary (Crum & Langer, 2007; Crum et al., 2011).

The same distinction applies outside medicine. Information supplied before an experience may influence how it is interpreted, but a pleasant report, a physiological change, and a lasting health benefit are different outcomes. Evidence for one cannot establish the others.

Ability beliefs and performance

A student who treats confusion as proof of inability may avoid the practice that could help. Another who sees confusion as part of learning may seek a new strategy. These are possible pathways from an ability belief to performance, not guarantees that either student will succeed or fail.

Self-efficacy, a concept developed by Bandura, refers to people’s beliefs about their capability to organize and execute actions required to achieve goals (Bandura, 1977, 1997). Self-efficacy affects effort, persistence, resilience, emotional response, and willingness to take on challenge. People with higher self-efficacy are more likely to approach difficult tasks, persist after setbacks, and recover from failure.

Self-efficacy is not the same as empty confidence. It is most useful when grounded in mastery experiences, modeling, feedback, and realistic strategies. False confidence can be dangerous. But low confidence can also become self-defeating if it prevents the very effort that would build ability.

Dweck’s work on implicit theories of intelligence makes a related point. A fixed mindset treats ability as relatively stable; a growth mindset treats ability as developable through effort, strategy, and support (Dweck & Leggett, 1988; Dweck, 2006). Students with growth-oriented beliefs may respond differently to difficulty because failure carries a different meaning. Blackwell, Trzesniewski, and Dweck (2007) found that growth mindset interventions could improve motivation and academic trajectories in some settings.

However, mindset should not be oversold. Meta-analytic work suggests that mindset effects and interventions can be modest and context-dependent (Sisk et al., 2018). Yeager and colleagues (2019) found that a brief intervention improved grades among lower-achieving US ninth-graders and increased advanced mathematics enrollment; the grade benefit depended partly on school context. It was not a uniform improvement for every student. The practical conclusion is not “just believe and success will follow.” A better conclusion is: beliefs about ability matter most when environments provide real opportunities, strategies, support, and feedback.

NoteResearch note: learning that an action can help

Early learned-helplessness research found that animals exposed to uncontrollable adverse events could later fail to escape even when escape was possible (Seligman, 1975). Human theories extended the account to explanations of adversity, including whether a cause is judged stable or widespread (Abramson et al., 1978).

Maier and Seligman’s (2016) later review substantially revised the original animal account: passivity under prolonged aversive stimulation need not itself be learned, while detecting control can recruit processes that protect active responding. This evidence should not be reduced to the claim that a person’s pessimistic beliefs cause their difficulties.

Expectations also shape performance through other people. Rosenthal and Jacobson’s famous study, Pygmalion in the Classroom, suggested that teacher expectations could influence student achievement (Rosenthal & Jacobson, 1968). Teachers were told that certain randomly selected students were likely to show intellectual growth. Those students later showed greater gains, especially among younger children. The interpretation and magnitude of teacher expectancy effects have been debated, but later reviews conclude that expectancy effects are real, though usually more moderate and context-dependent than popular accounts suggest (Jussim & Harber, 2005).

Expectancy effects can operate through multiple channels: teachers may provide more warmth, more challenging material, more response opportunities, more detailed feedback, and more patience to students from whom they expect growth (Harris & Rosenthal, 1985). Students then respond to the richer environment.

The same logic applies to management. Eden (1992) described Pygmalion effects in organizations: leaders’ high expectations can improve subordinate performance when they produce confidence, support, challenge, and opportunity. McNatt’s (2000) meta-analysis found evidence for Pygmalion effects in management contexts. Low expectations can produce the opposite, sometimes called a Golem effect, where negative expectations undermine performance.

The ethical distinction is important. “High expectations” should not mean pressure without support. It should mean credible belief in capacity combined with resources, coaching, autonomy, and feedback. Empty slogans can become blame: “If you failed, you must not have believed enough.” That is not decision science; it is moralized optimism.

For the manager, that means pairing a demanding assignment with clear authority, resources, and a way to obtain feedback.

NoteResearch Lens: identity changes what difficulty means

Expectations can be carried by identities and cultural meanings as well as by individuals. In stereotype-threat research, making a negative group stereotype relevant has sometimes impaired performance by changing the meaning of a difficult task (Steele & Aronson, 1995; Spencer et al., 1999). Anxiety, monitoring, working-memory load, belonging, and motivation are plausible pathways, but effect estimates are heterogeneous across populations, tasks, manipulations, and settings; some operationally plausible estimates are small and evidence of publication bias matters (Flore & Wicherts, 2015; Shewach et al., 2019).

The defensible lesson is conditional. A hard task can mean “I am learning,” “I do not belong,” or “I may confirm what others expect of people like me.” Those meanings can alter attention and action, but a performance gap does not identify stereotype threat as its cause. Structural conditions, prior preparation, discrimination, resources, and measurement remain part of the explanation. Chapter 29 develops identity and culture as hypotheses about meaning; here identity matters because it is one route through which an expectation can change the system.

Expectations between people

Expectations shape not only individual performance but also relationships.

Suppose you expect a colleague to be defensive. You enter the conversation cautiously, use guarded language, avoid warmth, and interpret questions as resistance. Your colleague senses distrust, becomes less open, and responds stiffly. You leave thinking, “I knew they would be defensive.”

But your expectation helped create the interaction.

Snyder, Tanke, and Berscheid (1977) demonstrated this kind of behavioral confirmation in a classic study. Men spoke by telephone with women whom they believed to be either physically attractive or unattractive, based on photographs that were not actually of the women. Men who believed they were speaking with an attractive woman behaved more warmly. The women, responding to that warmth, behaved in ways that independent observers judged as warmer and more sociable. The men’s expectations changed their own behavior, which elicited confirming behavior from the women.

Darley and Fazio (1980) described expectancy-confirmation processes in social interaction. Expectations shape how perceivers act toward targets; targets respond to that treatment; perceivers interpret the response as evidence for their original expectation. The process can happen without conscious intention.

The manager can apply this lesson without assuming the employee is already capable of everything. She could delegate one bounded assignment, specify the result, and agree on when help is needed. The response would then provide evidence about initiative under those conditions.

In a negotiation, the corresponding step might be to ask a useful question while protecting sensitive information. A guarded reply can reflect the other party’s aims, their response to your manner, or both. Changing one’s own behavior creates an opportunity to learn; it does not establish trustworthiness from a single interaction.

Building truthful, supported expectations

An expectation becomes useful when it leads to a specific, supported test. “This employee lacks initiative” is too broad; “Given a clear goal and authority over the method, will the employee propose a workable plan within a week?” identifies both the behavior and its conditions. Record the prediction before the assignment, provide the promised support, and compare the result with the original expectation.

Separate an assessment from a decision about treatment. Evidence that someone currently lacks a skill does not determine whether they should receive training, and a temporary performance problem should not silently become a permanent identity. The relevant question is what opportunity would allow improvement and what evidence would show it.

Support also has to be credible. Telling employees they are trusted while retaining every decision gives them conflicting instructions. In education, medicine, or management, expectations should be paired with resources and honest uncertainty. A disappointing result may reflect the task, material constraints, discrimination, or luck; it cannot be used to conclude that the person failed to believe strongly enough.

Practice Lab

Choose one consequential option and produce a one-page forecast-and-reactivity audit. Define the outcome and deadline, give a range or probability, defend a reference class, name two causal pathways, and state one update trigger. Then ask whether communicating or acting on the forecast can change physiology, the focal person’s behavior, other people’s responses, or resources and opportunities. Add one measure collected before that loop can operate and one comparison that could separate an accurate forecast from an outcome partly produced by the forecast.

Take it forward

The manager’s next review could begin with two records: what she expected, and what responsibility she actually delegated. Comparing them would make the employee’s performance more informative. Whenever a forecast seems repeatedly confirmed, inspect the treatment it prompted before treating the outcome as an independent verdict.
