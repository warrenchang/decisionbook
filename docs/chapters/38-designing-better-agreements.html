38  Designing Better Agreements

Make the promise clear enough to carry out and check

“And Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words, and of no strength to secure a man at all.”

— Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan, 1651, Part II, ch. XVII

The buyer and supplier agree on a promising package: a volume commitment, flexible call-offs, shared capacity investment, and a defined exception to exclusivity. Then the buyer asks for an urgent extra batch. The supplier says the forecast came too late; the buyer says flexibility was the point of the agreement.

Both may remember the deal accurately while assuming different operating rules. The package promises flexibility, yet it leaves the urgent batch uncertain and the parties arguing over who failed. Before signing, they need to decide what counts as an accepted order, who confirms capacity, and what happens when a commitment is missed. This chapter turns a package both sides value into a promise they can carry out and check, adding complexity only when it solves a specific problem.

NoteCore Idea

For each material promise, specify who is responsible, what will be observed, how it will be checked, and what happens when circumstances change or performance fails. These operating decisions make an agreement usable. Additional tools—alternative packages, contingent payments, protected disclosure, or third-party procedures—should answer a diagnosed need.

Learning goals

  • Produce a minimum viable agreement covering ownership, measurement, verification, review, failure, and exit.
  • Diagnose whether unclear priorities, forecast disagreement, sensitive claims, or process failure warrants an optional agreement-design module.
  • Add an advanced term only when its measurement, incentives, authority, and implementation burden are defensible.
  • Evaluate a contingent contract under different forecasts, risk exposures, and incentives, and distinguish mediation from arbitration.

Module 1: Reveal priorities through packages

The supplier can ask whether the buyer values capacity or flexibility more, but a general answer may conceal how much of one the buyer would exchange for the other. Committing to a single offer too early can close off that discovery. Multiple equivalent simultaneous offers, or MESOs, let the parties learn from a choice among workable packages before settling on one.

A MESO is a set of two or more offers made at the same time, each designed to be roughly equal in value to the offer-maker but different in how value is allocated across issues. The other side’s reaction can provide clues to its priorities.

The supplier might offer three packages it has privately valued as roughly equivalent:

  • Capacity package: lower unit price, firm annual minimum, narrow monthly call-off band.
  • Flexibility package: higher unit price, wider call-off band, quarterly forecast commitment.
  • Investment package: middle unit price, buyer contribution to dedicated tooling, longer term, and an exit schedule for unrecovered investment.

The buyer can compare these trade-offs without disclosing its reservation value, and its response gives the supplier a reason to ask which terms mattered. Research comparing MESOs with single-package offers has found economic and relational benefits from offering a choice of packages (Leonardelli et al., 2019). The useful information comes from examining the preference, rather than assuming the first choice reveals a complete ranking of priorities.

Prepare the packages carefully. Approximate equivalence is from the offer-maker’s perspective, not an assurance that the counterpart will regard them as equally attractive. Each must be sincere and understandable; a deliberately unavailable decoy would undermine the exchange.

Consider the service menu in Table 38.4, adapted from the course example. It varies both the start date and whether the customer buys a premium service (Zhang, 2022c).

The menu offers a concrete choice: premium service adds $50,000 in July or $45,000 in March. A customer who selects March with premium service reveals a combination worth investigating. Is the earlier start essential, is the premium valuable, or does the lower price drive the choice?

The prices alone do not establish that the four offers are equally valuable to the provider. The provider must account for capacity, costs, timing, and risk. For example, if the July standard package costs $500,000 to deliver and the March standard package costs $485,000, each leaves $150,000 before other differences. Those cost assumptions are illustrative; they show the calculation needed before using the term MESO. Otherwise, this is a useful package menu without an established equivalence claim.

Return to the supplier’s capacity and flexibility packages. Ask which is closer to the buyer’s needs, then ask why. A preference for flexibility might reflect expected demand variation, reluctance to commit cash, or a hidden approval constraint.

A useful next question isolates one trade: “If we widened the call-off band, could you accept a longer forecast commitment?” The answer gives more information than simply treating the first package choice as a stable priority ranking.

Module 2: Use contingent terms when forecasts differ

Suppose a builder expects early completion while a buyer fears delay. A fixed payment asks them to settle the price of that uncertain performance now, when their forecasts differ. Repeating the forecasts need not bring them closer to agreement. A contingent contract makes a term depend on the future outcome: a bonus for finishing early and compensation for specified delays may offer each something it values, provided the possible payments, risks, and incentives are acceptable.

The construction case starts with a demand made just before signing: substantial penalties if completion is more than a month late. Instead of accepting the demand, rejecting it, or merely reducing the penalty, the builder proposes a larger late penalty paired with a bonus for early completion. The request reveals that timing has value to the buyer, while the builder’s confidence creates an opportunity to exchange a fixed price concession for an outcome-dependent term (Malhotra & Bazerman, 2007). The useful question is what the demand reveals about the problem the other side wants solved.

The main questions concern measurement and control. Define completion so that speed cannot be achieved by omitting quality checks. Specify which delays the builder controls, how external disruptions are treated, who verifies the date, and how a disputed finding can be challenged. A payment tied to a vague “successful launch” simply moves the disagreement into the future.

Contingent terms also create new incentives. A party that controls the measurement or part of the outcome may be able to influence the payment in ways the other did not intend. The contracting and verification burden must be worthwhile relative to a simpler fixed term (Bazerman & Gillespie, 1999).

An author who expects strong sales might accept a smaller advance for a higher royalty. A producer forecasting a television audience share of nine might trade a fixed fee for a ratings bonus with a station forecasting seven. The station’s control over scheduling and promotion then matters: a payment rule should not make suppressing the audience profitable. Outcome-dependent professional fees raise the same questions about who controls effort, what the metric rewards, and which arrangements the applicable professional rules permit.

Compare packages under different forecasts

The MPharm classroom case gives this problem enough detail to work through (Zhang, 2022c). Dr. Chen can sell a patent to iDiet for $20 million, his BATNA. MPharm would obtain a patent worth $20 million without regulatory approval and $120 million with approval. Chen believes approval has a 60% chance; MPharm assigns 10%. These are the case’s different subjective forecasts. Initially assume risk neutrality, reliable payment, no additional costs, and no change in approval chances caused by the contract.

MPharm’s expected gross value is $30 million. A fixed price of $25 million therefore gives each side a $5 million gain relative to its alternative. A contingent package adds an upfront payment and a further payment only if approval occurs. Because Chen is more optimistic, the approval payment is worth more in his calculation than it costs in MPharm’s calculation.

Before reading the explanation, compare packages A–E in Table 38.5. A negative upfront amount means that Chen pays MPharm at signing. All amounts are millions of dollars; Chen’s gain is measured above his $20 million alternative and MPharm’s above its assumed no-purchase payoff of zero.

Package B pays Chen $20 million immediately and $15 million upon approval. Chen values that bonus at $9 million, while MPharm expects it to cost $1.5 million. Both therefore expect to gain more than under the fixed-price package A. Package C moves further toward a lower upfront price: it gives MPharm more expected gain than B but Chen less. Package E improves both parties’ expected monetary gains relative to A, B, and C under the stated assumptions.

These comparisons do not mean the patent has become more valuable in either eventual outcome. The parties disagree about which outcome is likely and therefore price the same risk differently. The mathematical box shows why adding their differently calculated expected gains can produce an apparently larger pie.

NoteMathematical analysis: MPharm’s contingent payments

Let \(X\) be the upfront payment to Chen and \(Y\) the approval bonus, both in millions. With the case’s fixed subjective probabilities and risk-neutral valuations, Chen’s expected gain above his alternative is:

\[ G_C=X+0.6Y-20. \]

MPharm’s expected gain is:

\[ \begin{aligned} G_M &= 0.9(20-X) \\ &\quad +0.1(120-X-Y) \\ &=30-X-0.1Y. \end{aligned} \]

Adding the gains under their respective forecasts gives:

\[ G_C+G_M=10+0.5Y. \]

With a bonus of 100, that sum is 60. Equal expected gains of 30 require \(X=-10\), producing package D. Chen pays 10 upfront and receives 100 upon approval.

With the same probability \(p\) used for both parties, their combined expected gain would instead be \(100p\), independent of the payment split. In either realized state, transfers between the parties cancel. Differing forecasts explain the extra apparent gains from increasing \(Y\); they do not manufacture additional patent revenue.

Check effort, liquidity, and risk

Package D appears especially attractive in the table, but now examine what each party would actually do. MPharm keeps $30 million without approval: the patent’s $20 million value plus Chen’s $10 million upfront payment. It also keeps $30 million with approval: $120 million plus the upfront payment, less the $100 million bonus. Approval adds nothing to MPharm’s own return. If MPharm must spend money or effort pursuing it, the payment design has removed that financial incentive. The fixed approval chances used in the first calculation may no longer be appropriate.

Chen must also be able to pay $10 million at signing. His receipts net of that payment are −$10 million without approval or $90 million with it, whereas selling to iDiet provides $20 million for sure. Credit constraints, risk aversion, or a need for dependable funds can make this exposure unacceptable even when his expected monetary gain is high.

Package E avoids the initial payment by Chen and leaves MPharm $18 million without approval or $38 million with approval. The $20 million difference preserves a financial stake in approval. Whether that stake motivates enough effort depends on the cost of effort and how much it changes the approval chance. The table identifies promising trades; selecting a workable contract also requires those missing facts.

The incentives of an agent deserve the same examination. Chen’s lawyer may receive most compensation upfront while Chen depends on a later bonus. Where professional rules permit an outcome component, fee design can give the agent a stake in the client’s result; oversight and clear responsibilities are still needed when important effort cannot be observed. Align the reward with the desired work, not merely with reaching a signature or reporting a favorable metric.

The buyer expects monthly demand to remain within a narrow band. The supplier expects volatile peaks that would require overtime and displaced orders. Arguing about whose forecast is right will not create capacity. A contingent term can allocate the consequence:

  • The buyer supplies a rolling twelve-week forecast and commits only to the first four weeks.
  • Volume inside the agreed band receives the base price.
  • Volume above the band is accepted only when capacity is confirmed and carries a predeclared surge price.
  • If the buyer’s committed forecast arrives late, the delivery clock moves by the same number of days.
  • If the supplier confirms capacity and then misses a dependency it controls, service credits apply; repeated failure activates cure and exit rights.

This term turns forecast disagreement into a measurable allocation of responsibility. It still needs definitions: what counts as a forecast, confirmation, surge volume, supplier-controlled delay, external shock, and cure? The agreement should avoid cliffs that reward strategic reporting just above or below a threshold. When parties disagree about the future, let future evidence affect terms only when measurement, control, incentives, and enforcement are credible (Bazerman & Gillespie, 1999).

Module 3: Stage disclosure and verification

To specify a meaningful delivery commitment, the supplier needs to know why timing matters. The buyer might explain, “Our customer contract contains penalties,” without immediately disclosing its maximum acceptable loss or price. Begin with information that helps define the problem and invite comparable information in return. More sensitive details can follow when they serve a clear purpose and have appropriate protection. Exact reservation values and confidential alternatives may remain private unless disclosure produces a process, legal, or agreement benefit.

How the supplier responds will affect what the buyer is willing to share next. Using the concern to propose a workable delivery safeguard gives openness a purpose; using it solely to intensify pressure gives the buyer a reason to withhold more. Each exchange should therefore supply information needed to design or check a term, without assuming that willingness to talk makes every claim reliable.

Chapter 33 distinguished perspective-taking from perspective-getting. Imagination can reduce egocentrism and improve strategic thinking, but it can also project one’s own priorities onto the counterpart; direct questions provide new, correctable evidence (Eyal et al., 2018; Galinsky et al., 2008). Ask what a term protects, who must approve it, what risk worries the other side, and what implementation success would look like. Partial or strategic answers remain possible, so verify consequential claims and reward useful disclosure with reciprocity rather than immediate exploitation.

Culture, interpretation, and commitment

A buyer may leave the meeting treating “yes” as a capacity commitment while the supplier intended only to acknowledge the request. The apparent agreement then conceals an order neither side knows how to implement. Culture, profession, organization, and law can change what a move means. Directness can signal efficiency or disrespect; silence can mean refusal, consultation, deference, or thought; a concession can invite reciprocity or threaten face. Cross-cultural negotiation studies document this variation, including differences across settings in how common measures predict joint gains (Brett & Okumura, 1998; Brett et al., 2021).

Return to the Meaning Audit in Culture and Identity. Treat cultural knowledge as a hypothesis and ask how this interaction works:

  • How is disagreement expressed safely here?
  • Who must be consulted before a proposal can become a commitment?
  • Does “yes” mean agreement, acknowledgment, or willingness to continue?
  • Which terms need explicit definition rather than assumed shared meaning?
  • Would a public concession, correction, or apology impose an avoidable face cost?

These questions turn broad cultural knowledge into checks of meaning, authority, and consent in this interaction (Gunia et al., 2016).

The cultural dimensions in Chapter 35 affect the sequence as well as the content of bargaining. One team may want to move quickly to a written contract; another may first seek confidence through introductions, hospitality, and conversation. For example, a Canadian buyer and a Chinese supplier might differ in this way—or have the opposite preferences. Ask what each needs before discussing sensitive terms, rather than assuming a national script.

A handshake can likewise mark a final understanding, agreement on broad principles, or a willingness to begin detailed work. Clarify the commitment explicitly: “Which terms are agreed, which remain open, and what approvals or documents are still required?” A handshake has no single meaning shared by all Western or Middle Eastern negotiators. Its legal effect is also a separate question governed by the relevant circumstances and law.

Record the agenda, use of interpreters, role of private consultation, authorized signatories, and points requiring senior approval. If one side treats a signed contract as a complete allocation of duties while the other expects continued adjustment through the relationship, specify both the initial obligations and the agreed way to revise them. Protocol becomes useful when it tells both sides what happens next.

Verify consequential claims

Clarifying what a counterpart means leaves a further question: is the claim accurate? Chapter 33’s discussion of lie detection explains why confidence, nervousness, and other demeanor cues provide a weak basis for that judgment. In negotiation, the practical task is to identify the evidence needed before relying on a claim.

Negotiation creates incentives to manage information. A person may lie by commission, omit a material fact, answer a different question, or use literally true statements to create a false impression—the practice called paltering (Rogers et al., 2017). A negotiator may also decline to reveal a reservation value, private preference, or confidential alternative without making a false claim.

If the supplier says the forecast arrived after the cutoff, compare the time-stamped submission with the agreed deadline. If the buyer says capacity was confirmed, check the confirmation record. Use those records to establish which obligation was triggered and whether it was met.

For every material claim, ask:

  1. What exactly has been asserted, and what has merely been implied?
  2. What record, date, document, authority, reference, test, or independent source could verify it?
  3. Can the claim become a representation, warranty, milestone, inspection right, or contingent term?
  4. What happens if the claim is mistaken rather than deceptive?

Schweitzer and Croson (1999) found that direct questions reduced deception overall in their first study; in their second, direct questions particularly reduced omission but could increase lies of commission. Therefore ask a precise question and verify the answer when it matters. Good agreement design makes important claims testable.

Module 4: Negotiate the process

A promising package can fail because the wrong people are in the room or because tentative suggestions are treated as commitments. Agree early on who can approve terms, which information is confidential, and when a proposal becomes binding.

For the supplier discussion, the buyer might say: “Could we first list the issues and compare two complete packages? Our planning leads can test capacity, while the commercial sponsors approve any price or volume commitment.” This allows operational knowledge into the search without confusing expertise with authority.

Record how an impasse will be handled as well. A pause for data, an internal approval, or an outside facilitator solves a different problem from another price concession. Revisit earlier assumptions when new information changes the package’s value.

NoteResearch Lens: when direct negotiation needs a third party

When direct bargaining stalls, bringing in a third party can seem like the obvious next step. Yet the parties may want help finding their own agreement or need a final decision they cannot reach together. Choosing a process without resolving that difference can surrender control when assistance was intended, or prolong the dispute when finality was needed. A third party can repair a process or replace it. The mediation–arbitration distinction in Table 38.6 follows the allocation of authority used in the UNCITRAL frameworks (UNCITRAL, 2022).

Mediation is therefore not “soft arbitration,” and arbitration is not “strong mediation.” A mediator can reframe, carry proposals, surface interests, manage difficult interaction, and reality-test a package while leaving acceptance to the parties. An arbitrator receives authority to decide.

Mediation can give a stalled conversation a different structure. The mediator may meet the parties together or separately, help distinguish positions from interests, and ask what each needs from a future working relationship. For the supplier, a payment dispute could conceal an unresolved quality check or unclear acceptance procedure. Clarifying that issue may reveal a settlement that another round of price demands would miss. The parties retain the decision whether to accept.

Final-offer arbitration, sometimes called baseball arbitration, is a particular form of arbitration. Under a whole-package selection rule, each party submits its final proposal and the arbitrator selects one; conventional arbitration is not generally limited to that choice (American Arbitration Association, n.d.). If the final figures are €25,000 and €37,000, that rule requires selecting a submitted figure rather than announcing €31,000 as a compromise. It can encourage defensible proposals because an extreme offer risks losing to the other side’s proposal. The agreed rules must specify whether selection applies to a whole package or separately to issues, what criteria guide the decision, and whether any revision is allowed.

Both mediation and arbitration can address practical relationships and contested rights, but they allocate decision authority differently. Evaluate a proposed process by whether participants can present their concerns fairly, whether the neutral has relevant expertise, and whether the procedure can produce an outcome the parties can implement.

A 2012 review of the preceding decade found diverse goals, settings, mediator strategies, and outcomes, but relatively few controlled studies comparing specific strategies or observing actual mediation behavior (Wall & Dunne, 2012). Choose the process by its task: Do the parties need help making a joint decision, or do they need someone else to make a decision?

A staged commercial clause might move from operational leads, to executive sponsors, to mediation, and finally to arbitration or litigation. It should specify the triggering dispute, decision authority, time limits, interim obligations, applicable rules, and how urgent action can be taken. Make the transfer of control explicit.

Hybrid procedures such as mediation–arbitration offer a settlement opportunity plus finality and may produce perceived time or cost savings in some disputes. They also create a predictable problem when one person performs both roles: information disclosed to a mediator in confidence may appear relevant when that person later becomes the decision-maker. Parties may become less candid or doubt impartiality. Barsky (2013) analyzes these risks in family-law disputes; recent American Arbitration Association commercial-practice guidance identifies the same concerns about candor, ex parte information, informed consent, and the integrity of an eventual award (American Arbitration Association, 2025). If a hybrid is used, the parties should decide in advance whether separate neutrals will serve, what information can cross stages, and whether renewed consent is required before the role changes. Potential speed should not hide a change in authority or confidentiality.

Module 5: Search safely after agreement

Once a deal is secure, both sides may see a further gain. Yet reopening the discussion can threaten concessions they thought were settled. A post-settlement settlement makes that search possible by looking for a mutually preferred replacement while protecting the initial agreement. The essential condition is explicit: the existing agreement remains in force unless every required party voluntarily accepts the replacement, and either party can stop the search without penalty.

The buyer and supplier might discover that a different payment schedule releases cash or reduces risk for both. They can investigate that trade without surrendering the terms already secured. If one side uses the search to reopen settled concessions under pressure, the protection has failed.

Use the procedure only when the original agreement is secure, participants have authority, and the possible gain justifies further effort.

Open the discussion by acknowledging what the first agreement achieved. Invite each side to identify something it would still value, then search for a linked improvement without demanding another unilateral concession. For example: “We have an agreement we can both use. Would you be willing to compare one alternative payment-and-delivery package? We keep the signed terms unless both of us prefer the change.” This invitation makes the protection part of the procedure.

The steps are to acknowledge the agreement, identify remaining interests, develop possible trades, and compare each proposed replacement with the signed package. Record any agreed amendment and who approved it. If either party prefers the original, stop without penalty. That fallback is what separates a post-settlement search from a last-minute attempt to reopen settled terms.

Complete the minimum agreement: implementation and review

Return to the opening disagreement about the urgent batch. “Flexible call-offs” becomes usable when the agreement specifies the permitted volume band, forecast deadline, confirmation procedure, and surge price. A shared record then shows whether the buyer requested capacity, whether the supplier accepted it, and which clock started.

Use the priority map from Chapter 37 to distinguish needs from assumptions. Later payment may protect the buyer’s cash flow, a longer term may support the supplier’s planning, and a service guarantee may protect reliability. Confirm those interests before turning them into contractual obligations.

In this illustrative design, the buyer submits the rolling forecast and the supplier confirms capacity within two working days. Their shared record distinguishes requested volume from accepted orders, so an urgent request cannot silently become a commitment. A monthly operating review examines forecasts, quality, and delays; designated sponsors can revise the agreed bands and investment milestones at a quarterly review.

The parties also specify opportunities to correct a failure, exceptions requiring urgent action, and the route from operational leads to commercial sponsors and any formal dispute process. If the relationship ends, the record must still explain which duties continue and how dedicated-tooling value is recovered. These terms need to fit the parties’ circumstances and governing law, rather than being copied as a complete contract.

Before relying on the signature, ask each side to explain these operating terms and failure rules in its own words. Resolve discrepancies, make material costs visible, and confirm that responsibilities sit with people able to carry them. A contingent bonus must not reward gaming, and a package menu must not hide an unattractive common term; either party still needs a meaningful opportunity to refuse the proposed agreement.

Test the six fields against the opening event: the buyer asks for an urgent batch, capacity is uncertain, and production is approaching. Can both parties identify the next action, who must take it, and which commitment follows? A clear answer shows what the added terms contribute; an unanswered question shows where the agreement still needs work.

Practice Lab

Produce a one-page minimum viable agreement for the supplier obligation most likely to fail. Complete its six fields: owner, measure, verification source, review date, failure rule, and exit. Then stress-test the record against one plausible failure and revise whichever field did not provide a clear response.

Choose no more than one optional module from Table 38.3. State the diagnosed problem that justifies it and add only the information that module requires—for example, two or three sincere packages for unclear priorities, one measurable contingent term for forecast disagreement, or one explicit escalation rule for process failure. If no module is justified, record “none.” Multiple packages, full contingent-contract drafting, independent audits, detailed mediation or arbitration clauses, and multi-shock testing belong in an advanced agreement-design file, not in the minimum exercise.

For an advanced seminar, use MPharm. First evaluate a fixed-price offer and one contingent package privately in each role. Then compare the forecasts and explain why your valuations differ. Reproduce the gains in Table 38.5, inspect MPharm’s payoff in each approval state, and propose an effort safeguard. Finally, compare one improvement with a signed fallback and choose a dispute procedure for a disagreement over whether the bonus trigger occurred. Report the economic calculation and the implementation judgment separately.

Take it forward

Return to the term in your agreement most likely to become disputed. Can both sides tell what was promised, how fulfillment will be checked, and what happens if circumstances change? Add the provisions needed to answer those questions, then check that the extra complexity has not made the agreement harder to carry out.
