27  Markets, Mispricing, and Bubbles

A price can aggregate information, depart from a defensible value model, and feed the expectations that move it next.

“A market in which prices always ‘fully reflect’ available information is called ‘efficient.’”

— Eugene F. Fama, “Efficient Capital Markets”, 1970, p. 383

NoteCore idea

Markets aggregate many strategically interdependent choices, and prices can transmit information quickly enough to guide later decisions. But rapid news incorporation, absence of an easy profit, equality between price and fundamental value, and a speculative bubble are different claims; social learning and risky correction can sustain visible inconsistency. Therefore this chapter separates efficiency, mispricing, and bubbles and treats each as a testable hypothesis about information, incentives, limits to arbitrage, and reinforcing feedback—not as a label proved by a rise or reversal.

WarningEducational analysis, not investment advice

The cases and historical return patterns in this chapter are tools for disciplined inference. They do not identify a security to buy or sell, guarantee that a pattern will persist, or replace a portfolio analysis suited to an investor’s goals, constraints, taxes, costs, and capacity for loss.

NoteApplied module

This chapter applies the book’s core ideas—social evidence, strategic expectations, risk, feedback, and limits on correction—to financial markets. Readers can skip the domain-specific event-study and laboratory-market detail without losing the main social-decision sequence; finance and business courses can use the chapter as a complete applied case.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish rapid information incorporation, no free lunch, fundamental-value accuracy, and bubble dynamics.
  • Evaluate one event study, one limits-to-arbitrage case, one laboratory market, and one field episode without collapsing their evidence.
  • Produce a falsifiable market memo and a two-direction bubble dashboard with a robust action.
NoteWhere this chapter enters the loop

Markets make Predict, Value, Learn, and Coordinate interact at scale. Each price is both an outcome of distributed judgments and new evidence that can alter attention, expectations, financing, and the next round of demand.

Opening puzzle: earnings beat expectations

A listed company reports earnings above the analyst consensus before the market opens. Should you buy immediately, wait, or do nothing?

“Good news means buy” is not yet an investment thesis. The question is not whether earnings were good in isolation. It is whether the announcement was better than the expectations already embedded in the price, whether your order can be executed before prices adjust, and whether the apparent return survives risk and costs.

Write down what your choice requires:

  • Was the information public and unambiguous?
  • At what time did it become tradable?
  • How much surprise was already anticipated?
  • What price reaction do you predict—immediate, delayed, or reversed?
  • Relative to what expected-return model would the subsequent return be abnormal?
  • What spread, fee, tax, liquidity, and position risk would implementation create?

The exercise reveals why behavioral finance is not a catalogue of profitable mistakes. It is a study of how information becomes price, how evidence is tested, and why identifying an error does not imply that it can be safely arbitraged.

Why study behavior in financial markets?

Many criticisms of behavioral research point to small stakes, artificial tasks, or limited opportunities to learn. Financial markets appear to be the hardest place for behavioral effects to survive: stakes are high, feedback is frequent, professional traders are present, and mistakes can be exploited.

That makes markets an informative stress test. If biased judgment still affects investment or price under these conditions, learning and competition have not automatically removed it. But the reverse is equally important: a biased investor does not prove a biased market. Other participants can trade against the error, information can aggregate, and prices can become useful even when the path is psychologically messy.

Conventional finance asks how assets and liabilities should be allocated across time and uncertainty under stated assumptions. Behavioral finance asks how people and markets actually behave when attention, beliefs, reference points, social learning, overconfidence, and constraints enter the system (Barberis & Thaler, 2003). The two perspectives need each other. A descriptive anomaly is visible only relative to a benchmark.

The €20 note and three meanings of efficiency

The familiar joke says that a financial economist would not pick up a €20 note because, in an efficient market, someone would already have taken it. But information is not free. People search precisely because a reward may remain.

The efficient market hypothesis concerns the incorporation of a specified information set into prices, conditional on a model of expected returns (Fama, 1970, 1991):

  • Weak form: the information set includes historical prices and returns.
  • Semi-strong form: it includes publicly available information.
  • Strong form: it includes all information, even private information—a much stronger benchmark.

Three claims are easily confused:

  1. Information is incorporated quickly. Price responds to a specified signal.
  2. No free lunch. A public rule does not reliably earn abnormal return after appropriate risk and realistic costs.
  3. The price is right. Market price equals a well-defined fundamental value.

The first two do not automatically establish the third. A price can be difficult to beat and still differ from a later estimate of fundamental value. Conversely, a price can be noisy without offering a low-risk implementable profit.

Figure 27.1 makes the €20-note intuition concrete using the photograph supplied for this book. Its separation of the three claims is the point: finding an unclaimed reward and establishing fundamental-value accuracy are different evidentiary tasks.

Grossman and Stiglitz (1980) expose the paradox. If prices revealed all costly information perfectly, nobody would pay to collect information; if nobody collected it, prices could not reveal it. Informational efficiency is better understood as an equilibrium in which information acquisition is costly and informed trading is rewarded enough to occur—not as costless omniscience.

Fast price discovery is possible

Public news can move prices extraordinarily quickly, but an announcement timestamp is not evidence of that reaction. The US Federal Reserve’s policy statement establishes 14:00 on September 18, 2013 as the public release time; by itself, it does not show whether or how quickly prices and trading changed. In an event study, the release anchors event time. Separate high-frequency price and volume data must establish the market response.

Price discovery can also aggregate dispersed clues. The space shuttle Challenger exploded at 11:39 a.m. on January 28, 1986; the news appeared on a major financial wire minutes later. Four publicly traded contractors were connected to the launch. By the end of the trading response, Morton Thiokol experienced the distinctive loss. A presidential commission identified the failure of booster O-ring seals made by Thiokol months later. Maloney and Mulherin (2003) found no simple news story or obvious insider-trading trail that fully explained the early discrimination. The market appeared to combine dispersed information, inference, and trading.

But “the market knew” is too strong. Price discovery can be correct for the wrong reason, and a single case does not show that markets always identify responsibility. It establishes a possibility: collective trading can reveal information no single public announcement states.

The wisdom of a market depends on conditions also familiar from crowd judgment: dispersed information, some independence, incentives to reveal beliefs, and an aggregation mechanism. If participants copy the same signal or face the same constraint, the crowd can amplify error instead.

Every efficiency test is a joint test

To label a return abnormal, we must first say what return was expected. Suppose an asset earns 12%. That is not evidence of mispricing until we specify its risk, exposures, time period, benchmark, and costs.

Fama (1991) emphasizes the joint-hypothesis problem: a test evaluates market efficiency together with an asset-pricing model. A rejection can mean:

  • information was not incorporated efficiently;
  • the expected-return model omitted a priced risk or exposure;
  • the implementation assumed impossible timing or ignored costs;
  • the sample or statistic was selected after seeing the data;
  • or some combination of these.

This is why “prices changed after the event” and “I can profit from the event” are separate claims.

Event studies: turning a story into a test

An event study estimates security returns around a defined event (MacKinlay, 1997). A credible design makes the inferential chain explicit:

  1. Define the event and its first public time.
  2. Specify an estimation window for the expected-return model.
  3. Specify an event window in which reaction is measured.
  4. Compute abnormal return as actual return minus expected return.
  5. Aggregate across securities or time into average abnormal returns or cumulative abnormal returns.
  6. Test uncertainty and inspect confounding events.

If prices incorporate a clean public surprise rapidly, abnormal returns should cluster near the release. A delayed pattern can indicate slow updating—but also a weak benchmark, overlapping news, trading frictions, or a sample construction that would not have been available in real time.

Figure 27.3 makes an important boundary visible. A price reaction is observed; an abnormal reaction exists only relative to the expected-return model. Continued separation after the event is evidence to explain, not an automatically tradable return.

Historical research on earnings announcements documented post-earnings-announcement drift: firms with high unexpected earnings continued, on average, to outperform firms with low unexpected earnings after the immediate announcement response in the studied samples (Rendleman et al., 1982). The pattern challenges the joint model used in the study. It does not guarantee that a modern trader can observe the surprise, transact at the assumed price, and earn the reported return after risk and costs.

Return to the opening puzzle. A good answer may still be “buy,” “wait,” or “do nothing,” but it must now name the information, expected-price response, benchmark, implementation assumptions, and evidence that would falsify the thesis.

CautionEvidence Boundary: an anomaly is not a mechanism or a trade

A documented return pattern challenges a specified benchmark; it does not say whether the source is behavioral error, omitted risk, friction, model misspecification, or data mining. Finance offers thousands of securities, variables, transformations, and subperiods, so out-of-sample, after-cost, and post-publication evidence matters (Harvey et al., 2016). McLean and Pontiff (2016) found that average predictability declined after publication, consistent with both correction through trading and bias in initial discovery. A good company, a low price, and a high future return remain three different judgments.

Limits to arbitrage: seeing an error is not enough

The textbook arbitrageur buys an underpriced substitute and sells an overpriced one, locking in convergence. Real mispricing is rarely so clean. Shleifer and Vishny (1997) show why informed traders may be unable or unwilling to correct it:

  • Fundamental risk: the asset’s underlying value can worsen before convergence.
  • Noise-trader risk: mispricing can become larger before it becomes smaller.
  • Horizon risk: clients or managers may withdraw capital after interim losses.
  • Short-sale constraints: borrowing can be unavailable, recalled, or expensive.
  • Model risk: the apparent mispricing may be the analyst’s error.
  • Transaction and funding costs: spreads, market impact, leverage, and margin calls can dominate.

Professional incentives can shorten the horizon precisely when a long-horizon correction is expected. An arbitrageur can be right eventually and insolvent first. Behavioral market outcomes therefore require both a source of biased demand and a reason better-informed capital does not fully offset it.

The Palm–3Com case: visible inconsistency, costly correction

On March 2, 2000, 3Com sold about 5% of its Palm subsidiary and announced an intention—subject to conditions—to distribute the remaining Palm shares to 3Com shareholders at roughly 1.5 Palm shares per 3Com share. Ignoring timing, taxes, and frictions, the relative-value relation was:

\[ P(3Com) \approx 1.5P(Palm) + \text{value of 3Com's other businesses}. \]

After Palm’s first trading day, observed prices implied that the residual 3Com businesses were worth about negative $22 billion in Lamont and Thaler’s (2003) calculation. Simple addition makes that hard to defend. Yet Palm shares were scarce to borrow, shorting was costly and risky, and the distribution was delayed and conditional. The case is unusually strong evidence of relative-value mispricing—and equally strong evidence that a visible inconsistency need not create a riskless free lunch.

A bubble is a reinforcing-process hypothesis

Fundamental value is not printed beside the price. It depends on uncertain cash flows, risk, discounting, and a terminal value. Let \(D_t\) denote the discount factor appropriate to a cash flow at horizon \(t\), and let \(P_T\) be the ex-dividend terminal price. A deliberately simplified discounted-cash-flow model can discipline the debate:

\[ V_0=\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_tE(CF_t)+D_TE(P_T). \]

Here \(D_t\) must incorporate the timing and risk relevant to that claim. Equivalently, one can discount risk-adjusted certainty-equivalent cash flows at risk-free rates. Discounting risky expected cash flows at a risk-free rate without such an adjustment would overstate value. But correct notation must not create false precision. Before calling a price irrational, state the growth, margin, risk-adjustment, discount-rate, and terminal assumptions that would make it reasonable. A bubble diagnosis becomes more plausible when recent gains increase expectations of further gains, attract attention and financing, induce demand or strategic resale, and thereby validate the forecast temporarily (Shiller, 2000; Greenwood & Shleifer, 2014).

Extrapolation, social transmission, leverage, liquidity, strategic resale, supply constraints, and limits to arbitrage can each strengthen the loop. None is necessary in every episode. A boom can begin with genuine innovation and later acquire a feedback component. Fundamentals and speculation are not mutually exclusive.

Strategic resale and synchronization risk

A buyer can believe that a price exceeds their own estimate of fundamental value and still purchase because they expect to resell to someone else at a higher price. The payoff then depends not only on cash flows but on beliefs about later buyers’ beliefs and on exiting before the correction. This makes a bubble partly a problem of strategic interdependence.

Skeptical traders face the mirror problem. Even when many privately judge the price fragile, each may be uncertain about when the others will act. Selling or shorting too early can be costly, and funding or career pressure can force an informed trader out before convergence. Abreu and Brunnermeier (2003) formalize how this synchronization risk can delay correction. Shared doubt is not the same as coordinated correction.

One field episode: dot-com innovation and extreme expectations

From the January 3, 1995 close of 743.58 to the March 10, 2000 peak close of 5,048.62, the NASDAQ Composite rose about 579%. By October 9, 2002, it closed at 1,114.11—a 77.9% decline from the peak (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, 2026). Young internet firms faced genuine innovation, profound valuation uncertainty, restricted share supply, disagreement, and optimistic growth narratives (Ofek & Richardson, 2003).

The internet was not imaginary. The mistake would be to infer that every firm exposed to a transformative technology deserved every price. Contemporaneous belief that valuations were high did not force exit: resale expectations, fear of missing out, institutional career pressure, and uncertainty about timing could sustain participation. The episode is useful because the fundamental and feedback stories coexist.

One laboratory market: a known risk-neutral benchmark can still be exceeded

Field data rarely reveal fundamental value with certainty. Laboratory markets can define the dividend distribution, asset life, information, cash, short-selling rules, and trading institution. In the classic Smith, Suchanek, and Williams design, a finite-lived asset paid a dividend after each period. If expected dividend is \(d\) and \(k\) periods remain, the risk-neutral expected-dividend benchmark without final redemption is \(FV_t=kd\). This benchmark is transparent, but it is not a unique subjective value for participants with different risk preferences. Prices in many baseline markets nevertheless rose above the benchmark and later fell (Smith et al., 1988; Porter & Smith, 2003).

The experiment shows that ambiguous fundamentals are not necessary for bubble-like paths. Repeated experience often reduces bubbles in the same design, while changed environments can rekindle them; learning is representation-specific rather than universal immunity (Dufwenberg et al., 2005; Hussam et al., 2008).

Different strategies can sustain the same path. Passive trading can remain broadly consistent with fundamental value; feedback trading can follow price changes; a speculator may buy above a current value estimate when expecting a still higher next price. NoteResearch Lens: what laboratory markets can transport

Laboratory markets identify mechanisms by changing conditions that field markets rarely hold constant. Bubble-like deviations have appeared with known, declining fundamentals and also with flat fundamentals; raising cash relative to asset value can enlarge deviations (Caginalp et al., 1998). Forecast data show that traders’ expectations can lag turning points and help explain price paths (Haruvy et al., 2007; Holt et al., 2017).

The design strength is control over fundamentals, liquidity, experience, and trading rules. The boundary is transport: participants, stakes, institutions, leverage, information, and asset supply differ from field markets. A laboratory result can rule out one proposed necessity or estimate the effect of a randomized design change. It does not by itself establish the cause, prevalence, or magnitude of a mechanism in a particular historical boom.

A two-direction bubble dashboard

Do not ask only “bubble or not?” Record a probability range and update it downward as willingly as upward.

Write both the fundamental story that would justify the price and the feedback story that would make it fragile. For each, name a discriminating observation. Even a high bubble probability does not reveal timing. Robust responses—limiting leverage and position size, stress-testing funding, preserving liquidity, and precommitting review conditions—may dominate a confident directional bet.

CautionBubble, crash, and systemic crisis are different claims

A crash can occur without a prior bubble when fundamentals or discount rates change sharply. A price correction becomes a systemic crisis only when leverage, maturity mismatch, balance-sheet exposure, networks, or confidence propagate losses widely enough to impair financial intermediation or real economic activity (Brunnermeier & Oehmke, 2013).

Policy should therefore target the mechanism rather than the label: disclosure targets information problems; underwriting standards, capital buffers, and leverage limits target fragility and loss propagation; consumer protection targets burden and informed choice. Each intervention carries false-positive costs from constraining a sustainable market and false-negative costs from leaving a dangerous mechanism unaddressed. Ask who receives the upside, who can exit, who carries the downside, and whether losses are absorbable or propagated through obligations to others.

Investor behavior: the decision process matters

Investment mistakes often combine mechanisms already developed in the book. The concepts do not change when they enter a market; what changes is that prices, other traders, implementation costs, and limits to arbitrage mediate their consequences:

  • Overconfidence creates narrow ranges, concentrated positions, and excessive trading.
  • Availability and attention make vivid news or salient stocks feel more diagnostic.
  • Representativeness turns a good company story into a high-return forecast.
  • Confirmation and hindsight protect a thesis after disconfirming evidence.
  • Mental accounting isolates positions and creates a disposition effect.
  • Loss aversion and narrow bracketing make frequent evaluation painful.
  • Social learning and cascades can turn price appreciation into apparently independent evidence that others know more.

Barber and Odean (2000) found that the individual investors who traded most in their brokerage sample earned substantially lower net returns than those who traded least. The result is consistent with overconfidence plus trading costs; it is not a claim that every trade is mistaken or that passive investing fits every objective.

The discipline is to apply the process–outcome separation from How Decisions Should Be Made—and How They Actually Are. A well-reasoned decision can lose because risk materializes. A poor decision can win because luck is favorable. In finance, outcome-only learning is especially dangerous because volatile prices generate many convincing but noisy stories.

A falsifiable investment memo

Before the outcome is known, write one page:

  1. Decision and noninvestment option. What exactly will be done, in what size, and why is doing nothing inferior?
  2. Thesis. State the causal claim in one sentence.
  3. Embedded expectations. What does the current price already require the firm or market to achieve?
  4. Base rate and comparison class. What happened in similar situations?
  5. Drivers. Name the two or three variables that must move.
  6. Disconfirmation. What observation would make the thesis materially less plausible?
  7. Risks and dependence. Which exposures could fail together?
  8. Implementation. Include spreads, liquidity, taxes, financing, capacity, and time horizon.
  9. Position and exit rules. Precommit a maximum exposure, review date, and conditions for reducing, adding, or closing.
  10. Evaluation. Separate frequent risk monitoring from the horizon on which performance will be judged.

Then red-team the memo. Look for an unfalsifiable escape (“the market will recognize it eventually”), a sunk argument (“we have already lost too much to sell”), a missing denominator, a selected backtest, or a position too large for the uncertainty.

The memo cannot remove risk. It creates an honest record of what was believed, why action was justified, and what should be learned.

Practice Lab

Prepare a two-page fictional market memo. Page one defines one event study: event time, expected-return model, windows, confounds, tradable timing, and costs. Page two applies the bubble dashboard to the same market, states a fundamental story and a feedback story, gives each a probability range and discriminating observation, and chooses a position limit or noninvestment action that remains defensible if timing is wrong. Exchange memos and red-team the process, not the person.

Take it forward

  • Use this when a price pattern, apparent mispricing, or boom is being converted into an investment or policy claim.
  • Do not infer fundamental accuracy from fast price discovery, a free lunch from visible inconsistency, or reversal timing from a bubble diagnosis.
  • Try this next by writing the rival fundamental and feedback stories before choosing exposure, leverage, and review conditions.

Prices can coordinate dispersed action without a single commander. Organizations also coordinate through leaders, roles, meetings, and assigned responsibilities. The next chapter asks when these concentrated social signals improve coordination and when they suppress evidence, dissent, ownership, or action.
