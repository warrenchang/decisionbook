36  Preparing and Claiming Value

Prepare, open, respond, move, and close without losing the walk-away discipline

“Now the general who wins a battle makes many calculations in his temple ere the battle is fought.”

— Sun Tzu, The Art of War, ch. I, §26, translated by Lionel Giles, 1910

1. Prepare: the case before the vocabulary

Imagine that you are selling a used car. Your best alternative is another buyer who has made a firm offer of €8,000. The current buyer has not named a number. You want a better price, yet a low opening could make a small improvement feel like a victory even when it leaves you worse off than accepting the offer already available. Refusing every price below your ambition creates the opposite risk: losing a deal that would improve on that alternative. Preparation must separate what protects you from what you hope to achieve before the conversation supplies its own reference point. Before reading on, write down:

  1. the lowest amount you would accept;
  2. what you would like to obtain;
  3. what you think the buyer might pay;
  4. what evidence could change each number; and
  5. whether you would make the first offer.
NoteCore Idea

Know what you can do without the deal before deciding what to accept. That alternative gives you a walk-away point; evidence about the market and the other party helps you set an ambitious target. During bargaining, compare offers with that preparation and revise your estimates when new information warrants it.

Learning goals

  • Produce a preparation sheet containing BATNA, reservation value, target, estimated ZOPA, standards, and learning questions.
  • Make or answer a first offer using independent analysis and a defensible rationale.
  • Conduct and audit a concession sequence from opening through close.

The five answers describe different parts of preparation. Start with the firm €8,000 offer: if this negotiation fails, you can accept it instead. That course of action is your BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement (Fisher et al., 2011).

Your reservation value is the point at which a deal is as good as that alternative. With identical payment, timing, risk, and selling costs, it is €8,000. If the competing offer involves travel, uncertain payment, or other costs, account for those differences before comparing prices. A BATNA is an available course of action; its value in this negotiation must be assessed.

Your target, or aspiration, is what you hope to obtain. It might be €9,800 if comparable sales and the car’s condition support that amount. The target guides your requests; the reservation value guards against accepting less than the alternative is worth.

The buyer has an alternative and a reservation value too, although you may know little about them. When acceptable prices overlap, there is a zone of possible agreement, or ZOPA. We will calculate it below. In a negotiation involving several issues, that zone is a set of packages rather than a single price interval.

A credible alternative is an important source of bargaining power because it makes refusal possible. It is not the only source: information, timing, authority, resources, and the rules of the negotiation also shape what each party can do.

The negotiation before the negotiation

Before meeting the buyer, confirm that the competing offer is still available and on what terms. Gather comparable sales, inspect any condition differences, and decide which facts you need to learn. Preparing this evidence gives your target a basis and helps you recognize when an attractive price comes with an unattractive condition.

Preparation also includes authority and process. If someone else must approve the sale, agree on the limit before the conversation. If an inspection could change the price, decide how that information will be handled rather than discovering the rule under pressure.

A complete preparation test

Prepare at least one tough question you would prefer not to hear, then answer it truthfully without surrendering private information. Also prepare one question whose answer could make you abandon your favored strategy. That second question protects preparation from becoming confirmation bias.

Map the bargaining zone

A distributive negotiation occurs when parties divide a resource and their interests are directly opposed on at least one issue. The clearest example is a single-issue price negotiation.

Continue the used-car case, assuming all non-price terms are equivalent. The seller’s competing offer makes the reservation value €8,000. Suppose the buyer’s reservation value is €10,000: above that, the buyer prefers another option.

The bargaining zone, also called the zone of possible agreement or ZOPA, lies between €8,000 and €10,000. Prices strictly inside that interval are better than the alternatives for both sides; an endpoint leaves one party indifferent. At €8,000, the seller is just indifferent between selling and walking away, while the buyer gets a very favorable deal. At €10,000, the buyer is just indifferent between buying and walking away, while the seller gets a very favorable deal. At €9,000, the surplus is split evenly.

The bargaining surplus is the overlap between the parties’ reservation values. In this case:

\[ \text{Bargaining surplus} = \text{buyer reservation value} - \text{seller reservation value} = €10{,}000 - €8{,}000 = €2{,}000. \]

If the final price is €9,200, the buyer’s surplus is:

\[ \text{Buyer surplus} = \text{buyer reservation value} - \text{settlement} = €10{,}000 - €9{,}200 = €800. \]

The seller’s surplus is:

\[ \text{Seller surplus} = \text{settlement} - \text{seller reservation value} = €9{,}200 - €8{,}000 = €1{,}200. \]

The total surplus remains €2,000, but the distribution changes. The seller claimed €1,200 of the surplus; the buyer claimed €800.

A positive surplus means mutually beneficial prices exist. If the seller instead requires €12,000 and the buyer will pay at most €10,000, there is no acceptable price overlap. Another issue might change that calculation, but pressure alone cannot make an inferior deal worthwhile.

BATNA, aspiration, and bargaining power

Keep the reservation value, target, and opening offer separate. In our example, €8,000 protects the seller’s alternative, while a €9,800 target directs the effort to claim part of the €2,000 surplus. Research on aspirations supports the importance of targets (White & Neale, 1994).

An opening offer should have a defensible relationship to that target and the available evidence. Asking too little can leave value unclaimed; asking an implausible amount can undermine credibility or end the discussion. Fast acceptance can prompt regret and a review of your preparation.

This regret is sometimes called a “winner’s curse” in negotiation advice. The classic auction concept is different: winning a common-value asset can be bad news because the winning bid may reflect the most optimistic valuation error. Keeping the concepts separate prevents a moment of bargaining regret from becoming a claim about auction theory.

Estimate the other side

Your alternative tells you when to walk away, but it does not tell you how much the buyer would pay. Guess too low and you may offer away value; guess too high and you may mistake a binding limit for a bargaining move. The buyer’s maximum is therefore a question to investigate. Comparable cars, urgency, inspection results, and stated constraints can support a range; record why you consider that range plausible.

Ask questions whose answers could change the range: “Which alternatives are you comparing?” or “How did you arrive at that price?” The buyer may decline to answer, and an answer may require verification. Hesitation, confidence, or irritation is especially ambiguous; it should not become a price detector.

Update when the content warrants it. A documented repair requirement changes the package; a small concession may reflect a limit or simply a bargaining strategy. Recording what would change your estimate helps prevent every response from confirming the number you guessed first.

Reservation value is private by default, not sacred in every case

Revealing an exact reservation value usually weakens value claiming because it tells the other side how far it can push. Yet secrecy is not an absolute rule. A credible disclosure can sometimes correct a mistaken estimate, prevent an avoidable impasse, satisfy a transparency obligation, or make a multi-issue agreement possible. The decision is not “honest or secretive”; it is whether this particular disclosure improves the chance of an informed agreement without unnecessarily surrendering value.

If disclosure is useful, explain the underlying constraint and evidence before giving a naked number. “Our board-approved ceiling is €10,000 and we cannot exceed it” is more informative than “€10,000 is my bottom line,” but it should be said only if it is true and if the strategic and relational benefits exceed the cost. Never fabricate a reservation value or alternative.

2. Open: anchor only when you can defend the frame

Suppose the buyer begins at €6,500. Even knowing your €8,000 alternative, you may now feel pulled toward explaining why the car is worth more than €6,500. The proposed number has become the reference point for discussion.

Experiments show that first offers can anchor settlements (Galinsky & Mussweiler, 2001), extending anchoring research on numerical judgment (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). A number can direct attention and also signal what the speaker expects. The effect is not solely a failure to adjust: an offer carries strategic information, too.

That creates a trade-off. An informed opening can shape the discussion, while a poorly informed one may reveal that you would accept far less than the counterpart was prepared to offer. Before choosing to open, consider how much you know and what your number would disclose.

3. Respond: return to independent analysis

Suppose the buyer opens at €6,500. The seller’s most costly response is to become outraged and begin negotiating inside the buyer’s frame. Pause. Return to the €8,000 alternative, the €9,800 target, comparable listings, condition, and the estimated buyer ceiling. Ask for the basis of the number only if the answer could teach you something; otherwise label the gap and re-anchor promptly:

“€6,500 is outside the comparable range and below a firm alternative I already have. Based on the car’s condition and recent listings, €9,800 is the appropriate starting point.”

A strong response does four jobs: it refuses the anchor, supplies an independent standard, places a new reference point, and gives the other side a face-saving explanation for moving. Precision can signal informed calculation when the assumptions justify it; excessive precision can look theatrical or uninformed (Mason et al., 2013). A narrow range may combine ambition with flexibility, but both endpoints anchor. If €9,800 is the target, “€9,800 to €10,200 depending on timing and payment” is safer than a range whose lower endpoint concedes the target (Ames & Mason, 2015).

If the first offer is unexpectedly attractive, do not invent conflict. Pause long enough to inspect hidden conditions, other issues, and implementation. Immediate acceptance can create regret on the other side or leave important terms undefined. A considered acceptance is still acceptance.

4. Move: make concessions carry information

Concessions change terms and teach the counterpart how to bargain with you. A large immediate move can imply that the opening lacked legitimacy. Two unreciprocated moves can teach that waiting produces value. A silent concession can disappear into the new baseline.

Use four disciplines:

  1. Make movement reciprocal. Do not negotiate against yourself.
  2. Label what you give. Make the cost and significance visible without exaggeration.
  3. Make movement conditional. “If payment is completed this week, I can move to €9,500.”
  4. Connect movement to information or value. A concession should respond to a changed term, verified constraint, or reciprocal move—not discomfort alone.

Shrinking concessions can signal an approaching limit. Across seven studies, recipients of decreasing concessions made less ambitious counteroffers and, in one interactive study, reached worse deals; the disadvantage fell on the recipient, not the sender, and a pre-set target was protective (Tey et al., 2021). Treat the pattern as communication and ask whether each move is deliberate, credible, and connected to exchange.

Silence can create time for deliberation and sometimes improve value creation, but it can also signal disrespect, anxiety, or disengagement (Curhan et al., 2022). Make the pause recoverable: “I want a moment to compare that with the full package.” Deadlines require the same discipline. Ask what changes at the deadline and for whom. Time pressure can accelerate agreement, but delay costs are rarely symmetric (Stuhlmacher et al., 1998).

5. Close: test the agreement, not the relief

Suppose the buyer offers €9,400 with payment this week. The seller should compare the whole agreement with the €8,000 alternative, not compare €9,400 with the emotionally salient €9,800 target. Before closing, confirm the condition of the car, payment method, transfer date, documents, and whether either side has added a new term. A midpoint is not fair merely because it is a midpoint; inspect the legitimacy of the endpoints.

Closing also means stopping. Do not let a late “nibble” convert relief into another unilateral concession. Link any new request to a reciprocal adjustment. If no package beats the BATNA, walk away. No deal can be the correct result of excellent preparation.

Practice Lab

Run the used-car negotiation in pairs. Before speaking, each side submits a preparation sheet with BATNA, reservation value, target, estimated counterpart limit, evidence, opening decision, and planned questions. Record every offer, concession, condition, pause, and justification. Afterward, calculate each party’s surplus; mark where an anchor changed an estimate; and evaluate economic outcome, information quality, relationship, ethics, and implementation separately.

Take it forward

A persuasive first number can make the rest of a negotiation revolve around it. Prepare your alternative and reservation value before that number arrives, then judge each package against what you could actually do without agreement. An accepted offer still needs to earn its place in that comparison.
