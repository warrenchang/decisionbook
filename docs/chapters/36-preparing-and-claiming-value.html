36  Distributive Negotiation

Preparing and Claiming Value

“Now the general who wins a battle makes many calculations in his temple ere the battle is fought.”

— Sun Tzu, The Art of War, ch. I, §26, translated by Lionel Giles, 1910

Imagine that you are selling a used car and already hold a firm offer of €8,000. A second buyer wants to negotiate but has not named a price. You hope for more, while knowing that you can return to the first offer.

Once bargaining starts, that comparison can become surprisingly easy to lose. A low opening makes each increase feel like progress, even if the eventual price remains below €8,000. Refusing anything below your hoped-for price creates the opposite risk: losing a deal that improves on your alternative. Before the conversation begins, separate what you need to protect from what you hope to achieve. Write down:

  1. the lowest amount you would accept;
  2. what you would like to obtain;
  3. what you think the buyer might pay;
  4. what evidence could change each number; and
  5. whether you would make the first offer.
NoteCore Idea

Know what you can do without the deal before deciding what to accept. That alternative gives you a walk-away point; evidence about the market and the other party helps you set an ambitious target. During bargaining, compare offers with that preparation and revise your estimates when new information warrants it.

Learning goals

  • Produce a preparation sheet containing BATNA, reservation value, target, estimated ZOPA, standards, and learning questions.
  • Make or answer a first offer using independent analysis and a defensible rationale.
  • Conduct and audit a concession sequence from opening through close.

1. Prepare: the case before the vocabulary

The five answers describe different parts of preparation. Start with the firm €8,000 offer: if this negotiation fails, you can accept it instead. That course of action is your BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement (Fisher et al., 2011).

Your reservation value is the point at which a deal is as good as that alternative. With identical payment, timing, risk, and selling costs, it is €8,000. If the competing offer involves travel, uncertain payment, or other costs, account for those differences before comparing prices. A BATNA is an available course of action; its value in this negotiation must be assessed.

Your target, or aspiration, is what you hope to obtain. It might be €9,800 if comparable sales and the car’s condition support that amount. The target guides your requests; the reservation value guards against accepting less than the alternative is worth.

The buyer has an alternative and a reservation value too, although you may know little about them. When acceptable prices overlap, there is a zone of possible agreement, or ZOPA. We will calculate it below. In a negotiation involving several issues, that zone is a set of packages rather than a single price interval.

A credible alternative is an important source of bargaining power because it makes refusal possible. It is not the only source: information, timing, authority, resources, and the rules of the negotiation also shape what each party can do.

Work out what the alternative is worth

The car example is simple because the alternative is another offer for the same car. Comparing different jobs or houses requires more care. Begin by listing available alternatives, comparing their full consequences, and selecting the best one. Then ask what terms in the current negotiation would make you indifferent between agreement and that alternative. The following classroom examples use illustrative amounts (Zhang, 2022a).

A job in Copenhagen. Suppose the alternative is to return home and live with your parents while continuing your search. After considering housing costs, independence, work experience, and the job itself, you judge that an annual salary of $17,500 would make taking the job equally attractive. Returning home is the BATNA; $17,500 is the salary threshold for this particular job. An alternative need not be another job offer to affect the terms you should accept.

Tom’s two job opportunities. Tom can work at McDonald’s for $1,600 a month or remain unemployed. He prefers the job. He also values the training and benefits of an IT position at $500 a month more than those of the McDonald’s job. An IT salary of $1,100 would therefore make him indifferent between the two packages. Now suppose another employer offers $2,500 a month with the same non-salary benefits as the first IT position. That offer becomes Tom’s BATNA, and his reservation salary in the original negotiation rises to $2,500. A stronger alternative changes the threshold; merely feeling more confident does not.

The dream house. You value one house at up to $550,000 relative to staying where you are. A second house is worth up to $410,000 to you and is available for $350,000. Before reading on, decide the most you should pay for the first house, assuming comparable transaction costs and no other relevant differences.

The second house provides a $60,000 gain over your current situation. The dream house must preserve that gain to be as attractive, so its reservation price is $490,000. Paying $550,000 would use up all its value relative to staying put while ignoring the better alternative. Confirm that the second house is actually available at the quoted price; an agent’s estimate is weaker than a firm offer.

NoteMathematical analysis: comparing unequal alternatives

For Tom, assuming the stated dollar valuations capture all relevant differences, the reservation salary in dollars per month is:

\[ 1{,}600-500=1{,}100. \]

For the house buyer, the gain from the alternative is:

\[ 410{,}000-350{,}000=60{,}000. \]

The reservation price for the first house is therefore:

\[ 550{,}000-60{,}000=490{,}000. \]

These examples express differences in a common monetary measure. When important consequences cannot reasonably be priced, compare complete packages and identify unacceptable conditions explicitly rather than inventing a precise cash equivalent.

Prepare your own side before approaching the table

Before meeting the buyer, confirm that the competing offer is still available and on what terms. Gather comparable sales, inspect any condition differences, and decide which facts you need to learn. Preparing this evidence gives your target a basis and helps you recognize when an attractive price comes with an unattractive condition.

Preparation also includes authority and process. If someone else must approve the sale, agree on the limit before the conversation. If an inspection could change the price, decide how that information will be handled rather than discovering the rule under pressure.

Improve the alternative while it is still possible to do so: seek another buyer, obtain another quote, test another supplier, or extend an offer’s validity. Record its expiry and the cost of exercising it. A competing offer that disappears tomorrow provides different leverage from one available throughout the negotiation. Likewise, accepting the first package above your reservation value can end the search before a better attainable package has been considered. The reservation value is an acceptance threshold, not the target.

Prepare at least one tough question you would prefer not to hear, then answer it truthfully without surrendering private information. Also prepare one question whose answer could make you abandon your favored strategy. That second question protects preparation from becoming confirmation bias.

Map the bargaining zone

A distributive negotiation occurs when parties divide a resource and their interests are directly opposed on at least one issue. The clearest example is a single-issue price negotiation.

Figure 36.1 shows the bargaining zone in the used-car case, assuming all non-price terms are equivalent. The seller’s competing offer makes the reservation value €8,000. Suppose the buyer’s reservation value is €10,000: above that, the buyer prefers another option.

The bargaining zone, also called the zone of possible agreement or ZOPA, lies between €8,000 and €10,000. Prices strictly inside that interval are better than the alternatives for both sides; an endpoint leaves one party indifferent. At €8,000, the seller is just indifferent between selling and walking away, while the buyer gets a very favorable deal. At €10,000, the buyer is just indifferent between buying and walking away, while the seller gets a very favorable deal. At €9,000, the surplus is split evenly.

The bargaining surplus is the overlap between the parties’ reservation values: €2,000 in this case. Changing the price reallocates that surplus. At a settlement of €9,200, the seller receives €1,200 above the competing offer, while the buyer pays €800 less than its maximum. The negotiated gain is different from the final price.

NoteMathematical analysis: dividing the bargaining surplus

The total surplus is the buyer’s reservation value minus the seller’s:

\[ €10{,}000-€8{,}000=€2{,}000. \]

At a settlement of €9,200, the buyer’s surplus is its reservation value minus the price:

\[ €10{,}000-€9{,}200=€800. \]

The seller’s surplus is the price minus its reservation value:

\[ €9{,}200-€8{,}000=€1{,}200. \]

The two shares add to the total bargaining surplus. These comparisons assume the non-price terms are equivalent.

A positive surplus means mutually beneficial prices exist. If the seller instead requires €12,000 and the buyer will pay at most €10,000, there is no acceptable price overlap. Another issue might change that calculation, but pressure alone cannot make an inferior deal worthwhile.

Assess bargaining power and the information to disclose

Keep the reservation value, target, and opening offer separate. In our example, €8,000 protects the seller’s alternative, while a €9,800 target directs the effort to claim part of the €2,000 surplus. Research on aspirations supports the importance of targets (White & Neale, 1994).

An opening offer should have a defensible relationship to that target and the available evidence. Asking too little can leave value unclaimed; asking an implausible amount can undermine credibility or end the discussion. Fast acceptance can prompt regret and a review of your preparation.

An excessively aggressive proposal can produce a chilling effect: the counterpart treats it as evidence that further discussion is pointless or disrespectful and leaves. A higher demand therefore need not yield a better outcome. Choose an ambitious request that you can explain, with room for reciprocal movement, rather than treating extremity itself as a strategy.

A quick “yes” can make you wonder whether a higher request would also have been accepted. Negotiation advice sometimes calls this regret a “winner’s curse,” but the classic auction concept is different: winning a common-value asset can be bad news because the winning bid may reflect the most optimistic valuation error. Keeping the concepts separate prevents a moment of bargaining regret from becoming a claim about auction theory.

Your alternative tells you when to walk away, but it does not tell you how much the buyer would pay. Guess too low and you may offer away value; guess too high and you may mistake a binding limit for a bargaining move. The buyer’s maximum is therefore a question to investigate. Comparable cars, urgency, inspection results, and stated constraints can support a range; record why you consider that range plausible.

Ask questions whose answers could change the range: “Which alternatives are you comparing?” or “How did you arrive at that price?” The buyer may decline to answer, and an answer may require verification. Hesitation, confidence, or irritation is especially ambiguous; it should not become a price detector.

Update when the content warrants it. A documented repair requirement changes the package; a small concession may reflect a limit or simply a bargaining strategy. Recording what would change your estimate helps prevent every response from confirming the number you guessed first.

Revealing an exact reservation value usually weakens value claiming because it tells the other side how far it can push. Yet secrecy is not an absolute rule. A credible disclosure can sometimes correct a mistaken estimate, prevent an avoidable impasse, satisfy a transparency obligation, or make a multi-issue agreement possible. The decision is not “honest or secretive”; it is whether this particular disclosure improves the chance of an informed agreement without unnecessarily surrendering value.

If disclosure is useful, explain the underlying constraint and evidence before giving a naked number. “Our board-approved ceiling is €10,000 and we cannot exceed it” is more informative than “€10,000 is my bottom line,” but it should be said only if it is true and if the strategic and relational benefits exceed the cost. Never fabricate a reservation value or alternative.

2. Open: anchor only when you can defend the frame

The anchoring mechanism from Chapter 11 now has direct consequences for who receives the surplus. A first offer supplies a reference point before the other side has necessarily settled on its own.

Suppose the buyer begins at €6,500. Even knowing your €8,000 alternative, you may now feel pulled toward explaining why the car is worth more than €6,500. The proposed number has become the reference point for discussion.

Experiments show that first offers can anchor settlements (Galinsky & Mussweiler, 2001), extending anchoring research on numerical judgment (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). A number can direct attention and also signal what the speaker expects. The effect is not solely a failure to adjust: an offer carries strategic information, too.

That creates a trade-off. An informed opening can shape the discussion, while a poorly informed one may reveal that you would accept far less than the counterpart was prepared to offer. Before choosing to open, consider how much you know and what your number would disclose.

Shell (2006) recounts Raymond Chandler’s first salary discussion for Double Indemnity: unfamiliar with Hollywood, Chandler asked for $150 a week when the filmmakers had contemplated $750. In the anecdote, they arranged representation instead of exploiting the low request. Shell also tells of Einstein naming $3,000 when the Institute for Advanced Study recruited him, then receiving a much higher offer. These reported stories illustrate both the cost of poor market information and a counterpart’s possible interest in a lasting relationship.

The same error needs no famous participant. In a classroom example (Zhang, 2022b), a graduate asks for $110,000 without discovering that comparable entrants at the firm receive $125,000. Naming a number confidently cannot replace researching the compensation range. Gather evidence about the relevant job, market, and complete package before deciding whether to open.

A campaign photograph and the counterpart’s opportunity

An often-retold case concerns Theodore Roosevelt’s 1912 campaign, which prepared publicity material before securing rights to a photograph. Facing costly delay or replacement, the campaign appeared to have little leverage. In the account discussed by Michael Wheeler, the campaign manager approached Moffett Studios by emphasizing the exposure the photograph would receive and asking what the studio would pay for that opportunity (Harvard Business School Online, 2023).

The instructive move is to examine the counterpart’s possible benefit as well as your own predicament. It is also a useful ethics discussion: a favorable description can conceal a material dependency. Ask which facts the studio would need to evaluate the proposal and which omissions could mislead it.

The four cells in Figure 36.2 show why “always make the first offer” is too crude: opening can create leverage, but it also discloses the quality and limits of your preparation.

Use Table 36.2 to pair each possible opening strategy with the risk you need to test. These are conservative preparation heuristics:

If you cannot assess your information or the counterpart’s, ask and investigate before committing.

A rationale gives the counterpart something to examine. “Comparable sales, adjusted for the recent service, put my asking price at €9,800” is more informative than a bare demand. Use accurate comparisons that the counterpart can check. Keep the distinctions in Table 36.3 explicit: an opening offer aims to shape the discussion, while the reservation value determines when agreement is no better than walking away.

Precise offers and range offers

How a number is expressed can communicate how it was formed. Precise offers can suggest informed calculation and produce smaller adjustments than round offers (Mason et al., 2013). Excessive precision can instead suggest poor judgment to experts; Loschelder and colleagues (2016) found that a credible rationale could reduce this problem. Show the actual comparison or calculation that supports a figure. Pretending to calculate merely adds a misleading signal.

Precision can also reflect a seller’s willingness to trade price for speed. In millions of online bargaining interactions, Backus and colleagues (2019) found that listings at multiples of $100 received lower offers but attracted offers sooner and were more likely to sell than nearby precise listings. The authors interpret round-number asking prices as signals of willingness to cut the price to sell sooner. A seller’s preferred price format depends partly on the cost of waiting.

A range offer supplies two anchors. The distinction in Table 36.4 matters more than merely using a range. Suppose a caterer would otherwise ask $100 per person; the examples follow the offer types studied by Ames and Mason (2015).

Across the authors’ studies, some bolstering ranges improved economic outcomes without the relational costs of a comparable increase in a single demand. A backdown range can instead advertise willingness to concede. The authors identified widths of roughly 5%–25% as conventional in their settings and found limited additional benefits from much wider ranges. An already extreme demand does not become persuasive by extending it into a range. For a buyer, the more ambitious direction is toward a lower price.

A range can also describe a real choice across terms: “€9,800 to €10,200, depending on timing and payment.” Specify which package earns which price, so both endpoints have a meaning beyond bargaining posture.

3. Respond: return to independent analysis

Return to the buyer’s €6,500 opening. If the seller starts from that number and argues for a modest increase, the discussion can drift below the firm €8,000 alternative. Pause to compare the offer with that alternative, the €9,800 target, and the market evidence. Ask what supports the buyer’s number if the answer could change your estimate. Otherwise, explain the gap and give an independently supported counteroffer:

“€6,500 is outside the comparable range and below a firm alternative I already have. Based on the car’s condition and recent listings, €9,800 is the appropriate starting point.”

A strong response does four jobs: it refuses the anchor, supplies an independent standard, places a new reference point, and gives the other side a face-saving explanation for moving. Thinking about your own target and the counterpart’s alternatives can counter the pull of a first offer (Galinsky & Mussweiler, 2001). Ask for a rationale when it might supply useful evidence, but do not let a prolonged debate over an unsupported opening displace your independent comparison.

If the first offer is unexpectedly attractive, do not invent conflict. Pause long enough to inspect hidden conditions, other issues, and implementation. Immediate acceptance can create regret on the other side or leave important terms undefined. A considered acceptance is still acceptance.

4. Move: make concessions carry information

A concession is meant to bring agreement closer. It also teaches the counterpart how to bargain with you: a large immediate move can imply that the opening lacked legitimacy, while two unreciprocated moves can teach that waiting produces value. The seller may give up money without gaining a commitment or learning anything new. Make each move communicate what changed and what reciprocal response would justify it.

Use four disciplines:

  1. Make movement reciprocal. Do not negotiate against yourself.
  2. Label what you give. Make the cost and significance visible without exaggeration.
  3. Make movement conditional. “If payment is completed this week, I can move to €9,500.”
  4. Connect movement to information or value. A concession should respond to a changed term, verified constraint, or reciprocal move—not discomfort alone.

Shrinking concessions can signal an approaching limit. Across seven studies, recipients of decreasing concessions made less ambitious counteroffers than recipients of constant concessions or a single large concession. In one interactive study, they also reached worse deals than recipients of a single concession; the disadvantage fell on the recipient, not the sender, and a pre-set target was protective (Tey et al., 2021). Treat the pattern as communication and ask whether each move is deliberate, credible, and connected to exchange.

For example, a seller might move from €9,800 to €9,500, then €9,350, then €9,300 as the buyer improves payment and collection terms. The reductions become smaller, but the sequence alone cannot establish the seller’s true limit. More numerous concessions are not automatically better: unnecessary rounds can cost time, and an immediate concession may be appropriate when a genuine error is discovered. A non-price addition, such as including spare equipment, is another trade to value rather than proof that price has reached an absolute floor.

The amount surrendered also does not determine satisfaction by itself. Ask whether the counterpart understands what changed, considers the process fair, and can explain the result afterward. Conceding repeatedly to purchase goodwill can leave both value and expectations poorly managed.

Silence can create time for deliberation and sometimes improve value creation, but it can also signal disrespect, anxiety, or disengagement (Curhan et al., 2022). Make the pause recoverable: “I want a moment to compare that with the full package.” Deadlines require the same discipline. Ask what changes at the deadline and for whom. Time pressure can accelerate agreement, but delay costs are rarely symmetric (Stuhlmacher et al., 1998).

5. Close: check the terms before accepting

Suppose the buyer offers €9,400 with payment this week. The seller should compare the whole agreement with the €8,000 alternative, not compare €9,400 with the emotionally salient €9,800 target. Before closing, confirm the condition of the car, payment method, transfer date, documents, and whether either side has added a new term. A midpoint is not fair merely because it is a midpoint; inspect the legitimacy of the endpoints.

If the buyer offers €6,000 and the seller asks €10,000, “split the difference” produces €8,000. If the buyer had opened at €8,000, the same phrase would produce €9,000. The rule rewards whichever opening first moved the endpoints in that party’s favor. A midpoint may close a reasonable gap, but the arithmetic does not justify the numbers being averaged.

A late request for an extra benefit changes the package you have just evaluated. Compare the revised terms with your alternative and, where appropriate, ask for a reciprocal adjustment. If no available package beats that alternative, walking away is a successful use of the preparation.

Practice Lab

Run the used-car negotiation in pairs. Before speaking, each side submits a preparation sheet with BATNA, reservation value, target, estimated counterpart limit, evidence, opening decision, and planned questions. Record every offer, concession, condition, pause, and justification. Afterward, calculate each party’s surplus; mark where an anchor changed an estimate; and evaluate economic outcome, information quality, relationship, ethics, and implementation separately.

Use the same debrief for a real-estate exercise: compare the settlement with each role’s confidential reservation value, rather than ranking negotiators by sale price alone. Identify one assumption you revised, one question you failed to ask, and one concession that obtained nothing in return. Revisit your opening decision in light of what you knew at the time.

Take it forward

A persuasive first number can make the rest of a negotiation revolve around it. Prepare your alternative and reservation value before that number arrives, then judge each package against what you could actually do without agreement. An accepted offer still needs to earn its place in that comparison. The next chapter asks whether a better package becomes available when the negotiation includes more than one issue.
