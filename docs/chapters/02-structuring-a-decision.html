2  Structuring a Decision

Alternatives, Trade-offs, and Information

“Plans are worthless, but planning is everything.”

— Dwight D. Eisenhower, remarks at the National Defense Executive Reserve Conference, 14 November 1957

Two job offers are open on a student’s laptop. The consulting firm promises structured training and a familiar name; the sustainability start-up offers early responsibility and work she cares about. She starts a comparison table, then stops at “work–life balance.” Neither employer has told her what an ordinary week actually looks like.

A comparison table should help her choose the job that best fits her life. But without the missing information, it cannot establish whether the working conditions are sustainable. Its fixed columns may also hide a better possibility: changing an offer before accepting it. Her income, learning, and daily life depend on more than which column wins. What can she negotiate, what would each commitment give up, and what does she need to learn before deciding?

NoteCore Idea

Before choosing, clarify what you can actually do, what each commitment would give up, and which uncertainties could change your mind. Separating expected outcomes from what matters about them reveals whether the next step is to seek evidence, reconsider a priority, or find another alternative.

Learning goals

After reading this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish what is feasible from what receives serious consideration.
  • Develop new alternatives and distinguish fixed constraints from terms that can be changed.
  • Identify opportunity cost as the value of the best feasible alternative forgone, and distinguish it from an unrecoverable sunk cost.
  • Separate predictions about consequences from values over those consequences.
  • Assess information by whether plausible results could change action.
  • Explain how uncertain assumptions and the cost of further analysis affect a choice.

Define the decision before comparing options

The student may be able to negotiate workload, request another conversation, or agree on a later start date. None of these possibilities appears in a table labeled only “consulting” and “start-up.” Comparison becomes useful after the alternatives have been examined.

A useful sequence is to expand the feasible alternatives, identify the best forgone option, seek information that could change the choice, and test whether the ranking survives different assumptions. These questions can send the student back to an earlier step. A forecast may reveal a new option, and a priority may reveal a term worth negotiating.

“Should I accept?” sounds like a clear question. It can nevertheless close off negotiation, further search, and the option to learn before committing. To keep those possibilities available, a useful decision statement specifies the commitment being considered, the decision-maker, the relevant horizon, and the conditions under which the choice can be revised. Compare:

Should I accept the start-up job?

with:

By Friday, should I accept either offer, ask for different terms or more time, or keep searching, given the income I need and the work I want over the next two years?

The second statement makes clear what must be decided now and what can still change. It gives the student a deadline for comparing feasible paths, including a revised offer, against the life she wants those paths to support.

The student can decide whether to accept an offer, while the employer must agree to any change in its terms. She also needs to consider anyone whose income or time the choice would affect. Clarifying authority, whose outcomes matter, and what happens if she does nothing gives the comparison a boundary. The consequences of accepting may last for years even though the decision is due on Friday.

Construct the feasible set

A feasible alternative is a course of action that can actually be taken given relevant limits of money, time, law, authority, technology, health, capability, and commitment.

For the student, “work anywhere” may be imaginable, a remote role may be feasible, and only the two written offers may receive serious consideration. These are three different sets:

  • The imaginable set: everything one can describe, including options that are impossible or impermissible.
  • The feasible set: the alternatives that can genuinely be implemented.
  • The consideration set: the alternatives noticed, recalled, or taken seriously, some of which may still need a feasibility check.

People commonly consider only part of what is feasible, while also entertaining options that turn out to be unavailable. A consistent ranking cannot recover a good alternative that never entered the comparison.

A visa condition may rule out one location; a deadline imposed by an employer may be negotiable. The student needs to find out which kind of limit she faces. Asking for a later start date changes nothing if the employer cannot accommodate it, but the request may reveal flexibility that the original offer concealed.

Ask: What would have to change for this option to become feasible, and who could change it? The answer might identify a negotiation, permission, investment, or redesign. It might also confirm that the boundary is fixed.

When the visible menu is too narrow, use a small set of generative moves:

  • Maintain: Keep the status quo or continue the current path.
  • Delay: Preserve the option while waiting for a defined event or date.
  • Learn: Gather information, run a test, seek a second opinion, or conduct a pilot.
  • Negotiate: Change price, timing, scope, responsibilities, safeguards, or exit terms.
  • Combine: Create a package or hybrid from parts of existing alternatives.
  • Stage: Begin with a limited, reversible commitment and expand only if conditions are met.
  • Search: Reopen the market or generate alternatives outside the current menu.
  • Exit: Stop, decline, or abandon the project.

These moves matter because careful comparison cannot recover a better path that never entered the choice. The student need not invent an ideal job; discovering one feasible change to the terms may be enough to improve her options.

More options do not invariably make choosing harder. Here, aim for meaningfully different alternatives that can be compared. Chapter 40 examines the evidence on choice overload and how to organize a menu without hiding useful options.

A useful stopping question is:

Do we have at least one meaningfully different alternative, not merely a slightly altered version of the current favorite?

Name the opportunity cost

Each offer makes its own benefits visible: training, responsibility, income, or meaningful work. Accepting those benefits, however, can close off a more valuable path. The student needs to compare what she gains with the best feasible opportunity she gives up. Opportunity cost is the value of the best feasible alternative forgone, including the consequences of that complete path. It is neither just the money paid nor the sum of every benefit in every unchosen life. The same reasoning applies to a meeting that displaces focused work or a project that uses capital needed elsewhere.

Opportunity cost depends on the alternative set. Discovering a better feasible option raises the cost of accepting every proposal already on the table. Conversely, if the apparent alternative is not actually feasible, it should not be used to exaggerate the sacrifice.

Research on opportunity-cost neglect shows that people do not always generate the displaced alternative spontaneously. Frederick et al. (2009) demonstrated this with a minimal change in how a hypothetical purchase was described. One hundred and fifty students imagined deciding whether to buy a desirable DVD for $14.99. When the alternative was simply “not buy,” 75% chose the DVD. When that same alternative added the reminder “Keep the $14.99 for other purchases,” the purchase rate fell to 55%. The objective options had not changed; the reminder made an otherwise implicit use of the money easier to represent.

A later meta-analysis of 39 experiments, mainly involving hypothetical choices, found a robust but considerably smaller average effect than the original studies reported (Maguire et al., 2023). Making a forgone alternative salient can improve awareness of the trade-off and change choice. Decision quality depends on how feasible and valuable that alternative is.

Before commitment, ask:

If I choose this, what is the best feasible path I will no longer be able to take?

Naming that path often clarifies the decision more than adding another score to the favored option.

Separate sunk costs from future costs

Opportunity cost concerns what a new commitment gives up. A sunk cost is money, time, or effort already spent that cannot be recovered through any available action. Suppose the student has spent weeks preparing for the consulting interviews and paid non-refundable travel costs. The same past expenditure remains whether she accepts, negotiates, or chooses the start-up. It therefore provides no reason to prefer one over another; the comparison concerns outcomes she can still change.

The sunk-cost fallacy occurs when an irrecoverable investment makes an option more attractive even though it adds nothing to what that option can now deliver (Arkes & Blumer, 1985). “I have put too much into this application to turn it down” lets the preparation substitute for the job’s future value.

The Big Bang Theory captures the same mistake in “The Einstein Approximation” (season 3, episode 14). Leonard’s phone rings with the Joker’s laugh. When Penny says it creeps her out, Leonard replies, “Me, too, but I paid three bucks for it.” The three dollars are gone whether he keeps the ringtone or switches to a free default tone. Keeping an unpleasant ringtone does not recover the purchase price; it adds future irritation to a past expense.

Looking forward still requires learning from the past. Earlier work may have created useful skills, a partly completed asset, or evidence that changes the forecast. A cancellation penalty is a future cost, while a refund or resale value is recoverable. A project that was a poor initial choice can nevertheless be worth finishing if its remaining benefits exceed its remaining costs and the value of the best alternative. Past investment alone does not justify future investment.

For the student, the relevant question is which offer best serves her life from now on. Understanding that comparison does not always make it easy to abandon an earlier commitment. Chapter 7 examines why defending a past choice can encourage further investment despite unfavorable feedback; Chapter 19 explains why stopping can feel like closing an account at a loss.

Separate predictions from values

Suppose the student and her adviser disagree about the start-up. Another report about the firm might resolve a disagreement about its prospects, but it cannot settle how much autonomy is worth relative to stable income. Before seeking more evidence, they need to identify which kind of disagreement they have. A consequence-based decision combines two distinct judgments:

  • Prediction: What is likely to happen if this alternative is chosen?
  • Valuation: How desirable or undesirable would that consequence be?

The distinction sounds obvious but is often lost in ordinary discussion. “The candidate is the best fit” may combine a prediction about performance, a value placed on similarity, a feeling of interpersonal ease, and an unstated view of what the organization should become. “The project is too risky” may combine the probability of failure, the size of the loss, the ability to recover, and the speaker’s tolerance for uncertainty.

For the student and her adviser, the next step is to state their disagreement precisely: do they expect different working conditions, or place different weights on the same conditions? That answer determines whether another conversation with the employer should seek evidence, explore different terms, or both.

For each alternative, write the consequential forecast, its horizon, a plausible range, and the evidence supporting it. “The start-up will offer faster learning” becomes more useful when the student specifies what she expects to learn in the first year, from whom, and what would make that expectation wrong. Comparable past cases can provide a reference class against which to check the forecast.

Important decisions usually involve several objectives. A job can affect income, learning, autonomy, health, location, family time, mission, relationships, and future options. A public decision may affect different groups unequally. Some consequences can be traded off; others may be unacceptable even when an aggregate score is high. Multiattribute decision analysis can help make these objectives and trade-offs explicit (Keeney & Raiffa, 1976).

The student should explain what “learning” means before assigning it a weight. Is it valuable in itself, or mainly because it opens future opportunities? Counting both without checking their overlap can exaggerate the advantage. Health may instead be a threshold: an option is unacceptable if its expected demands exceed what she can sustain.

The separate columns in Table 2.1 keep forecasts open to an evidence check and values open to discussion. A weight such as “health: 30 percent” is hard to interpret until the relevant range and trade-off are specified, and uncertainty should remain visible in the entries.

The table should preserve uncertainty rather than hiding it. If one option looks best only because every uncertain input has been entered at its most favorable value, the apparent precision is misleading.

Assess the value of further information

When a consequential choice feels uncertain, another question or report can seem like progress. But the offer deadline still approaches, and an answer that leaves every action unchanged may consume time without improving the choice.

Information has instrumental value when it can improve action enough to justify its costs. Those costs include money, delay, attention, privacy, processing, false reassurance, and strategic exposure.

The pursuit itself can also change how much weight a detail receives. Bastardi and Shafir (1998) asked participants to act as loan officers considering a mortgage applicant. When an otherwise qualified applicant’s overdue credit-card debt was stated as $5,000, 29% approved. Another group was told the debt might be $5,000 or $25,000 and could wait for clarification; 75% chose to wait. After the uncertainty was resolved at $5,000, the overall approval rate in that group was 56%, including those who had decided earlier. The same final debt now looked reassuring against the larger amount it might have been.

This hypothetical decision does not show that checking debt is generally wasteful. It shows why wanting an answer is not sufficient evidence of its decision value: searching for a detail can change the comparison against which it is judged. A useful safeguard is to specify how each possible answer would affect the choice before pursuing it.

To assess a proposed question or test, list its plausible answers and the action that would follow each. Then compare the expected benefit of acting on the answer with the cost of obtaining and using it. In Table 2.2, the pilot and timing question can redirect action; the diagnostic test, as specified, cannot.

If every plausible result leaves action unchanged, the inquiry may still serve curiosity, reassurance, or documentation. It has little instrumental value for that particular decision.

The technical appendix formalizes the expected value of information. For now, the student can ask the employers about ordinary working hours and decide in advance which answers would change the offer she accepts or the terms she requests.

Check what the choice depends on

The comparison may favor the start-up because the student expects close supervision and values early responsibility. If she would prefer consulting without that supervision, an unanswered question about who will guide her work could reverse the choice. A high total score would hide this dependence unless its assumptions remained visible.

A robust recommendation remains preferred across specified, plausible changes in the assumptions. A fragile recommendation reverses after a small credible change. Sensitivity analysis examines that dependence: which forecast or priority would have to change, and by how much, for another option to become preferable? For the student, checking supervision may matter more than refining every entry in the table. Chapter 41 develops the practical checks and safeguards for decisions that depend on uncertain assumptions.

Match analysis to the decision

The offer deadline still approaches. The student could keep interviewing employees and adjusting scores, but further analysis has a cost. Bounded rationality recognizes that search, attention, time, memory, and computation are scarce (Simon, 1955, 1956). People often satisfice: they search until an alternative meets an aspiration level rather than proving that it is the best possible option. That can be reasonable when further search costs more than it is likely to improve the decision.

A familiar, inexpensive, reversible choice usually needs less analysis than a consequential decision that is difficult to undo. Chapter 8 examines when experience makes intuition trustworthy; Chapter 41 explains how to match a decision procedure to its stakes and difficulties. When uncertain outcomes must be combined, Chapter 16 develops the relevant comparison.

By Friday, the student’s task is to make a choice whose reasons she can explain. She may prefer the start-up if supervision is available and the workload is sustainable, or consulting if those conditions fail. She now knows what she would give up and which answers could change her mind. The comparison has given her a basis for choosing despite the uncertainty that remains.

Practice Lab

Use a decision you currently face to answer five questions:

  1. What must be decided? State the commitment, deadline, and what happens if you do nothing.
  2. What could you do? List the feasible alternatives, including one you had overlooked or could create by changing the terms.
  3. What would you give up? Identify the best feasible alternative forgone. Separate sunk costs from costs and benefits that can still change.
  4. What do you expect, and what matters? Separate your main predictions from your priorities and unacceptable trade-offs.
  5. What could change your choice? Identify one important uncertainty, the question that could resolve it, and how different answers would affect your decision.

Take it forward

The student began with two offers and a blank comparison table. She can now see alternatives, trade-offs, and questions the original columns concealed. Yet even a well-defined choice depends on what evidence enters the comparison. Chapter 3 turns to attention: why some information becomes visible while other information is missed.
