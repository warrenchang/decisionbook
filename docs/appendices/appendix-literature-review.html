Appendix E — Conducting and Writing a Literature Review

From a broad topic to a research question worth investigating

A student plans to write about automatic enrollment in savings plans. The idea seems straightforward: if more employees join, the policy helps them save. Then the student notices that the papers measure different things. Some track participation, others follow contributions, and another asks whether money entering a retirement account would otherwise have been saved elsewhere. The question changes. What would count as evidence that the policy increased saving?

Reading more papers will help only if the student learns how to compare them. A literature review develops an answer from the available research, showing what the evidence supports and where the answer remains uncertain. It can be a contribution in its own right. It can also establish why another study is needed and which comparison would make that study informative.

This appendix follows the work from choosing a question to writing a defensible synthesis, then connects the remaining uncertainty to the experimental study developed in Appendix F.

A topic becomes a review question

“Defaults and saving” names a topic. The problem is that increased participation need not establish increased total saving. A focused review question might therefore be: Under what conditions do automatic saving arrangements increase total saving, rather than shift money between accounts? The question tells the student which outcomes and comparisons need attention.

Start with an uncertainty that matters to an explanation or a decision. Identify what is being compared, for whom, and over what period. Then do enough preliminary reading to find out whether the question can be answered within the time and evidence available. A broad question may need narrowing; a seemingly novel one may already have a substantial literature.

Each boundary should serve the argument. Focusing on employees makes sense if payroll deductions and employer contributions are central to the explanation. Restricting the review to one country could help examine a particular institutional rule. Adding a country or date merely to make the title narrower does not explain why that restriction matters.

Keep a short question record: the current question, why its answer matters, its boundaries, and what the preliminary search suggests. Revise the question when the reading exposes a better comparison, while recording why the scope changed. This makes exploration useful without quietly redefining the review around a preferred conclusion.

Choose a review that fits the question

A focused term-paper review may aim to explain a debate or compare competing accounts. Its scope can be modest, provided the selection is transparent and the conclusion respects that scope. A systematic review takes on a more demanding task: use explicit, reproducible methods to identify and evaluate the evidence relevant to a specified question. A broad mapping question may instead call for a scoping review and its corresponding methods.

The choice affects the search, selection rules, appraisal, and form of synthesis. Decide what kind of answer the review promises before collecting a large folder of papers (Snyder, 2019). A meta-analysis is a statistical method for combining suitable estimates; a systematic review can proceed without one when pooling would not answer a meaningful common question.

For a systematic review, use reporting guidance appropriate to its type. PRISMA 2020 helps authors report how an intervention-effect review was conducted and what it found; it is not a substitute for the conduct of the review or an assessment of its methodological quality. Drawing a flowchart does not, by itself, make a review systematic (Page et al., 2021).

For the savings question, the student first needs to know which policy designs and saving measures the literature contains. A transparent comparison may reveal that studies cannot be combined into one estimate because they answer different questions. That is a useful result to explain, rather than an inconvenience to hide.

Build a search you can explain

Turn the question into concept groups and identify alternative terms within each group. For the savings example, one group concerns the arrangement and another the outcome. An initial search might be:

("automatic enrollment" OR "automatic enrolment" OR "savings default")
AND
(saving OR savings OR retirement OR pension)

This is a starting query, not a complete search strategy. Adapt the syntax to the database and inspect what it retrieves. An early search that requires the exact phrase “total saving” might miss a relevant paper that discusses wealth accumulation or crowd-out. Read promising abstracts to discover those terms, then refine the search.

Use bibliographic databases alongside the references and later citations of useful papers. A recent review can reveal terminology and important disagreements; the primary studies are still needed to assess the comparisons behind its conclusions. Search for results that could challenge the emerging answer as deliberately as those that support it (Pautasso, 2013).

Before selecting the main evidence, state the inclusion criteria. For example: which populations and saving arrangements are relevant; whether the review requires a comparison group; which outcomes and follow-up periods count; and how it will handle working papers, unavailable texts, languages, or publication dates. The criteria should follow the question. A rule that admits only statistically significant findings would instead select the answer.

Keep the record in Table E.1 as you work. It lets a reader understand the scope and lets you recover a decision you might otherwise forget.

Check versions before counting studies. A working paper and its published article may report the same experiment; several articles may reuse one sample. Keep useful versions linked in the record and identify overlapping data so the synthesis does not treat them as independent confirmations. Check corrections and retractions on the publisher’s record, and return to the search before finalizing a review of an active topic.

Search tools and generated summaries can suggest terms or papers to inspect. They cannot establish that a citation exists, that it says what the summary claims, or that its evidence answers your question. When the full text is unavailable, record that limitation and restrict the claim to material you could examine.

Read studies for the comparison they support

Begin each paper with the question you need it to answer. Locate the design, comparison, outcome, and result before treating the authors’ interpretation as your own. A paper can offer strong evidence for one claim and little for another: a randomized change in contributions may identify a behavioral effect while leaving its mechanism or welfare consequences unresolved.

An evidence matrix keeps these distinctions visible. Use one row per study, adding separate rows for materially different experiments or outcomes and marking shared samples. Record enough detail to reconstruct a claim, without copying every result into the review.

Keep three kinds of notes separate: what the study reports, how its authors interpret that finding, and your assessment of what follows. Mark copied language as a quotation immediately, with its location. This prevents a later paraphrase from inheriting either the wording or the confidence of the original without inspection.

Assess limitations by their consequences. “The sample was small” is incomplete criticism until you explain which effects the uncertainty leaves plausible. A convenience sample may limit generalization while still supporting a randomized comparison within that sample. As Appendix F explains, random sampling supports population generalization, whereas random assignment supports the internal validity of a causal comparison. Neither alone answers every question about the study.

Synthesize the evidence

The matrix becomes a review when the comparisons produce an answer. Organize sections around questions, explanations, or consequential differences in design. Explain a study’s essential design where it first matters, then recall only the detail needed for later comparisons. This is the difference between taking notes on a collection and developing an argument from it (Pautasso, 2013).

The three papers in Table E.3 are a deliberately selected teaching example, adapted from the lecture. They illustrate how studies can perform different jobs in a synthesis; they are not an exhaustive or current review of saving arrangements.

A synthesis could begin:

Automatic saving arrangements can change behavior, but evaluating their effect on total saving requires following the money beyond the target account. Participation rose after automatic enrollment in Madrian and Shea’s (2001) employer study. In Denmark, Chetty et al. (2014) found that automatic pension contributions generated additional saving, while tax subsidies largely shifted saving between accounts. Blumenstock et al. (2018) showed experimentally that contribution defaults changed participation among employees who already had accounts, but their follow-up measures were too imprecise to establish displacement of other savings. Together, these findings make additional total saving a separate outcome to investigate, rather than a conclusion to infer from enrollment alone.

The paragraph links a claim, evidence, a comparison, and an implication. It does not need one paragraph for everything each paper contains. Notice also what it leaves open: the studies use different arrangements and outcomes, so their effect estimates cannot simply be averaged as measurements of the same intervention.

When findings appear to conflict, compare what was actually estimated. Differences may concern treatment content, comparison group, population, measurement, follow-up, or research design. They may also reflect imprecision or selection in what reached publication. A significant finding in one paper and a nonsignificant finding in another do not establish that their effects differ; inspect the estimates and uncertainty rather than counting significant papers as votes. Appendix G examines how selective reporting can distort the body of evidence itself.

Identify a gap worth investigating

A useful gap emerges from what the comparison cannot yet settle. It could be an unresolved explanation, a result that may depend on a particular condition, a poorly measured outcome, or a mismatch between the available evidence and an important decision. Explain both the uncertainty and the consequence of resolving it.

For the saving example, observing greater contributions does not identify whether employees became more attentive, followed an apparent recommendation, or avoided the effort of changing a setting. Blumenstock and colleagues already use additional experiments to investigate why defaults matter. A proposed mechanism gap must explain what remains unresolved after those tests, rather than infer a gap from the contribution result alone. A new study becomes useful if it can separate explanations that the existing comparisons leave together.

Before claiming that gap, search specifically for studies that might already address it. Check mechanism experiments, later follow-ups, relevant reviews, and research using different terminology. The three-paper teaching set above cannot establish that the wider literature lacks such evidence. State the scope of a gap claim: “In the studies covered by this search…” is more defensible than “No research has examined…” when the search cannot support that universal statement.

Writing a gap statement requires four connected decisions:

  1. State what the strongest relevant evidence establishes.
  2. Identify what remains unresolved and why the existing designs cannot settle it.
  3. Explain what an answer would change in theory, practice, or the interpretation of an earlier finding.
  4. Describe the comparison or evidence that could reduce the uncertainty.

A new country or population may matter when institutions, incentives, or other conditions could change the result. Their novelty alone is not enough. Conversely, a careful replication, better measurement, or a more precise estimate can make a valuable contribution even when the topic is familiar. The review should explain the gain in understanding rather than manufacture a claim of being first.

Write the review as an argument

Draft from the question and evidence matrix. Give each section a job in answering the question, then write paragraphs that make an interpretable claim, examine its support, and explain what the comparison adds. Revise the introduction once you know the answer so that it promises the review you actually deliver.

The introduction can move from common ground to a consequential difficulty and then to the review’s response. For example: saving-plan defaults can affect behavior; increased participation leaves additional saving uncertain; the review therefore compares evidence that measures these different outcomes. Use this progression when it clarifies the argument, without forcing every paragraph into a fixed template.

Theory belongs where it changes a comparison. If inertia and inferred recommendation could both explain remaining at a default, introduce those accounts before evaluating studies that try to separate them. A long background catalogue that the analysis never uses delays the question instead of preparing the reader for it.

Make a limitation do explanatory work. Rather than end every paragraph with “more research is needed,” state what the design leaves unresolved and whether that uncertainty changes the review’s answer. Likewise, a conclusion should first say what can now be concluded. The remaining gaps make sense once the reader knows what the review has established.

Before submitting, reverse-outline the draft: write one sentence naming the job of every paragraph. Two adjacent paragraphs doing the same job may be combined; a paragraph whose purpose cannot be named may need moving or removal. Check whether the transitions express the actual relationship between claims and whether the conclusion answers the original question.

Finally, audit citations against the text. A citation should support the sentence it accompanies. Read the original source when making a claim about its design or result, and identify secondary citation honestly when that is all you could access. Use your own sentence structure when paraphrasing, preserve the author’s meaning, and mark direct quotations with a locator. Reconcile in-text citations with an alphabetized reference list and verify names, years, titles, and identifiers. A reference manager can assist with formatting; it cannot determine whether a source supports your claim.

From review to study

The review’s final research proposal should follow from its comparison of evidence. Record what is known, the unresolved question, why it matters, and the observation that could distinguish the remaining explanations. Carry forward the closest studies’ treatments, measures, populations, and weaknesses so that the next design builds on them deliberately.

For the clinic introduced in Appendix F, this means reviewing existing reminder evidence before proposing another trial. If a focused review leaves uncertainty about whether understandable wording adds value beyond a reminder’s timing, a useful comparison would vary wording while holding timing fixed. If the uncertainty concerns what causes the effect, attendance alone will not settle it; the design needs evidence that distinguishes the proposed pathways. These are illustrations of how a documented gap can guide design, not claims that the reminder literature currently lacks those studies.

A clear review may also show that another experiment is premature or unnecessary. An existing answer may already support the decision; a measurement problem may need resolving first; access or ethics may prevent the proposed comparison. The transition to research design is therefore a reasoned choice about what evidence is needed next.

Practice: build a review plan

Choose a question from the book that matters to a decision or an explanation. Draft its scope and a search strategy, then build a small evidence matrix from an initial set of relevant studies. Actively search for evidence that could challenge your first answer, and explain whether it changes your conclusion. This is a pilot for the review, not a claim of complete coverage.

Write one synthesis paragraph that compares the studies, followed by a provisional answer and a gap statement. For the proposed gap, specify what you would search next to check whether it is already addressed. If it survives that check, explain what new comparison would be informative. The result should make clear why each selected paper is needed and how the evidence changed the question with which you began.
