Appendix C — Conducting and Writing a Literature Review

From a broad topic to a research question worth investigating

A student decides to review the advice to “think positively.” Its appeal is clear: expecting success may make a difficult goal worth pursuing. Yet someone can feel encouraged while leaving the hardest preparation for later. For a student facing an exam or a graduate seeking work, encouragement matters only if it supports the effort and judgment the goal requires. When does positive thinking help, and when might it get in the way?

Reading more papers will help only if the student learns how to compare them. A literature review develops an answer from the available research, showing what the evidence supports and where the answer remains uncertain. It can be a contribution in its own right. It can also establish why another study is needed and which comparison would make that study informative.

This appendix follows the work from choosing a question to writing a defensible synthesis, then connects the remaining uncertainty to the experimental study developed in Appendix D.

A topic becomes a review question

“Positive thinking” names a topic. A starting question is: Under what conditions do positive expectations help rather than hinder goal attainment? Its importance comes from a practical tension: people need enough confidence to attempt difficult goals, while also recognizing obstacles, preparing adequately, and knowing when to reconsider a goal.

Preliminary reading sharpens the question. Positive expectations are judgments that success is likely; positive fantasies are pleasant images of a desired future. Someone can enjoy imagining an excellent result without believing it likely or preparing for it. Studies distinguish these forms of thought and report different relationships with later effort and achievement (Oettingen & Mayer, 2002). If the review covers both, its question should become: Under what conditions do different forms of positive thinking support or hinder goal attainment? This revision changes the comparison, not just the wording.

Start with an uncertainty that matters to an explanation or a decision. Identify what is being compared, for whom, and over what period. Then do enough preliminary reading to find out whether the question can be answered within the time and evidence available. A broad question may need narrowing; a seemingly novel one may already have a substantial literature.

Each boundary should serve the argument. A term paper could focus on academic goals if it needs comparable measures of preparation and performance. Including job searches might help examine goals whose outcomes also depend on other people’s decisions. Adding a country or date merely to make the title narrower does not explain why that restriction matters.

Keep a short question record: the current question, why its answer matters, its boundaries, and what the preliminary search suggests. Revise the question when the reading exposes a better comparison, while recording why the scope changed. This makes exploration useful without quietly redefining the review around a preferred conclusion.

Choose a review that fits the question

A focused term-paper review may aim to explain a debate or compare competing accounts. Its scope can be modest, provided the selection is transparent and the conclusion respects that scope. A systematic review takes on a more demanding task: use explicit, reproducible methods to identify and evaluate the evidence relevant to a specified question. A broad mapping question may instead call for a scoping review and its corresponding methods.

The choice affects the search, selection rules, appraisal, and form of synthesis. Decide what kind of answer the review promises before collecting a large folder of papers (Snyder, 2019). A meta-analysis is a statistical method for combining suitable estimates; a systematic review can proceed without one when pooling would not answer a meaningful common question.

For a systematic review, use reporting guidance appropriate to its type. PRISMA 2020 helps authors report how an intervention-effect review was conducted and what it found; it is not a substitute for the conduct of the review or an assessment of its methodological quality (Page et al., 2021).

For the positive-thinking question, the student first needs to distinguish studies that measure existing expectations from experiments that ask people to imagine success or consider obstacles. Combining them into one estimate of “positive thinking” could conceal the distinction the review needs to explain.

Build a search you can explain

Turn the question into concept groups and identify alternative terms within each group. For this example, one group concerns thoughts about the future and another concerns action or achievement. An initial search might be:

("positive expectations" OR "positive fantasies" OR optimism OR "mental contrasting")
AND
(effort OR preparation OR persistence OR "goal attainment")

Adapt the syntax to the database and inspect what it retrieves. Requiring the exact phrase “goal attainment” might miss studies reporting grades, job offers, or task performance. Read promising abstracts to discover those terms, then refine the search. Search terms can bring related research into view without making its concepts interchangeable: a general optimistic outlook is not the same measure as an expectation about a particular exam.

Use bibliographic databases alongside the references and later citations of useful papers. A recent review can reveal terminology and important disagreements; the primary studies are still needed to assess the comparisons behind its conclusions. Search for results that could challenge the emerging answer as deliberately as those that support it (Pautasso, 2013).

Before selecting the main evidence, state the inclusion criteria. For example: which populations, goals, and forms of positive thought are relevant; whether both observational and experimental studies are included; which outcomes and follow-up periods count; and how the review will handle working papers, unavailable texts, languages, or publication dates. The criteria should follow the question. A rule that admits only statistically significant findings would instead select the answer.

Check versions before counting studies. A working paper and its published article may report the same experiment; several articles may reuse one sample. Keep useful versions linked in the record and identify overlapping data so the synthesis does not treat them as independent confirmations. Check corrections and retractions on the publisher’s record, and return to the search before finalizing a review of an active topic.

Use search tools and generated summaries to find papers, then verify citations and claims against the original sources. When the full text is unavailable, record that limitation and restrict the claim to material you could examine.

Read studies for the comparison they support

Begin each paper with the question you need it to answer. Locate the design, comparison, outcome, and result before treating the authors’ interpretation as your own. If confident students later perform better, they may already have stronger skills or better preparation. That association does not establish what would happen if an intervention increased their confidence. Conversely, an experiment that changes feelings of energy does not by itself establish a lasting change in achievement.

The fields in Table C.2 keep the study’s design, result, and role in the review distinct. Use one row per study, adding separate rows for materially different experiments or outcomes and marking shared samples. Record enough detail to reconstruct a claim, without copying every result into the review.

Keep three kinds of notes separate: what the study reports, how its authors interpret that finding, and your assessment of what follows. Mark copied language as a quotation immediately, with its location. This prevents a later paraphrase from inheriting either the wording or the confidence of the original without inspection.

Assess limitations by their consequences. “The sample was small” is incomplete criticism until you explain which effects the uncertainty leaves plausible. A convenience sample may limit generalization while still supporting a randomized comparison within that sample. As Appendix D explains, random sampling supports population generalization, whereas random assignment supports the internal validity of a causal comparison. Neither alone answers every question about the study.

Synthesize the evidence

The matrix becomes a review when the comparisons produce an answer. Organize sections around questions, explanations, or consequential differences in design. Explain a study’s essential design where it first matters, then recall only the detail needed for later comparisons. This is the difference between taking notes on a collection and developing an argument from it (Pautasso, 2013).

The selected studies in Table C.3 show why a synthesis needs more than a list of favorable and unfavorable results. Compare what each study measures or changes before deciding whether its finding answers the same question.

A synthesis could begin:

Positive thinking does not describe one process. Oettingen and Mayer (2002) found that favorable expectations predicted better achievement, whereas positive fantasies predicted poorer achievement. Their observational comparisons do not establish what increasing confidence would cause. Kappes and Oettingen (2011) instead manipulated idealized imagery, finding lower reported energy and subsequent accomplishment in one student experiment. Oettingen and colleagues (2001) tested a further distinction: connecting a desired future with obstacles made commitment more dependent on perceived chances of success. Together, the findings suggest that a review should distinguish believing success is likely, enjoying an image of success, and considering what achieving it requires.

Chapter 39 develops the practical use of mental contrasting.

When findings appear to conflict, compare what was actually estimated. Differences may concern treatment content, comparison group, population, measurement, follow-up, or research design. They may also reflect imprecision or selection in what reached publication. A significant finding in one paper and a nonsignificant finding in another do not establish that their effects differ; inspect the estimates and uncertainty rather than counting significant papers as votes. Appendix E examines how selective reporting can distort the body of evidence itself.

Identify a gap worth investigating

A useful gap emerges from what the comparison cannot yet settle. It could be an unresolved explanation, a result that may depend on a particular condition, a poorly measured outcome, or a mismatch between the available evidence and an important decision. Explain both the uncertainty and the consequence of resolving it.

For the positive-thinking example, the distinction between expectations and fantasies is already established in the cited literature. Rediscovering that distinction is not a new research gap. A review can still contribute by explaining how definitions, study designs, and outcome measures affect the advice the evidence supports. For instance, do studies assess whether expectations are accurate, and do they follow actual preparation as well as reported energy or commitment?

Before claiming that gap, search specifically for studies that might already address it. Check mechanism experiments, later follow-ups, relevant reviews, and research using different terminology. State the scope of a gap claim: “In the studies covered by this search…” is more defensible than “No research has examined…” when the search cannot support that universal statement.

Writing a gap statement requires four connected decisions:

  1. State what the strongest relevant evidence establishes.
  2. Identify what remains unresolved and why the existing designs cannot settle it.
  3. Explain what an answer would change in theory, practice, or the interpretation of an earlier finding.
  4. Describe the comparison or evidence that could reduce the uncertainty.

A new country or population may matter when institutions, incentives, or other conditions could change the result. Their novelty alone is not enough. Conversely, a careful replication, better measurement, or a more precise estimate can make a valuable contribution even when the topic is familiar. The review should explain the gain in understanding rather than manufacture a claim of being first.

Write the review as an argument

Draft from the question and evidence matrix. Use Table C.4 to give each section a job in answering the question, then write paragraphs that make an interpretable claim, examine its support, and explain what the comparison adds. Revise the introduction once you know the answer so that it promises the review you actually deliver.

The introduction can move from common ground (And) to a consequential difficulty (But) and then to the review’s response (Therefore). The difficulty must explain why the reader needs an answer; the response must say what the review will clarify. In this example, advice that encourages confidence may leave preparation and realistic assessment unspecified. Comparing forms of positive thought can clarify what that advice should ask people to do. Use this progression when it clarifies the argument, without forcing every paragraph into a fixed template.

Theory belongs where it changes a comparison. If expectations, fantasies, and attention to obstacles imply different routes to action, explain those differences before evaluating studies that try to separate them. A long background catalogue that the analysis never uses delays the question instead of preparing the reader for it.

Make a limitation do explanatory work. Rather than end every paragraph with “more research is needed,” state what the design leaves unresolved and whether that uncertainty changes the review’s answer. Likewise, a conclusion should first say what can now be concluded. The remaining gaps make sense once the reader knows what the review has established.

Before submitting, reverse-outline the draft: write one sentence naming the job of every paragraph. Two adjacent paragraphs doing the same job may be combined; a paragraph whose purpose cannot be named may need moving or removal. Check whether the transitions express the actual relationship between claims and whether the conclusion answers the original question.

Finally, audit citations against the text. A citation should support the sentence it accompanies. Read the original source when making a claim about its design or result, and identify secondary citation honestly when that is all you could access. Use your own sentence structure when paraphrasing, preserve the author’s meaning, and mark direct quotations with a locator. Reconcile in-text citations with an alphabetized reference list and verify names, years, titles, and identifiers. A reference manager can assist with formatting; it cannot determine whether a source supports your claim.

From review to study

The review’s final research proposal should follow from its comparison of evidence. Record what is known, the unresolved question, why it matters, and the observation that could distinguish the remaining explanations. Carry forward the closest studies’ treatments, measures, populations, and weaknesses so that the next design builds on them deliberately.

For the clinic introduced in Appendix D, this means reviewing existing reminder evidence before proposing another trial. If a focused review leaves uncertainty about whether understandable wording adds value beyond a reminder’s timing, a useful comparison would vary wording while holding timing fixed. If the uncertainty concerns what causes the effect, attendance alone will not settle it; the design needs evidence that distinguishes the proposed pathways. These are illustrations of how a documented gap can guide design, not claims that the reminder literature currently lacks those studies.

A clear review may also show that another experiment is premature or unnecessary. An existing answer may already support the decision; a measurement problem may need resolving first; access or ethics may prevent the proposed comparison. The transition to research design is therefore a reasoned choice about what evidence is needed next.

Practice: build a review plan

Begin with the proposal below. It gives the review a practical reason to exist while leaving the answer open.

1. Working title

Positive Thinking: When It Helps—and When It Backfires

2. Problem statement

  • AND: Expecting success can encourage people to pursue opportunities, invest effort, and persist after setbacks.
  • BUT: People can also feel confident while underestimating obstacles and preparing too little. Advice to “think positively” does not explain when encouragement supports useful persistence and when it leaves someone underprepared or pursuing an unrealistic goal. Understanding that difference matters for students and job seekers deciding how to prepare and when to change course.
  • THEREFORE: This review will distinguish positive expectations from idealized fantasies and compare how each relates to effort, preparation, and goal attainment. It aims to clarify how to encourage confidence without sacrificing realistic assessment or preparation.

3. Starting research question

Under what conditions do positive expectations help rather than hinder goal attainment?

This first question still needs refining to match the proposed comparison of expectations and fantasies. The contribution is an explanation of the conditions under which encouragement supports effective action. “Positive thinking” works as an accessible title, but the review needs to define and distinguish the forms it examines.

Develop the proposal in four steps:

  1. Refine the question. Decide whether to focus on expectations alone or compare forms of positive thinking, and revise the question accordingly. Choose a population and goal for reasons that affect the comparison. Define how you will recognize adequate preparation, goal attainment, and useful reconsideration of a goal.
  2. Build the evidence. Draft a search strategy and inclusion criteria, then use the evidence matrix above for an initial set of relevant studies. Include evidence that could challenge your first answer. Distinguish measured confidence from experimentally assigned exercises, and feelings of readiness from observed behavior or performance.
  3. Write a synthesis. Compare the studies in one paragraph, give a provisional answer, and explain what a student or job seeker could reasonably take from it. Make clear where advice would go beyond the evidence.
  4. Justify the next question. Write a gap statement that names what remains uncertain, why resolving it matters, and what comparison could help. Search for work that might already answer it before calling it a research gap. If it survives that check, use Appendix D to develop the design.

You can then apply the same structure to another question from the book. The plan should show how the evidence changed the question, not simply how many papers you collected.

NoteA second example: retirement-saving defaults

Automatic enrollment creates a different tension: the tendency to accept a preset option can bring employees into a pension plan while also keeping their contributions at its default rate. In Madrian and Shea’s (2001) employer study, participation increased and many employees retained the default settings. Designers therefore need to consider both how defaults encourage entry and how the chosen contribution rate influences what happens afterward.

A review titled Beyond Enrollment: How Pension Defaults Shape Saving could ask: How do default contribution settings affect participation, contribution levels, and additional total saving? Its contribution would be to connect outcomes often evaluated separately, clarifying what enrollment figures can and cannot tell a policymaker about saving. A review restricted to U.S. 401(k) contribution choices would need a narrower evidence set than this broader question.

The three selected studies in Table C.5 compare how saving changes under different interventions.

Chetty et al. (2014) found that automatic pension contributions generated additional saving, while tax subsidies largely shifted saving between accounts. Blumenstock et al. (2018) showed that contribution defaults changed participation among employees who already had accounts, but their follow-up measures were too imprecise to establish displacement of other savings. Additional total saving is therefore a separate outcome to investigate, rather than a conclusion to infer from enrollment alone.

The same caution applies to explanations. Remaining at a default could reflect inattention, an inferred recommendation, or the effort of changing a setting. Blumenstock and colleagues already use additional experiments to investigate why defaults matter. A new review or study must explain what remains unresolved after that work, rather than present those explanations as an untouched gap.
