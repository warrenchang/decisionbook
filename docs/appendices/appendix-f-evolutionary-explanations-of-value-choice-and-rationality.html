Appendix F — Evolutionary Explanations of Value, Choice, and Rationality

Why minds value what they do—and why origin is not obligation

NotePurpose of this appendix

This appendix extends Chapter 6, where options acquire subjective value, and Chapter 8, where shortcuts are evaluated by cue–environment fit. Read it beside Appendix A, which formalizes rational-choice benchmarks. The aim here is to ask why humans possess these motives and decision processes without assuming that every preference is an adaptation, every bias is secretly optimal, or evolutionary history tells anyone what they ought to value.

1. Four questions, not one master cause

Why does a pastry become attractive to a hungry person? At least four scientific questions are available.

  1. Mechanism: Which bodily, perceptual, affective, learning, and neural processes produce the attraction now?
  2. Ontogeny: How did the response develop through maturation, individual learning, family practices, and culture?
  3. Function: Under which past conditions, if any, did similar mechanisms affect survival or reproduction relative to alternatives?
  4. Phylogeny: Through what evolutionary history did the relevant capacities arise?

Tinbergen (1963) distinguished these four questions to prevent one kind of explanation from displacing the others. Mechanism and ontogeny are commonly grouped as proximate explanations; function and phylogeny as ultimate or evolutionary explanations (Mayr, 1961; Scott-Phillips et al., 2011). Ultimate does not mean deepest, final, or morally superior. It identifies a kind of historical question.

Neuroscience therefore contributes to a proximate explanation of valuation; it does not complete that explanation. Behavior, physiology, learning, development, and social context remain relevant. Evolutionary theory asks why some design features and sensitivities persisted, but it does not reveal the immediate cause of one person’s choice.

“Genes are the distant reason” is also too simple. Gene expression and gene–environment interaction operate during development, so genetic processes can be part of a proximate account. Evolutionary explanations concern changes in populations across generations, not a special gene located behind each behavior. Cultural transmission can recalibrate motives within a lifetime, preserve practices across generations, and alter the environments in which biological selection occurs. The useful distinction is among questions and evidence, not between genes and everything else. Modern accounts accordingly emphasize interaction among development, ecology, inheritance, and culture rather than a one-way chain from distant gene to present act (Laland et al., 2011; Richerson et al., 2010).

2. Evolutionary function is not a preference or a command

Natural selection requires variation, some form of inheritance, and systematic differences in survival or reproduction among variants (Lewontin, 1970). It works through historical population change. It has no foresight, does not design from scratch, and cannot guarantee a globally optimal result (Gould & Lewontin, 1979).

Even “survival and reproduction” needs precision. Survival matters evolutionarily insofar as it affects genetic transmission; selection can favor reproductive benefits that carry survival costs. Fitness accounting can also include effects on relatives’ reproduction under the conditions specified by kin-selection theory (Hamilton, 1964). None of this implies that a person consciously computes genetic fitness or that reproductive success is the person’s experienced objective.

Four quantities are easily confused:

A natural-selection account can explain why sweetness, social acceptance, sexual opportunity, care, or threat cues sometimes acquire motivational force. It does not follow that people consciously want reproduction, that present preferences maximize fitness, that evolved motives improve present well-being, or that fitness-enhancing conduct is morally right.

The distinction matters whenever origin and justification diverge. A status motive may once have improved access to resources and allies while making a current career choice anxious or destructive. Sweetness can retain strong appeal where concentrated sugar is abundant. People may deliberately value contraception, celibacy, adoption, scholarship, art, spiritual practice, truth, or justice. Such commitments are not errors in a hidden reproductive utility function. They are products of human capacities for learning, language, reflection, culture, and institutional life.

ImportantExplanation is not justification

An evolutionary explanation is a causal and historical hypothesis. It supplies neither a personal objective nor a moral permission. Whether an outcome should be valued requires a separate argument about welfare, agency, rights, duties, relationships, and effects on others.

Nor should every recurring response be labeled an adaptation. Alternatives include a flexible learned response, cultural transmission, a by-product of another capacity, an exaptation, developmental constraint, drift, pathology, or a mismatch between a mechanism and its present environment. Several can contribute at once. Gould and Lewontin (1979) warned against treating a plausible adaptive story as sufficient evidence; Buss and colleagues (1998) later clarified distinctions among adaptations, by-products, and exaptations. The burden is to derive discriminating predictions, not merely to tell a coherent origin story.

3. Needs and motives: Maslow and Kenrick

Maslow (1943) proposed five broad classes of goal: physiological needs, safety, love and belonging, esteem, and self-actualization. He described them as a hierarchy of relative prepotency. On Maslow’s account, when several needs are seriously deprived, the relatively prepotent need is more likely to dominate attention. This is a claim about motivational priority, not a declaration that only one need can exist at a time.

The familiar pyramid makes the account look more rigid than Maslow’s text. Historical research found that Maslow did not create that diagram; later management writers turned the hierarchy into the now-standard stepped pyramid (Bridgman et al., 2019). Maslow explicitly allowed partial satisfaction, simultaneous motives, reversals, and exceptions. A meal can serve hunger, affiliation, identity, celebration, and status together.

Evidence supports the relevance of multiple needs more clearly than a universal staircase. Wahba and Bridwell’s (1976) review found limited support for the fixed ordering and little convincing support for the proposed sequence in which satisfying one level activates the next, while noting serious measurement problems. Tay and Diener (2011), using survey data from 123 countries, found that fulfillment of several kinds of need was associated with different aspects of subjective well-being. Those associations were largely independent of whether other needs had been fulfilled. The study was observational and does not establish a universal causal order, but it weighs against the claim that social connection, respect, or autonomy matter only after material needs are complete.

Kenrick and colleagues (2010) renovated the hierarchy using evolutionary, developmental, and life-history reasoning. Their proposal retains immediate physiological needs, self-protection, affiliation, and status or esteem, then distinguishes mate acquisition, mate retention, and parenting. Later-developing systems overlap rather than replace earlier ones, and current cues can activate any system that has developed.

The renovated account separates three claims that a pyramid can blur:

  • Developmental order: when a motivational system typically becomes available.
  • Momentary priority: which motive receives attention in this situation.
  • Evolutionary function: which recurrent problem a system is hypothesized to have addressed.

Parenting can arise later in development without becoming every parent’s dominant motive, producing the most pleasure, or possessing greater moral worth. Kenrick and colleagues also removed self-actualization as a separate fundamental motive; they proposed that activities commonly described as self-actualizing could be largely subsumed under status or esteem and mating-related motives. Commentators argued that this reduction underweights culture, meaning, and distinctively human symbolic goals, and that discarding self-actualization was premature (Kesebir et al., 2010; Peterson & Park, 2010). Showing that creativity can carry status does not show that status exhausts its causes or meaning.

Maslow and Kenrick are therefore compatible with evolutionary analysis in different senses, not two confirmed evolutionary laws. Maslow proposed recurring needs that he treated as broadly shared, but he did not derive their exact hierarchy from modern evolutionary models. Kenrick and colleagues explicitly offered an evolutionary reorganization, but the precise inventory, ordering, and functional claims remain hypotheses. Their best use in valuation is as prompts: Which motive is active? Which cue activated it? How was it learned and culturally interpreted? What evolutionary function is being proposed? What evidence would distinguish that account from alternatives?

4. Rationality has more than one benchmark

Arguments about whether humans are rational often persist because the word names different standards. Appendix A develops the formal benchmark. Here the task is to keep its purpose distinct from ecological and evolutionary questions.

Simon (1955, 1956) made limits and environmental structure central to theories of choice. The heuristics-and-biases program then documented systematic departures from specified benchmarks (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974), while the adaptive-toolbox program formalized simple procedures and asked when they succeed (Gigerenzer & Goldstein, 1996; Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, 2011). These are not mutually exclusive questions. A judgment can violate a coherence rule, arise from an identifiable heuristic, and perform well under one environment but badly under another.

Ecological rationality is strategy–environment fit. Recognition, for example, can help when exposure genuinely covaries with the target, and mislead when publicity is manufactured (Goldstein & Gigerenzer, 2002). Resource-rational analysis goes further by modeling the value of improved performance against the costs of computation (Lieder & Griffiths, 2020). It can turn an apparent imperfection into an efficient approximation under stated constraints. But “optimal” belongs to the model, not automatically to the human. Critics note that a useful modeling method does not by itself establish that people are generally resource-rational (Rahnev, 2020).

Optimally irrational?

Lionel Page’s Optimally Irrational (2023) develops the productive thesis that some behavioral anomalies become intelligible when evolution, cognition, strategic interaction, and omitted constraints are brought into the analysis. The title is best treated as a question, not a verdict.

Before calling a behavior optimally irrational, specify:

  1. The benchmark it violates. Logic, Bayesian updating, expected utility, consistency, or something else?
  2. The objective being optimized. Accuracy, current payoff, time, computational cost, experienced welfare, strategic commitment, or fitness?
  3. The information and constraints. What could the decision-maker actually know and compute?
  4. The environment. Which cue–outcome relations and feedback conditions make the rule succeed?
  5. The comparator. Better than which feasible alternative procedure?
  6. The evidence. Does the model predict new variation, or merely rationalize an observed error after the fact?

Sometimes the richer model will vindicate a simple rule. Sometimes it will reveal a mismatch. Sometimes the original benchmark remains appropriate and the person made a consequential error. “Evolution produced it” cannot decide among these possibilities.

5. Heuristics can be tools and sources of bias

A heuristic is not rational or irrational in isolation. Its performance belongs to a system:

\[ \text{strategy} + \text{information representation} + \text{environment} + \text{error costs} + \text{feedback}. \]

The same rule can be an efficient tool and a predictable source of error.

Kahneman and Klein (2009) provide a useful bridge: intuitive expertise deserves trust when an environment contains learnable regularities and the decision-maker has substantial practice with sufficiently clear feedback. Confidence is not one of those conditions.

Error-management theory makes the evolutionary logic precise in a different way. If the fitness costs of false negatives and false positives were systematically asymmetric, selection could favor a decision threshold that produces many errors of the cheaper kind (Haselton & Buss, 2000). Nesse’s (2005) smoke-detector model applies the same signal-detection logic to defensive responses. The model shows that apparently excessive alarms can be optimal under specified costs and base rates. It does not establish that any observed human bias is an adaptation. Historical ecology, heritable variation, mechanism, and distinctive predictions still require evidence.

Three empirical claims must remain separate:

  1. A strategy can outperform an alternative in a modeled environment.
  2. People actually use that strategy under the stated conditions.
  3. The strategy or its selection mechanism evolved because of that performance.

Evidence for the first does not prove the second or third. This separation prevents ecological rationality from becoming an all-purpose defense of behavior.

6. Mismatch is a testable hypothesis, not a slogan

A mechanism whose performance or fitness consequences depended on a recurrent cue–outcome relation can perform poorly when the distribution, intensity, or meaning of cues changes. This evolutionary mismatch is one bridge from adaptation to contemporary bias (Li et al., 2018). But “Stone Age mind in a modern world” is too vague to explain an observation.

A useful mismatch claim identifies four elements:

  1. Cue: What feature does the mechanism detect?
  2. Past relation: What outcome did that cue reliably predict in the relevant historical ecology?
  3. Environmental change: How has the cue–outcome relation, intensity, or timing changed?
  4. Predicted consequence: When and for whom should the response now become excessive, insufficient, or misdirected?

Sweetness offers a plausible structure: a cue related to energy availability can remain attractive when food production concentrates it beyond earlier ranges. A digital notification offers a more tentative hypothesis: cues of socially relevant information may capture attention even when platforms generate far more cues with weaker informational value. The second account needs direct evidence about the cue, mechanism, learning history, and comparison environment; “technology hijacks evolution” is not evidence.

Ancestral environments were diverse, and human learning and culture can change quickly. A response that looks mismatched may instead reflect current incentives, habit, social learning, measurement error, or deliberate choice. A good mismatch hypothesis predicts when the behavior should strengthen, weaken, or disappear as the cue–outcome structure changes.

7. How to evaluate an evolutionary explanation

An evolutionary account earns confidence by surviving alternatives and generating evidence beyond the observation it was invented to explain.

Before accepting an adaptation claim, ask:

  1. What precisely is the trait, mechanism, or decision rule?
  2. Which alternative variants are being compared?
  3. What recurrent problem and historical ecology are hypothesized?
  4. What fitness-relevant trade-off or error-cost asymmetry generates the prediction?
  5. What observation distinguishes adaptation from learning, culture, by-product, constraint, drift, pathology, or current incentives?
  6. Does the account predict variation across environments, development, cultures, or species?
  7. Is the proposed proximate mechanism independently measured?
  8. What finding would count against the explanation?
  9. Have historical fitness, current welfare, and normative value been kept separate?

If the account cannot answer these questions, call it a possibility or organizing hypothesis, not an established explanation.

8. What the evolutionary lens changes in a decision

Return to the invitation to give a prestigious talk from Chapter 6. An evolutionary lens does not produce a hidden correct answer. It improves the diagnosis.

This is the practical contribution of the evolutionary perspective. Before dismissing a response as foolish, ask what problem its mechanism may be solving. Before celebrating it as adaptive, test the proposed function against alternatives. Before intervening, check whether the response is useful in the present environment. And before declaring an outcome valuable, ask whose value, over which horizon, under which ethical standard.

Evolution can deepen an account of human choice. It cannot finish the choice for us.
