Appendix C — Portable Tools

Choose a tool for a decision you are working on, then complete its record or exercise. These worksheets turn the chapter ideas into practical prompts. A useful output is a forecast you can resolve, a question you can ask, or a change you can try and review.

Choose a tool

If the outcome is still unknown, the One-Page Decision Journal helps preserve your reasoning. If you are reviewing a completed decision, the Decision Audit Studio helps separate the process from the result. For a more specific difficulty, use the table below. Add another tool only when the first reveals a question it cannot answer.

Twelve core tools

The first column identifies the task. Follow the tool link to its instructions; the final column tells you what to produce.

Individual judgment and decision process

Preserve a decision before its outcome with the journal, or examine an earlier decision with the audit. The shorter tools below focus on evidence, disagreement, and intuitive judgment.

NoteOne-Page Decision Journal

Use one entry for a consequential, uncertain, repeated, contested, or difficult-to-reverse decision. Complete Before choosing before the result is knowable. At the review trigger, append After the outcome without rewriting the original. Definitions and evidence are in Chapter 41.

Use the same seven labels at both moments. The right-hand entry must answer the left-hand entry on the same row.

Learning loop Before choosing — freeze After the outcome — append; do not overwrite
1. Decision and execution Entry ID, time stamp, owner, deadline, scope, and exact decision. What was implemented, when, and with what deviations or delayed effects?
2. Alternatives and opportunity cost Serious options, best feasible alternative forgone, and excluded options. Was an important option missing? What is known—and still unknowable—about the forgone paths?
3. Evidence and reference class Sources, base rate or comparable cases, missing evidence, and fact–estimate–assumption labels. Which evidence proved diagnostic, weak, misleading, missing, or unresolved? What new evidence arrived?
4. Predictions and assumptions Resolvable forecasts with horizons, probabilities or ranges, and load-bearing assumptions and causal rationale. Resolve the same forecasts. Which assumptions were supported, failed, or remain untested? Score calibration across comparable entries where useful.
5. Values and stakeholders Criteria, priorities, affected people, protected constraints, and trade-offs. Evaluate the same criteria and people. What was misweighted, neglected, or legitimately changed?
6. Context, incentives, dissent, and tools Affect, pressure, incentives, roles, conflicts, strongest contrary view, and model or AI advice with follow-or-override reasons. Which influence, dissenting concern, or tool output mattered? Was reliance or override calibrated to demonstrated value?
7. Choice, thresholds, and learning rule Choice and reason, implementation owner, review trigger, evidence to collect, and continue–stop–scale–revise thresholds. Were the process and thresholds followed? Rate process and outcome separately. State what should change, what should not, and the next test or review.

Remember the rhythm: freeze → compare → update. A concern noticed only afterward is useful evidence, but label it “not anticipated” rather than rewriting the original reasoning.

Keep access and retention proportionate to the stakes. The journal is a learning aid, not a substitute for required minutes, approvals, professional records, or privacy safeguards.

NoteDecision Audit Studio

Part 1 — capture the story. In five sentences, state the decision, choice, why it seemed right at the time, what happened, and how good the decision now seems on a 1–7 scale.

Part 2 — expose the process. Reconstruct alternatives, predictions, valuation, opportunity cost, context or state, and intended feedback. Identify the weakest link. Rate process quality and outcome quality separately. Finish: “The weakest link was ___ because . Process quality: /7. Outcome quality: /7. Next time I will improve it by .”

The audit cannot prove that the chosen road was best or recover the counterfactual road not taken. It can make missing options, weak evidence, hidden values, social influence, and unresolved uncertainty visible.

NoteAttention Audit

Write down what received attention and what was overlooked. Identify a feature of the situation that could explain the difference—for example, a vivid story, the order of information, or time pressure. Propose one change that would make the missing evidence easier to notice.

NotePrediction–Valuation Split

Write the disputed claim twice. Under Prediction, state what each person believes will happen, with probability or range and the evidence that could change it. Under Valuation, state what matters, to whom, the consequence if the prediction is wrong, and the trade-off each person is willing to accept. Finish with one sentence: “We disagree mainly about , so the next useful step is .”

NoteExpectation Loop Map

Choose one expectation and draw a possible path through which it could help change an outcome. The links might involve attention, interpretation, action, another person’s response, or physiology where relevant. Mark which links are observed and which are conjectures. Identify one comparison that could distinguish an expectation’s causal influence from an accurate forecast.

NoteBias Redesign Studio

Choose one process—hiring, investing, exam preparation, project planning, purchasing, or negotiation. Name a likely trigger such as an early anchor, vivid anecdote, identity threat, global impression, or time pressure. Redesign the process before the trigger appears: specify who does what, when, using which independent evidence. Prefer a changed environment to a promise of willpower.

NoteHeuristic Detector
  1. What question needs an answer?
  2. Which cue or rule appears to guide the judgment? If an easier question was substituted, name it.
  3. When would that cue or rule give a useful answer?
  4. What comparison or missing evidence could reveal a mistake here?

Finish with one change to the judgment process that addresses the identified weakness.

Probability, risk, time, and well-being

Start with the Probability Judgment Audit for a forecast or the Risky-Choice Audit for a choice among uncertain outcomes. The remaining tools address the specific problems named in their titles.

NoteProbability Judgment Audit

Define the event, population, time horizon, and the condition on which the probability depends. Translate percentages into counts within a shared population, keeping clear which subgroup each conditional rate refers to. For a conjunction, check that it is no more likely than either component; when adding probabilities, account for overlap. Record the forecast and a rule for resolving it. After resolution, score the same event—for a binary outcome, the Brier score is \((p-y)^2\), where \(p\) is the forecast probability between 0 and 1 and \(y\) is 1 if the event occurred and 0 otherwise.

NoteRisky-Choice Audit

List outcomes, probabilities or ambiguity, expected value, utility-relevant consequences, and the maximum affordable loss. Elicit the certainty equivalent before naming a risk attitude. State the reference point and whether the same prospect was described as a gain or a loss. Ask how insurance or diversification changes the possible payoffs. Separately, ask whether new information would change the probability estimates or the chosen action enough to justify its cost. Finish by naming what expected utility explains, what prospect theory describes, and what neither model identifies from one choice.

NoteExperience-Sample Audit

What outcomes were encountered, how many times, and through which sampling policy? Which rare outcomes could easily be absent? What caused sampling to stop? Are recent, vivid, or similar cases overrepresented? Did earlier choices remove opportunities for corrective learning? Add the best available description—base rates, denominators, time horizon, and uncertainty—then explain why description and experience agree or conflict.

NoteIntertemporal MOST Audit

Write the full stream of outcomes for each option. Use MOST to check magnitude, opportunity cost (including hidden zeros), sign, and time frame. Then ask whether cash needs, trust in future payment, inflation, or transaction costs explain the choice before attributing it to impatience. If you propose a commitment, specify the goal it protects and what happens after a missed step.

NoteMental-Accounting Audit

Name the label, reference point, budget boundary, opening and closing date, and payment–consumption link. Separate acquisition utility from transaction utility and sunk costs from future consequences. Reconstruct the choice once inside the current account and once across the full balance sheet or portfolio. Preserve a boundary when it protects a valued goal; relax it when the label, rather than future value, drives the decision.

NoteMarket Claim Audit

State the information set and expected-return model behind an efficiency claim. For an anomaly, ask about risk, mispricing, data mining, transaction costs, financing, short-sale constraints, and post-publication decay. For a bubble claim, separate uncertain fundamental value from evidence of feedback, resale motives, leverage, liquidity, and arbitrage limits. Historical patterns are evidence to diagnose, not trading instructions.

NoteWell-Being Measurement Audit

Specify whether the claim concerns experienced affect, remembered experience, life evaluation, meaning, or behavior. State the question wording, time window, scale, sample, comparison, and uncertainty. Separate income level from changes and adaptation; correlation from intervention; average effects from distribution and subgroup differences. Use forecasts from comparable lives or episodes before trusting a focusing intuition about what will make life go well.

Social and strategic decisions

NoteStrategic Interdependence Map

Identify the players, their available actions, and how each person’s outcome depends on the combination of choices. Record what they know and when they move. Find best responses under the stated beliefs, then ask how repetition, social preferences, norms, or enforcement could change the prediction. Mark the assumptions you would need to check before applying the game to a real situation.

Behavior and self-regulation

Use the Behavior Redesign Canvas to change a repeated action. The short urge-observation exercise is useful when a noticeable impulse is part of the episode.

NoteBehavior Redesign Canvas

Choose one mild, repeated behavior. Reconstruct cue → impulse → action → outcome, using impulse to mean readiness to act even when it was not consciously noticed. Record what the action accomplished in the moment.

Specify one feasible replacement and one change to the setting that would help it occur. Decide what you will record for a week, when you will review it, and how you will resume after a lapse. The output is the old routine, the proposed change, and a measure of whether it helped the person’s own goal.

NoteOne-Minute Urge Observation

Choose a mild everyday impulse. Pause, breathe, and notice what the urge feels like without requiring it to disappear. After about a minute, record what changed and choose the next action. This is an observation exercise; a minute is a practical interval, not a prediction of how quickly an urge should fade. It is not treatment for addiction or a crisis.

Persuasion, communication, and connection

Use the Verified Understanding Loop when people may be interpreting the same words differently. The message and listening tools help you prepare what to say or ask.

NoteModel-Updating Message

Before drafting, state who the audience is, what they currently believe, what concerns them, and what evidence could reasonably change their view. Name the desired response and the strongest objection to it.

For a short message, use AND–BUT–THEREFORE: establish shared reality, explain the problem, and propose the next step. For a fuller message, STORY means Shared reality, Trouble, Opportunity, Reasons to believe, and Yes to a specific decision. Make the action feasible and the uncertainty visible. Both are writing prompts; neither guarantees persuasion.

NoteLayered Listening

Begin with what happened, then ask what it meant to the speaker. Explore feelings, history, or relationships when they are relevant and the person welcomes the question. Check what is wanted from the conversation: understanding, advice, a decision, repair, or a commitment. The layers guide curiosity; they are not a required interrogation sequence.

NoteVerified Understanding Loop

Ask, listen, and restate the meaning in your own words. Invite the speaker to correct what you missed, then check again. The aim is an account the speaker recognizes as accurate. You can understand a position while continuing to disagree with it.

Negotiation

Begin with Negotiation Preparation. Use the later tools when an offer, tactic, disagreement, or implementation problem needs closer attention.

NoteNegotiation Preparation
  1. State your best alternative to a negotiated agreement (BATNA) and how you could improve it. Use it to determine the least acceptable agreement, allowing for risk and implementation costs.
  2. List the issues, priorities, target, proposed opening, and supporting standards.
  3. Record what you know about the other side’s interests and alternatives, and what you need to ask.
  4. Prepare possible packages, a concession plan, and terms for implementation and review.
NoteTactic Diagnostic

Treat a tactic as a possible signal, not proof of the counterpart’s constraint. Diagnose it before reacting.

NoteMediation and Arbitration Are Different Choices

A mediator helps parties seek a voluntary agreement and ordinarily does not impose the result. An arbitrator hears the dispute and issues a decision under the applicable procedure. In final-offer arbitration, the arbitrator chooses between submitted final offers; whether that choice concerns a whole package or individual issues depends on the rules. Before choosing a process, establish the neutral’s authority, costs, confidentiality, and whether the decision is binding or reviewable.

NotePackage and Contingency Worksheet

List the negotiable issues and estimate what matters to each side. Prepare several packages of approximately equal value to you that trade differently across issues; these are multiple equivalent simultaneous offers (MESOs). Use the response to learn about priorities.

If the disagreement concerns a future event, consider a contingent term with a clear measure and verification process. Check whether either side could manipulate the measure or bear a risk it cannot manage. A signed agreement can remain the fallback while the parties voluntarily search for an improvement.

Organizational choice and decision systems

Use the Structured Judgment Pipeline to organize a group decision. Add the choice-architecture or AI-use record when the design involves those features, and use the ethical audit to examine who is affected.

NoteChoice-Architecture Audit

Trace one real path: objective → menu → understanding → action → feedback. Mark the options, order, categories, default, mapping, friction, timing, social information, and exit. Write the mechanism as a testable sentence: “Changing ___ should affect ___ because ___.” Compare a nudge with a boost, incentive, mandate, or structural change. Check purpose, welfare, evidence, transparency, exit, and distribution of burden.

NoteStructured Judgment Pipeline

Define the decision, its owner, and the people affected. Break the judgment into relevant criteria and establish a comparison or reference class. Have members record estimates independently before hearing the group or an AI recommendation.

Compare estimates and the reasons behind them, then examine the strongest objection. Record the decision, trade-offs, implementation responsibility, and conditions for reconsidering it. Set a review date and an outcome that can be checked. Use fewer steps when the stakes and complexity are low.

NoteMinimum AI-Use Record

Use this extension when a model materially informs a consequential decision:

  1. Task and tool: task delegated; tool, model, or rule; version or access date; and reason for use.
  2. Inputs and boundaries: material inputs and sources, missing information, and privacy, consent, or use restrictions.
  3. Output and reliance: material output; what was followed, modified, or rejected; and why.
  4. Independent checks: fact and source verification, baseline or independent human comparison, uncertainty and subgroup checks where relevant, and strongest contrary evidence.
  5. Authority and correction: decision owner, affected people, exception or appeal, monitoring signal, review date, and who can pause or reverse the use.

Scale the record and validation with consequence, repetition, irreversibility, unequal power, and automation. Do not place sensitive data in an unauthorized record. Chapter 41 explains the distinction between prediction, valuation, intervention, and responsibility.

NoteEthical Audit

Answer six questions before implementation and again at review:

  1. Whose objective is being optimized?
  2. Who receives the benefit, and who bears the cost or tail risk?
  3. Would affected people understand and endorse the mechanism under informed reflection?
  4. Is refusal, correction, appeal, or exit genuinely available?
  5. Does unequal power change the meaning of apparent consent?
  6. What evidence will reveal harm or failure after implementation?

Add the domain-specific risk—deception, stigma, distribution, coercion, privacy, automation, or another foreseeable harm—and name who has authority to pause, correct, compensate, or end the intervention.

Classroom extensions

These activities are teaching protocols, not additional decision tools. Assign one only when it serves a stated learning objective.

NoteCase, Video, and Conversation Practice

Case or video debrief. Before viewing or reading, predict the central difficulty. During the case, record observable behavior rather than inferred motive. Afterward, identify the proposed mechanism, a plausible alternative explanation, information learned or lost, economic or relational cost, one boundary condition, and one better action to test. A clip or case illustrates behavior; it does not establish effectiveness outside its context. Linked videos remain with their rights holders.

Message production. Create a 3–5 minute message for a real audience and attach a design note of no more than 200 words: current audience model; meaningful contradiction; proposed update; strongest evidence; protected value or identity; specific action; uncertainty; and ethical safeguard. Peer feedback diagnoses the message rather than the presenter.

Connection prediction. Before a four-minute paired conversation, privately predict enjoyment, awkwardness, and connection. Compare forecast with experience. Participation and depth must be voluntary.

Depth and responsiveness. Begin naturally, then ask permission to follow one answer a level deeper—from fact to experience, meaning, emotion, or value. Debrief the difference between curiosity and intrusion.

Warm honesty. In triads, practice care + observable behavior + owned impact + invitation to the other person’s view + one testable request. Unsafe or high-power conflicts may require documentation, representation, or formal support rather than a classroom script.
